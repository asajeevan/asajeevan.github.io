Module 0 · the foundation

Charge, field, potential, voltage

A torch cell says 1.5 V on its side. What is a volt, and what exactly is being pushed?

Where we are. Before the first cell, four words that every later screen uses: charge, the thing that pushes and is pushed; field, the push per unit charge at a place; potential, the energy per unit charge at a place; voltage, a difference of potential. Each gets one figure, one equation and one number.

Everything on this page moves because charges push on one another. Charge comes in two signs, and the names are a convention older than the electron: Benjamin Franklin called an excess of what we now call electronic charge negative and a deficiency of it positive.

Between two charges q and q′ a distance r apart there is a force F = qq′/(4πε0r2), Coulomb’s law, where ε0 is a measured constant, the permittivity of free space. Read the formula: the force grows with either charge, falls with the square of the distance, and its sign follows the product of the charges, so like charges push apart and unlike charges pull together.

The unit of charge is the coulomb, and one electron carries 1.602 × 10−19 C of it. Put two electrons one nanometre apart, about the width of three water molecules, and the formula gives 0.23 nN pushing them apart: tiny in newtons, enormous for something that weighs 10−30 kg.

Charges push on each other with a force that falls as the square of the distance.

Take the second charge away and ask what is left at its place. The answer is the electric field: the force that would act on a unit charge put there, a vector with a direction and a strength at every point in space. Around a positive charge it points outward and falls as 1/r2, because that is how the force falls.

Now bring a unit positive charge in from very far away, pushing against that field all the way. The work you do is the electric potential φ at the point you reach, and it does not depend on the path you took: φ = −∫E·dl.

Only differences of φ between two points can be measured; the value at one point is a bookkeeping choice, and the usual choice sets φ = 0 infinitely far away. For a single elementary charge the integral gives φ = q/(4πε0r), which is 1.44 V one nanometre out and half that at two.

The two pictures are one: the field is the slope of the potential. Where φ falls steeply the push is strong; where φ is flat there is no push at all. That last case is about to matter.

Field is force per unit charge; potential is energy per unit charge; the field is the slope of the potential.

A voltage is a difference of potential between two points, and its unit, the volt, is one joule per coulomb: the energy available to move each coulomb of charge from one point to the other. Move a charge q across a potential difference Δφ and its energy changes by qΔφ.

For one electron across one volt that change is one electron-volt, and per mole of electrons it is 96.5 kJ, comparable to the energies of chemical bonds and reactions; which is why a few volts are enough to drive chemistry.

Metals are conductors: they contain charges that are free to move. Leave a metal alone and those charges settle until there is no field left inside it, because any field would move them further; so a conductor at rest sits at a single potential throughout, and any excess charge it carries lies on its surface.

The plates in the figure are two such flat regions of the potential; between them, where there is no conductor, the potential falls in a straight line and the field is simply V divided by the gap.

A picture to keep: potential is height, field is slope, and a positive charge rolls downhill while a negative one is pushed uphill. The picture breaks in one place: charges also push on each other and rearrange, so the hill is not fixed scenery. Module 3 is about how a cell builds its own hill.

Worked example. How much charge does it take to raise a metal by one volt? Bard, Faulkner and White give the answer for a mercury drop of 0.5 mm radius in vacuum: about 5 × 10−14 C per volt. electrons per volt = 5 × 10−14 C ÷ 1.602 × 10−19 C ≈ 3 × 105 Three hundred thousand electrons, out of the 1020 or so in the drop, shift its potential by a full volt. Potentials are set by minute charge imbalances, which is why they are so easy to change and so hard to see.

A volt is a joule per coulomb; a conductor at rest is all at one potential.

A cell keeps its two terminals at a fixed potential difference; module 1 shows how. Join them with a wire and electrons flow through the wire from the more negative terminal to the more positive one. The amount of charge passing a point each second is the current, i = dQ/dt, measured in amperes: one ampere is one coulomb per second.

A wire carrying current is no longer at one potential. Between any two points on it there must be a potential difference to drive the current through the resistance between them. That is Ohm’s law, V = IR: the same current through twice the resistance needs twice the voltage.

The resistance of a piece of conductor grows with its length and shrinks with its cross-section, R = ρl/A, where ρ is the resistivity of the material. Metals have a resistance too, but it is so much smaller than that of an electrolyte that on the scale of a cell the drop along the copper is invisible. In the figure, nearly the whole voltage is spent across the lamp.

Energy per coulomb times coulombs per second is energy per second: the power delivered is P = IV, in watts.

Worked example. A 1.5 V cell drives a lamp of 3 Ω. I = V / R = 1.5 V / 3 Ω = 0.5 A, that is 0.5 C every second electrons per second = 0.5 C ÷ 1.602 × 10−19 C = 3.1 × 1018 P = I V = 0.5 A × 1.5 V = 0.75 W Each electron gives up 1.5 eV on its way round; three billion billion of them a second add up to three quarters of a watt of light and heat.

Current needs a potential difference to drive it through resistance; the power is current times voltage.

In a liquid the mobile charges are not electrons but ions, atoms or molecules that have lost or gained electrons; free electrons in a solution are so short-lived that they carry no current worth counting. Put an ion of charge number z in a field E and it feels the force |z|eE. As it speeds up the liquid drags on it, with a drag that grows with speed; when the drag balances the push the ion stops accelerating and drifts at a steady terminal velocity. The velocity per unit field is the ion’s mobility, u = |z|e/(6πηr): a smaller ion in a thinner liquid moves faster.

When current flows through a liquid the potential slopes across it, exactly as it did along the wire, and the slope is the field: cations are driven down the slope and anions up it. Both carry current, and the liquid’s conductivity adds up every kind of ion, its charge, its mobility and its concentration: κ = FΣ|z|uC.

One more rule holds everywhere in the liquid: on any scale you can see, positive and negative charge balance. An imbalance cannot build up, because it would create an enormous field that would move ions until it was gone.

The constant F in that formula is the Faraday constant, the charge carried by one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C. It is the bridge between the chemist’s moles and the physicist’s coulombs, and it returns in module 4 to count how much charge a gram of material can hold.

You can now explain what a volt is, why a field makes charges move and which way, why a current needs a potential difference to drive it, and why an ion in a liquid drifts at a steady speed rather than accelerating.

Go deeper: Gauss’s law, and why the charge sits on the surface

Draw an imaginary closed surface anywhere. Gauss’s law says that the net charge inside it equals ε0 times the integral of the field over that surface, q = ε0∮E·dS. Inside a conductor at rest the field is zero everywhere, so the integral over any surface drawn inside the conductor is zero, so the charge inside is zero: whatever excess charge the conductor carries must lie on its boundary. In an electrolyte the same argument leaves the charge at the boundary too, but thermal motion smears it into a layer that can be tenths of a nanometre to thousands of nanometres thick, the space charge region; in a metal that layer is negligibly thin. Module 3 uses this to build the double layer at an electrode.

Bring an electrolyte next to the metal and the coulombic field of the metal’s surface charge is largely cancelled by the polarization of the liquid: the same mercury drop then needs about 10−6 C, some 6 × 1012 electrons, for a one-volt change, more than 107 times what it needed in vacuum.

Module 1

What a battery does

Why does the lamp light when you close the switch, and why not when it is open?

Where we are. Module 0 gave you the volt (a joule per coulomb), the field (the push per unit charge), the conductor at rest (one potential throughout) and the two kinds of carrier: electrons in metals, ions in liquids. Now two different metals go into a liquid, and a potential difference appears between them.

Close the switch. Zinc atoms at the left electrode give up two electrons each and dissolve into the liquid as Zn2+ ions; at the right electrode Cu2+ ions take two electrons each and settle as copper metal. Each of those is a half-reaction, and neither can happen alone: each must be coupled, through a cell, to a second half-reaction running the other way.

The electrons cannot cross the liquid, so they take the wire, from the more negative electrode to the more positive, and the lamp lights on the way. Inside the cell the current is carried by ions: cations drift toward the copper and sulfate toward the zinc, so no charge piles up anywhere.

Open the switch and everything stops, but nothing is lost. The voltage you measure with nothing else connected is the open-circuit voltage, and a high-impedance voltmeter can read it without drawing any appreciable current. For this pair of metals it is 1.10 V: the difference between the standard potentials of the two half-reactions, +0.340 V for copper and −0.763 V for zinc.

Where a potential sits inside the cell is the strip under the figure: it jumps at each metal|liquid boundary, in a zone so narrow that a very strong field exists there, and stays flat through the liquid; the voltmeter reads the sum of the jumps along the path from one electrode to the other.

Module 3 shows how each jump is built: the chemistry moves a minute charge across the boundary, and the field of that separated charge is what the strip shows.

That is the whole trick, in Goodenough and Park’s words: the electrolyte “conducts the ionic component of the chemical reaction between the anode and the cathode, but it forces the electronic component to traverse an external circuit where it does work”.

Worked example. Where does 1.10 V come from, and what does it buy? E = E°(Cu2+/Cu) − E°(Zn2+/Zn) = 0.340 V − (−0.763 V) = 1.10 V energy per electron round the circuit = 1.10 eV; two electrons per zinc atom per mole of zinc: 2 × 96 485 C × 1.10 V = 213 kJ Module 3 shows that this 213 kJ is the free energy of the reaction Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu, and module 4 turns the “per mole” into “per gram”.

A battery is a chemical reaction that is only allowed to happen through a wire.

Why do the electrons choose the wire? Because they have no other road. In a metal, charge is carried by electrons moving through a lattice of fixed ion cores; in an electrolyte it is carried by ions, and the “free” electrons a liquid might briefly hold are so short-lived that they contribute nothing to its conductivity.

At the boundary between the two, charge can pass only if the two kinds of carrier are linked by an electrode reaction: electrons consumed or produced in the metal, ions produced or consumed in the liquid.

The field does the rest. One field points from the positive side to the negative side; it pushes a positive ion along itself and an electron against itself, so the cations in the liquid and the electrons in the wire go round the circuit in opposite senses and meet again at the electrodes.

In the wire, the potential difference that drives the electrons through the lamp is the one you met in figure 0.4; in the liquid, the potential slopes gently and drives the ions.

Electrons travel in metals, ions travel in liquids, and only a reaction can hand charge from one to the other.

Strictly, one of these units is a cell, and several connected together are a battery, although the two words are used loosely. Connecting cells in series adds their voltages; connecting them in parallel adds their current and their run time. The strip says why the voltages add: a potential difference between two points is the sum of the rises and drops along any path between them, so three cells in a chain give three rises of V, and a coulomb that travels the whole chain picks up energy at each one.

Why the capacity does not add in series is a matter of counting coulombs. The cells in a chain carry one and the same current: the charge that leaves the first cell has nowhere to go but through the second and the third, so every coulomb drawn from the chain is a coulomb drawn from each cell. When one cell has given up its Q, they all have. In parallel the picture is reversed. All three + terminals are tied together and all three − terminals are tied together, so the ends of the battery see one potential difference, V, and no more; but the current is shared, each cell supplies a third of it, and together they can deliver three times the charge before they are empty. Series adds the rises along the path; parallel adds the paths.

A battery keeps its reacting materials inside, so it is a closed system: storage and conversion happen in the same box. A fuel cell is an open system, fed with fuel and oxidant from outside, so its energy is stored in a tank and converted in the cell. A supercapacitor stores charge in the electrical double layers at its electrode surfaces, without a chemical reaction.

None of the three turns heat into work, so none is bound by the Carnot limit that caps every heat engine. Everything on this page happens inside a single cell.

You can now explain why a cell has a voltage even when nothing is connected, where inside the cell that voltage sits, why the electrons take the wire and the ions the liquid, and why voltages add in series.

Go deeper: primary and secondary cells

A primary battery is assembled in the charged state and used until exhausted. A secondary, or rechargeable, battery can be restored to its charged condition by a current flowing in the opposite direction to the discharge current; since secondary batteries are usually assembled in the discharged state, they must be charged before their first use. To charge, a power supply is connected across the cell and its voltage is adjusted; the interfacial potential differences shift, especially at the electrode|electrolyte boundaries, and current can be driven in either direction. Which reactions can be run backwards, and which cannot, is a question for the modules still to come.

Module 2

Anatomy of a cell

If you cut a cell open, what would you find, and what does each part do?

Where we are. Module 1 showed the four jobs in a cell: give electrons, take electrons, carry ions, keep the two electrodes apart. Now the parts that do those jobs in a real cell, and why they have the shapes they have.

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide. The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.

The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching. If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words, and they name a reaction, not a piece of metal: the anode is the electrode where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, the cathode the electrode where reduction takes them back. On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced, so the negative electrode is the anode and the positive electrode the cathode; that is the battery convention. On charge the charger drives the current the other way: electrons are pushed into the negative electrode, which is now reduced, and pulled out of the positive electrode, which is now oxidized, so by the reaction definition the names swap, and the graphite that was the anode while you used the phone is the cathode while it charges. The metals stay where they are and keep their signs; only the labels move. To avoid the confusion this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and the lithium host on aluminium, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell. Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal. The electrolyte is a liquid, added drop by drop during assembly: in Murray, Hall and Dahn’s research cells, about 38 mg of a 1 mol/L solution of LiPF6 in a carbonate solvent, enough to soak the separator and wet both electrodes.

New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell” against a piece of lithium foil; such cells are easy to make but can fail to predict how the material behaves against graphite in a full cell, which is why full coin cells like this one are built.

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal (module 0 showed why: an ion drags its way through a liquid at a terminal speed set by its mobility). So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals. A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.

Worked example. Why area matters: the resistance of a layer is R = ρl/A (module 0). Keep the electrolyte layer as thin as the separator allows (small l) and make the electrodes as wide as will fit (large A), and the ionic resistance of the whole cell falls with both. A wound strip is the way to put a large A inside a small can.

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.

The porous structure extends the surface area where the reaction can happen and lowers the current density, so the cell works more efficiently. A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; a later module returns to what happens where one of them does not.

The same four parts, in different materials, give very different cells. The table behind this chart lists the systems on the market in 2004: zinc and manganese dioxide in the 1.5 V cells of a torch, lead and lead oxide in the 2.0 V cells of a car battery, and graphite with lithium cobalt oxide in the 4.0 V cells of a phone.

Two patterns are already visible: every system with a water-based electrolyte stays at or below 2 V, and every system above 2 V is built on lithium with a nonaqueous electrolyte. Where the voltage comes from, and why water sets a ceiling, is the next question.

Go deeper: what a research coin cell taught its makers

Murray, Hall and Dahn built full coin cells with graphite and a nickel-rich oxide and found that three “identical” cells kept 95 %, 90 % and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. Taking them apart showed the cause: the positive and negative discs were slightly misaligned, and lithium had plated on the parts of the positive electrode not covered by the negative one. The spring and spacer had been dropped in at an angle from tweezers; placing them from directly above with a vacuum pen, and using a thicker, more compliant separator, brought the cells to the reproducibility of machine-made pouch cells. Why plated lithium is lost for good is a story for a later module.

Module 3

Where the voltage comes from

Why does a lithium-ion cell give over 3.6 V and a torch battery 1.5 V?

Where we are. Module 1 measured 1.10 V between zinc and copper and showed that it sits in two jumps, one at each metal|liquid boundary. Module 2 named the parts. Now the physics of a jump: what a potential means for the electrons in a metal, what happens at the boundary, and why the two electrodes of your cell sit where they do on a ladder.

In module 0 a potential was energy per unit charge. For an electrode that has a sharp meaning: to observe or control the potential of an electrode is to observe or control the energy of its transferable electrons. Drive an electrode to a more negative potential and the energy of its electrons is raised; for each volt, each electron gains one electron-volt, 96.5 kJ per mole of electrons, comparable to the energies of chemical bonds.

The energy of those transferable electrons has a name: the Fermi energy, or Fermi level. In a metal the electron states form a continuum, filled from the bottom up; the Fermi level marks the division between filled and empty, and because empty states lie just above it, an electron can move with almost no activation, which is what makes a metal conduct.

Raise the Fermi level above an empty orbital of a species in the electrolyte and electrons flow from the electrode into the solution, a reduction current; lower it below a filled orbital and electrons flow from the solution onto the electrode, an oxidation current.

This is the same fact as the potential ladder further down: a more negative electrode holds its electrons at a higher energy, so it is the stronger reducing agent. Lithium, at the bottom of every ladder, holds them highest of all.

Electrode potential is electron energy in disguise: more negative means higher-energy electrons, one electron-volt per volt.

Now zoom into one of the jumps of figure 1.1. Put a metal into an electrolyte and the two phases exchange a little charge until their electron energies match; equality of the Fermi energies of metal and solution is the condition for equilibrium, and electrons flow from the phase with the higher Fermi energy to the one with the lower until it is reached. The amount of charge that moves is tiny, so the composition of the solution does not noticeably change.

Here is how that field is built, step by step. Dip a piece of zinc into a solution of its own ions. A zinc ion sitting in the metal and a zinc ion dissolved in the liquid are not in the same state: their electrochemical potentials differ, and a species moves across a boundary in the direction that lowers its electrochemical potential until it is the same on both sides.

So a few Zn2+ ions leave the metal and enter the liquid, and each leaves its two electrons behind. Now the metal is slightly negative and the liquid touching it slightly positive. Those separated charges are the field: the excess electrons sit on the metal’s surface, the excess cations gather in the liquid right against it, and between two sheets of opposite charge a molecular distance apart runs a strong field, pointing from the liquid into the metal. Every ion that crosses makes that field a little stronger, and the field pulls back on the next ion that tries; the crossing stops when the electrical pull exactly balances the chemical push, which is the equilibrium of equal electrochemical potentials.

At a copper electrode in copper sulfate the exchange runs the other way, ions from the liquid plating onto the metal and leaving it positive. Where the exchange is of electrons rather than ions, as at a platinum wire in a solution of iron ions, the rule is the same: electrons cross from the phase with the higher Fermi energy to the one with the lower until the two are equal.

In every case the amount of charge that crosses is tiny, far too small to change the composition of the liquid, yet it sets up a potential difference of a volt or so across a few nanometres.

That charge cannot spread through the metal: a conductor at rest keeps its excess charge on its surface (module 0), and on an electrode it lies in a layer thinner than a nanometre. The liquid answers with an equal and opposite charge right at the boundary, an excess of cations or of anions.

The whole array, charged metal surface, oriented solvent molecules, a compact layer of ions one solvent shell out and a diffuse layer beyond, is the electrical double layer. Above 0.01 M the diffuse part is less than about 10 nm thick.

Why does the field stay inside that thin layer and not reach out into the liquid? Inside the metal it is zero for the reason module 0 gave: any field in a conductor at rest would move the free charges until it was gone. On the liquid side the Coulomb field of the metal’s surface charge is almost entirely cancelled by the polarization of the electrolyte: ions of the opposite sign crowd in and solvent molecules turn, and a few nanometres out the field has been screened to nothing, so the bulk of the liquid sits at one potential.

That is why the strip under figure 1.1 is flat through the solution at open circuit. The liquid can even hold a potential step with no excess charge at all: water molecules touching a metal tend to line up, and a layer of aligned dipoles is itself a charge separation.

Draw current and one thing changes. A gentle field appears through the bulk liquid, the slope of the potential that drives the ions, and it is small because the electrolyte conducts well; this is the ohmic drop. The two interfacial fields remain, shifted a little from their rest values because each reaction needs an extra push, an overpotential, to run at a finite rate.

So the field in a working cell has three parts: a strong, nanometre-thin one at each electrode, built by the chemistry itself out of a minute charge separation and held in place by the electrolyte’s screening, and a weak one across the liquid that exists only while current flows.

Two sheets of opposite charge a molecular distance apart: the potential steps across them, and the field inside the step can reach 107 V/cm, strong enough to distort the molecules that sit in it. The arrangement behaves like a capacitor, q = C·E, with 10 to 40 µF for every square centimetre of electrode, and it forms and relaxes in about 10−8 s. A cell’s voltage is the sum of such steps, taken along the path from one electrode to the other.

A cell’s voltage lives in two double layers, each a few nanometres thick, built by a tiny charge separation that the chemistry itself creates.

Chemists carry the same idea one step further. The energy of a charged species in a phase has two parts: a chemical part, from its surroundings on the scale of a molecule, and an electrical part, its charge times the local potential. Their sum is the electrochemical potential, μ̄ = μ + zFφ, and a species is at equilibrium between two phases when its electrochemical potential is the same in both.

Apply that to the electrons in the two electrodes of a cell and the open-circuit voltage falls out: it is the difference between the electrochemical potentials of the electrons in the two electrodes, divided by the electron charge, VOC = (μA − μC)/e.

So picture each material as a rung on a ladder of electron energy, drawn as a potential: the cell voltage is the gap between the two rungs you choose. Battery scientists measure the rungs against lithium metal, which is why every value here reads “versus Li/Li+”. Lithium sits at the bottom. In Tarascon and Armand’s words it is “the most electropositive” metal, the one with the most negative standard potential, −3.04 V against the standard hydrogen electrode, and it is also the lightest metal, at 6.94 g/mol and 0.53 g/cm3.

The gap has a ceiling, and the ceiling is the electrolyte. Water is thermodynamically stable over only 1.23 V, between the potential at which it gives off hydrogen and the potential at which it gives off oxygen; slow kinetics stretch that to about 2 V in practice, which is why cells with an aqueous electrolyte and a long shelf life stop near 1.5 V.

But where that 1.23 V sits matters as much as its width: at pH 0 it runs from 3.05 to 4.27 V versus lithium, and it slides 59 mV lower for every pH unit. Every rung below about 3 V, which is every negative electrode on the ladder and most of the positive ones, is simply out of reach for water. The organic carbonate electrolytes of lithium-ion cells are stable from about 1.1 V to about 4.3 V versus lithium, about 4.6 V in practice, and there is the answer to the hook: not a stronger reaction, but an electrolyte that tolerates a wider gap, in the right place.

Two reservoirs at different heights: the pressure at the pipe between them depends on the height difference, not on how wide the reservoirs are. The picture breaks as soon as water flows: the height stays, but the flow costs pressure. The next module to come is about that cost.

Voltage is a property of the pair of materials, and the electrolyte sets how far apart they may be.

The ceiling has a mechanism. An electrolyte is a collection of molecules, and each has a highest filled and a lowest empty energy level; the gap between them is the electrolyte window. A negative electrode whose electrons sit above the empty level will reduce the electrolyte, and a positive electrode whose empty states sit below the filled level will oxidize it, unless a passivating layer forms at the surface and blocks the reaction.

Graphite sits about 0.2 V above lithium, outside the carbonate window, so a layer must form on it during the first charge; lithium titanate at 1.5 V sits inside the window and needs none. A later module follows that layer as it forms.

Notice the path of the electron in the figure. It gives up its e·VOC only by travelling round the external circuit, from the negative electrode to the positive one; it never crosses the electrolyte, which has no free electrons (module 1). Inside the cell it is our ion that crosses, keeping every region neutral.

Why do oxide positive electrodes reach 4 V when the sulfide of the first rechargeable lithium cell reached only 2.2 V? In a solid the electron levels of the many atoms merge into bands, continuous ranges of energy, filled from the bottom up. The redox couple of the host’s transition metal cannot be pushed below the top of the host’s filled anion band. In the layered sulfides that top sits about 2.5 eV below the lithium level; in the layered oxides it sits about 4.0 eV below, which is why oxide hosts are used in today’s lithium-ion cells. Polyanions such as phosphate pull the band lower still through what chemists call the inductive effect, which is how LiFePO4 reaches 3.5 V.

In the language of thermodynamics the same gap reads ΔG = −nFE: the free energy of a reaction, the energy it can deliver as work, equals the charge it moves per mole, nF, times the cell voltage, with the minus sign saying that a spontaneous reaction has a positive voltage.

So the voltage is the reaction’s free energy per unit of charge, and nF, the total charge available, is its capacity factor. The Faraday constant is the charge carried by one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 coulombs.

Worked example. The Daniell cell of module 1, Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu, moves two electrons per zinc atom at 1.103 V. ΔG = −nFE = −2 × 96 485 C/mol × 1.103 V = −213 kJ/mol per electron: 1.103 eV; per zinc atom: 2.21 eV That 213 kJ per mole of zinc is what the lamp of figure 1.1 can get, at most, from 65 g of zinc; module 4 turns “per mole” into “per gram” and “per hour”.

Go deeper: the electrochemical potential, from two metals in contact to the cell voltage

For electrons in a metal the electrochemical potential is μ̄e = μ°e − Fφ, with no activity term because the electron concentration never changes appreciably. Touch a copper wire to a zinc electrode and electrons move between them until their electrochemical potentials are equal, which leaves a potential difference φZn − φCu = (μ°eZn − μ°eCu)/F between the two metals: a contact potential, one of the steps in figure 1.1’s profile. Write the equilibrium of the whole cell reaction in electrochemical potentials and the terms in φ cancel everywhere except at the two copper leads, giving −nFE = n(μ̄eCu′ − μ̄eCu) and, on expanding the chemical potentials, the Nernst equation. Per electron, the same statement is Goodenough and Park’s VOC = (μA − μC)/e.

A potential is always a difference, so every value needs a reference. Textbook tables use the standard hydrogen electrode, against which lithium sits at −3.045 V; battery papers use lithium metal itself as the zero, which is why the same LiCoO2 electrode reads about 4.0 V “versus Li/Li+” and about 1 V versus hydrogen. In a lithium-ion cell the negative electrode is not lithium metal but a host that holds lithium near lithium’s potential, so the cell voltage is a little less than the positive electrode’s ladder value: graphite costs about 0.2 V.

Module 4

Capacity, energy and power

How long would your cell run a lamp?

Where we are. Module 3 gave your cell a voltage: the gap between two rungs, which is the free energy of its reaction per unit of charge. Now the other factor: how much charge the materials hold. Charge times voltage is energy; energy per second is power.

Capacity is a count of electrons. Faraday’s law ties the amount of material transformed to the charge that flows, Q = nFN for N moles of reactant, and it has “no known exceptions”. Turn it around: a gram of a material of molar mass M that gives up n electrons per formula unit holds nF/M coulombs, which the battery world writes in milliampere-hours per gram, Q = nF/(3.6 M). The Faraday constant is the charge of one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C or 26.8015 Ah.

Two conventions hide in that formula. Graphite’s 372 mAh/g refers to the mass of carbon, the host as assembled, not to the mass of LiC6: Tarascon and Armand give 372 mAh/g for the end compound LiC6 and about 350 mAh/g in practice. Positive-electrode materials are referred to their lithiated mass: LiFePO4 holds 170 mAh/g in theory and is used at about 90 % of it, while LiCoO2 is limited to about half of its lithium for safety, 140 mAh/g in practice.

Worked example. Graphite, one lithium per six carbons, referred to the carbon. M(C6) = 6 × 12.011 = 72.07 g/mol, n = 1 Q = 1 × 96 485.3 C/mol ÷ (3.6 × 72.07 g/mol) = 372 mAh/g at 3.8 V: 372 mAh/g × 3.8 V = 1414 mWh/g = 1414 Wh/kg of graphite, graphite alone The same calculation on the mass of LiC6 (79.0 g/mol) gives 339 mAh/g. Both are right; they answer different questions.

Light elements and many electrons per formula unit give high capacity; high voltage turns capacity into energy.

Energy is the integral of the voltage over the charge delivered: on a discharge curve, the area under the line, which is also the average voltage times the capacity. Per kilogram it is called specific energy (Wh/kg), per litre energy density (Wh/L); how fast it can be delivered is specific power (W/kg) and power density (W/L).

The capacity itself depends on how hard you pull. At high current the transfer of ions across the interfaces becomes diffusion-limited and the cell reaches less of its charge before the cut-off; that part of the loss is reversible and comes back at a gentler rate. The next module to come takes the curve apart.

Worked example. A cell of capacity 2 Ah discharged at an average of 3.6 V. energy = 3.6 V × 2 Ah = 7.2 Wh a 6 W lamp (P = IV, module 0) runs for 7.2 Wh ÷ 6 W = 1.2 hours, drawing 6 W / 3.6 V = 1.7 A At that current the curve would sag and the capacity would fall short of 2 Ah; the figure shows by how much, schematically.

To compare the two abilities, energy and power, engineers use the Ragone plot. On it, supercapacitors are high-power systems, fuel cells high-energy systems, and batteries sit between them, overlapping both: a battery with thin-film electrodes reaches the power of a supercapacitor. No single electrochemical system matches the combustion engine on both axes at once, which is why the review suggests combining them: batteries or supercapacitors for the power, fuel cells for the energy.

One caution before you trust any Wh/kg figure: the theoretical energy of a chemistry, reckoned on the mass of everything that reacts, is never what a cell delivers. Practical values are significantly lower, and Winter and Brodd list among the causes the inert parts a cell needs (conductive diluents, current collectors, containers), the internal resistances of electrodes, electrolyte and contacts, and incomplete use of the active masses.

Module 2 showed the inert parts; module 0 showed why resistance costs voltage, and therefore energy, whenever current flows. How large the gap is depends on the chemistry and on how the cell is built, so this page quotes delivered values only where a source gives them.

To give the numbers a feel, Winter and Brodd note that a specific energy of 30 Wh/kg means one kilogram of lead-acid battery can power a 60 W lamp for only half an hour. The first commercial lithium-ion cell of 1991 gave over 3.6 V and 120 to 150 Wh/kg, two to three times the usual nickel-cadmium cell of the day.

Worked example. Module 5

Charging and discharging

What actually happens when you charge a cell and then use it, why does the voltage sag when current flows, and where does the lost energy go?

Where we are. Module 4 counted the charge your cell can hold and turned it into energy, with the cell mostly at rest. Now the current flows. This module starts at the test bench, with how a charge and a discharge are actually done and recorded. Then it follows what current costs (voltage, capacity and heat), why some curves are flat and others slope, what happens inside real electrodes made of many particles, how a curve can be read for signs of ageing, and how many cycles a cell survives. From here on the page follows one lithium ion, our ion, through the cell.

A discharge is the cell doing work: current flows out through a lamp, a phone or a motor. A charge is the reverse: a charger pushes current back in, in the opposite direction, and restores the cell to its charged condition. On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced; charging runs both reactions backwards. In a lithium-ion cell that means lithium ions leave one electrode, cross the electrolyte and enter the other, while their electrons go round through the wire (module 6).

The test bench

Cells are studied, and their capacities measured, on a cycler: an instrument that sets the current and records the voltage at regular intervals (once a second in Olson and co-workers’ tests). Most tests hold the current constant. This is galvanostatic cycling, and its record, the voltage against the charge passed, is the raw material of everything else in this module.

At constant current the charge passed is simply current times time, Q=It. Currents are quoted as C-rates: 1C is the current that passes the cell’s full nominal charge in one hour, so C/20 takes twenty hours. Olson and co-workers, for example, recorded a 210 mAh cell at C/20, that is 10.5 mA.

Each step stops at a cut-off voltage. Overcharging or overdischarging drives unwanted reactions that cost capacity. Charging often ends with a constant-voltage step: the cycler holds the upper cut-off, the current falls away by itself, and the step ends when it reaches a small limit. Olson and co-workers held 4.2 V until the current fell to C/20; Safari and Delacourt until it fell to C/25.

Worked example. A 2.0 Ah cell discharged at 1C for 30 minutes. I=1C×2.0 Ah=2.0 A Q=It=2.0 A×1800 s=3600 C = 1.0 Ah, half the cell At C/20 the same half takes ten hours, at 0.10 A.

A test is a recipe of currents, limits and rests; the cell answers with its voltage.

At rest, a cell’s voltage is the sum of the potential jumps at its two electrode-electrolyte boundaries (module 3). Draw a current and two things change. The ions must be driven through the electrolyte, which has a resistance, so the potential now slopes across it, cations running down the slope and anions up it: the ohmic drop, IR. And each boundary has to be pushed off its equilibrium before its reaction keeps pace with the current; that extra push is the electrode’s overpotential.

Goodenough and Park put the whole cell in two lines. On discharge the terminals give the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization η; on charge the charger must supply the open-circuit voltage plus it:

Vdis=VOC−η,Vch=VOC+η

Winter and Brodd split the polarization into three parts: activation, from the charge-transfer reactions at the interfaces; ohmic, from the resistance of the cell’s components and contacts; and concentration, from the limits of mass transport.

Polarization or overpotential? You will meet both. Winter and Brodd use polarization for the whole cell, η=EOCV−ET, the open-circuit voltage minus the voltage under load; Bard, Faulkner and White use overpotential, η=E−Eeq, for one electrode, with its own sign. This page keeps them apart.

Current costs voltage: a working cell gives less than its open-circuit voltage, and charging it takes more.

Record the voltage while the current runs and you get the discharge curve. Winter and Brodd draw its typical shape with the three polarizations marked, and it tells you the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the health of the cell. A LiCoO2 electrode, for instance, is charged only to about 4.2 V, using about half its lithium, for safety.

Push harder and the curve pays twice. The polarization grows with the current, so the two branches move apart; and the discharge reaches its cut-off sooner, before all the charge is out. That missing capacity is not lost for good: at high current the transfer of ions becomes diffusion-limited, and what the cell could not deliver comes back at a gentler rate.

Worked example. A 2 Ah cell with VOC = 3.8 V and an internal resistance of 50 mΩ, run at 1C, that is 2 A, counting only the ohmic part. η=IRb=2 A×0.050 Ω=0.10 V discharge: 3.8−0.1=3.7 V; charge: 3.8+0.1=3.9 V EoutEin=3.7×2 Ah3.9×2 Ah=0.95; the other 5 % leaves as heat Double the current and the ohmic part alone doubles: 0.2 V each way, and the share returned falls to 90 %.

The gap between the charge and discharge branches is energy paid as heat on every cycle, and it widens with current.

The three parts can be told apart by their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears almost instantly, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10−4 to 10−2 s. Concentration polarization is slow: 10−2 s or longer. So switch the current off and time the recovery: the step is ohmic, the fast relaxation activation, the slow drift concentration. Interrupting the current in this way is how the polarizations are separated in practice.

The middle clock is set by the charge-transfer reaction itself. A reaction that runs fast in both directions at equilibrium, one with a large exchange current, needs only a small overpotential to carry a given current, and battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10−2 A/cm2 are favoured. Ohmic, activation, concentration: instant, milliseconds, tens of milliseconds and longer.

Why does a battery’s curve look the way it does? It helps to start from two extremes. Olson, López and Dickinson, following Conway, define an ideal battery material as one that supplies all its capacity through one uniform chemical process at one fixed potential. Its charge per volt, the differential capacity dq/dU, is a single spike, and the energy it stores is simply proportional to the charge.

The opposite extreme is an ideal capacitor material: its differential capacity is constant, its specific capacitance Csp, so the potential rises in a straight line as charge goes in.

battery:  U=Ueqcapacitor:  U=Uref+qCsp

“Ideal” here means a mathematical limit, not the best material for a job. And “capacitor material” covers more than capacitors: double layers, surface reactions, and insertion electrodes that take lithium as a single phase all behave this way to some degree. For any real material, a capacity only means something together with the voltage window it was measured in.

A real supercapacitor

Moya charged and discharged three commercial double-layer capacitors at constant current and read their curves with a small circuit. It has a resistance RH in series with the capacitance C, plus an inner branch, a resistance Ri in parallel with a capacitance Ci, that he links to the diffusion of ions in the capacitor. v(t)=RHI0+RiI0(1−e−t/τi)+I0tC,τi=RiCi

Reverse the current and the voltage drops at once by 2RHI0, because the current has changed by twice I0. The bend that follows is twice as large as at the start, and Moya measured that 2 : 1 ratio on all three capacitors (for the 1 F one at 10 mA, 0.34 mV on charge and 0.69 mV on discharge; his fitted circuit gives larger bends, so only the ratio is compared here). A battery stores charge at a fixed potential, a capacitor in proportion to its voltage; real electrodes sit between the two.

Why are some curves flat and others sloping? Winter and Brodd answer with the Gibbs phase rule, which counts the freedoms F left to a system of C components in P phases:

F=C−P+2

When the reaction turns one solid phase into another, two phases coexist. With temperature and pressure fixed nothing is left free, so the voltage stays constant: a plateau. When lithium dissolves into a single phase, the composition stays free and the voltage slopes with it.

Olson and co-workers give a neat example. An electrode with the overall composition Li0.75M can be one uniform phase of that composition, or half Li0.5M and half LiM. In the second case, adding lithium only changes the proportions of two fixed phases; materials that split like this are often called staging materials.

LiFePO4, measured

LiFePO4 is the classic two-phase case. Most of its range is a mixture of a lithium-poor and a lithium-rich phase. Safari and Delacourt measured its open-circuit curve as the average of a charge and a discharge at C/100 and fitted it: flat at 3.43 V vs Li over the middle of the range, sloping at both ends, where the material takes lithium as a partial solid solution.

Even this plateau is not perfectly single-valued. Dreyer and co-workers found a gap of about 20 mV between the charge and discharge voltages even at C/1000. That is a true, thermodynamic hysteresis, which they traced to the many particles of the electrode.

The spinel LiMn2O4 shows steps near 4 V and a drop to 3 V. Which shape is better depends on the use: electronics prefer a flat, steady voltage, while a sloping curve makes it easy to read how much charge is left.

Two phases pin the voltage to a plateau; one phase lets it slope.

Real electrodes are not one particle but billions, and they do not all react together. LiFePO4 conducts electrons very poorly, about 10−9 S/cm on its own, and is made usable by coating its particles with carbon and making them small. Each particle then gets its electrons through its contact with the conductive carbon, and some contacts are better than others.

Safari and Delacourt built that into a simple model, a resistive reactant: four groups of identical particles, differing only in their contact resistance. Its parameters were fitted to their cells, and it does not try to describe the two phases inside a particle at all. It reproduced their charge and discharge curves from C/25 to 1C with one set of parameters, the first charge simulation of LiFePO4 they knew of.

Why the plateau tilts

Their measured curves tilt more steeply in the middle as the rate rises. Since the open-circuit curve there is flat, that means the cell’s resistance grows as the charge or discharge goes on. The model explains why: the particles with the lowest resistance react first, the worse-connected ones later, and the faster the current, the stronger the effect.

They tested the idea directly. Adding one or two extra steel discs inside the coin cell squeezed the electrode harder. The curves sloped less and the cells delivered more, and the fitted contact resistance of the worst group fell by up to 44 %.

LiFePO4 also gives more on charge than on discharge at the same rate, by 8 % at 3C and 11.5 % at 5C in their cells. The model captures this with a solid diffusion coefficient that is lower when the particle is full of lithium.

An electrode with a memory

Bring the electrode to half charge in two ways, by charging from empty or discharging from full, rest it for two hours, then charge it at 1C. The two charges differ: 3.96 % in delivered capacity in their cells. This is path dependence, and it fades at low rates.

It has two ingredients. The particle groups end up holding different amounts of lithium, and nothing evens them out. Lithium moves between particles only where the open-circuit curve slopes, because only there does a fuller particle sit at a different voltage from an emptier one. On the plateau it barely moves, even after two hours of rest. Near 5 % charge, on the sloping end, the same experiment gave about a fifth of the effect.

A flat curve hides a crowd of particles reacting in turn; how well each is wired decides the rate, and the past decides the present.

An electrode is not a solid block. It is a composite: particles of active material, often wired together by a conductive additive such as carbon, with the pores between them filled with electrolyte. Doyle, Fuller and Newman modelled a composite of TiS2 particles, themselves good electronic conductors, mixed with a polymer electrolyte, treating the particles and the electrolyte as two continua laid over each other. Their cell had a lithium foil, the same polymer electrolyte as separator and this TiS2 positive electrode.

Their computed discharges show the main lesson at once. The faster the current, the less of the material is used before the voltage collapses: at 20 A/m2 the voltage drops sharply when only about 30 % of the positive material has reacted.

Where the reaction happens

At first the reaction crowds near the separator. The particles carry electrons about 105 times better than the polymer carries ions, so the short ionic path wins. As the particles at the front fill up, the reaction moves towards the middle of the electrode.

Meanwhile the salt piles up near the lithium foil and is drawn down at the back of the electrode, where it cannot be replaced fast enough. There the conductivity falls and the material is poorly used.

Two dimensionless numbers decide which transport matters, each a ratio of a transport time to the discharge time. For diffusion inside their particles, of 1 µm radius, it was only 0.0001, so solid diffusion hardly mattered: the particles would have had to be as large as the electrode is thick. For the electrolyte it was 0.15 at 10 A/m2, and at high currents it is the electrolyte that ends the discharge early.

The design lesson follows. In general, thinner electrodes use their material better when the electrolyte is the bottleneck, at the cost of harder processing and the extra weight of more, thinner cells. They also suggested a stronger starting salt solution, because the 1 M presumably chosen for its conductivity is far weaker by the time it reaches the depths of the electrode.

At high current the back of a thick electrode starves of ions, so the cell stops with material unused.

A voltage curve hides a lot in its gentle bends. Plot its slope instead and the bends become peaks. Two plots are used: the differential voltage dV/dQ against charge, and the differential capacity dQ/dV against voltage.

Peaks in differential capacity correspond to plateaus, where two phases are in equilibrium. Materials that take lithium as one smooth solid solution show no sharp peaks. Olson and co-workers quote Safari and Delacourt’s study of an aged graphite and LiFePO4 cell (a different paper from the one behind figures 5.6 and 5.7): “A peak in the [dQ/dV] plot features the equilibrium between different electrochemically-active phases in the electrode whereas a peak in the [dV/dQ] plot locates the transition from one equilibrium to another.”

Two electrodes in one curve

A cell’s voltage is the positive electrode’s potential minus the negative’s, each set by how much lithium that electrode holds:

V=Upos(xpos)−Uneg(xneg),dVdQ=dUposdQ−dUnegdQ

The two electrodes’ slopes simply add, so every dV/dQ peak of the cell can be traced to one electrode. A dQ/dV peak needs the voltage to stand still while charge flows, so both electrodes must be on a plateau at once.

The square plot and ageing

Olson and co-workers draw the state of a cell on a square plot: the negative electrode’s lithium content across, the positive electrode’s (inverted) up. A cell charging from empty to full is a straight line on it. The slope of the line is the ratio of the electrodes’ capacities, and where it meets the left edge measures the lithium the cell has lost for good.

1−xpos=axneg+b

Ageing comes in two classic kinds. Loss of lithium inventory (LLI) is cyclable lithium used up by side reactions. Loss of active material (LAM) is material that has changed irreversibly, dissolved or come loose from the current collector. LLI moves the line up; LAM tilts it, losing positive material steepens it and losing negative material flattens it. The window between the cut-offs then picks out a shorter piece of line, so the capacity falls.

In their LiCoO2 and graphite example, lost lithium leaves the graphite peaks where they were on the charge axis but moves the LiCoO2 peaks to lower charge. The toy cell does the same. Differentiating real data is sensitive to noise: they used slow C/20 data and thinned it 144-fold before differentiating, to reduce the noise.

Slopes turn a smooth curve into peaks that can be traced to each electrode, and their shifts tell lost lithium from lost material.

Two numbers summarize a cell’s life. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in on charge, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value.

CE=100×QdisQch %

A commercial rechargeable cell must survive at least 300 full cycles with less than 20 % loss; most need three to eight hours to charge, a quarter of an hour is what users want, and fast charging shortens cycle life.

Not every loss is permanent. Capacity hidden by a high rate comes back at a low one; what does not come back is lost to changes in the electrodes’ volume, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes. The first charge of a lithium-ion cell is a special case, a loss paid once (module 6).

Worked example. How good must the Coulombic efficiency be for a cell to keep 80 % of its capacity after 300 cycles? Take the simplest case, in which every bit of charge that does not come back is lost for good. after 1 cycle: CE; after 2 cycles: CE×CE=CE2; after N cycles: CEN the goal: CE300=0.80 take the 300th root: CE=0.801/300=0.99926, that is 99.93 % the other way round, at 99.9 %: 0.999N=0.80⇒N=ln0.80ln0.999=−0.223−0.001=223 cycles So losing just one coulomb in a thousand on every cycle uses up the 20 % allowance after 223 cycles, well short of 300.

Lifetime is set by tiny losses repeated hundreds of times.

The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. An irreversible part, often loosely called Joule heat, comes from the whole polarization: it is released whichever way the current flows. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction, and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage, dE/dT. If it is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as nickel-cadmium does; lead-acid has a negative coefficient and does the opposite. Bernardi’s energy balance writes the two side by side:

Q˙=I(U−V)−ITdUdT

The heat is released inside the cell, at the electrode surfaces, so a cell meant for high currents has to be built to shed it. Because the entropic term changes sign with the current, the heat it takes in on one half of a cycle it gives back on the other; the irreversible term only ever adds up.

Go deeper: the exchange current, the Butler-Volmer equation and the Tafel line

For one electrode, the current and the overpotential η=E−Eeq are linked by the Butler-Volmer equation. Bard, Faulkner and White write it with reduction current positive,

i=i0[exp(−αfη)−exp((1−α)fη)],f=FRT

with α the transfer coefficient (between 0.3 and 0.7 in most cases) and i0 the exchange current. It holds while the current stays below about 10 % of the limiting current, so that mass transport does not interfere. Near equilibrium the exponentials can be expanded and the current is simply proportional to η: the electrode behaves like a resistor, the charge-transfer resistance Rct=RT/(Fi0) for a one-electron reaction. A large exchange current means a small Rct.

Far from equilibrium one exponential wins and η grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation η=a+blogi, which holds once |η| exceeds about 118 mV at 25 °C; Winter and Brodd quote the same empirical form. Winter and Brodd write it with oxidation current positive (their printed eq. 13 lost a bracket and a sign); the physics is the same with the roles of α and 1−α swapped.

One caution about the heat equation. It takes the open-circuit voltage U from a slow measurement and dU/dT from small temperature steps, and Jindal and co-workers found that it estimates heat reasonably during continuous discharge but overestimates it during pulsed discharge, by up to 26 % for a LiFePO4 cell and 49 % for an NMC cell.

Module 6

Inside a lithium-ion cell

Where does a lithium ion actually sit, and what happens on the very first charge?

Where we are. Module 5 watched the voltage curve from outside, at the terminals. Now go inside, with our ion: where it sits in each electrode, how it gets in and out, and the one-off price a new cell pays the first time it is charged.

Neither electrode of a lithium-ion cell is used up. Both are insertion hosts: solids that conduct ions and electrons and let guest ions in and out by a solid-state redox reaction. Because their structure and shape survive, insertion electrodes are highly reversible; for layered hosts the process is called intercalation.

Replacing the lithium-metal negative electrode with a second insertion host gave the lithium-ion or rocking-chair cell: demonstrated by Murphy and by Scrosati, put on the market by Sony in June 1991 as carbon against LiCoO2. In the graphite, lithium sits between the carbon sheets, up to one lithium per six carbons, LiC6.

In LiCoO2 it sits between the oxide layers; only about half of it is taken out on charge, to about 4.2 V, because once more than about 0.55 of it is removed the oxide starts to give off oxygen.

Worked example. How many ions rock in a 3 Ah phone cell on each full discharge? charge = 3 Ah × 3600 s/h = 10 800 C ions = 10 800 C ÷ 1.602 × 10−19 C = 6.7 × 1022, that is 10 800 ÷ 96 485 = 0.112 mol of lithium mass of that lithium = 0.112 mol × 6.94 g/mol = 0.78 g Less than a gram of lithium travels back and forth; the rest of the cell is the hosts, the electrolyte and the packaging that let it do so.

Nothing is consumed in an ideal lithium-ion cell: the ions only change address.

Graphite: sheets, and lithium by stages

Look closer at the two hosts. Graphite is a stack of graphene sheets: carbon atoms joined in hexagonal rings, the sheets held to one another by weak van der Waals forces and π–π interactions, 0.335 nm apart; in natural graphite they are mostly stacked in the order A, B, A, B. The lithium goes into the gaps between the sheets, at most one lithium for every six carbons, LiC6. The edges of the sheets are the favourable sites for lithium to go in, and it diffuses faster there; the flat faces, the basal planes, are less reactive.

It does not fill all the gaps at once. Lithium fills some gaps and leaves the others empty, forming stages: the stage number is the number of graphene sheets between two lithium layers. As the cell charges, the graphite passes from stage 4 through stage 3 to stage 2, LiC12, and finally stage 1, LiC6, with lithium in every gap. Stages 2 and 3 are stable phases that affect the equilibrium potential and the diffusion of lithium. Each change of stage is favoured thermodynamically but held back kinetically, the last one, from LiC12 to LiC6, most of all. Rüdorff and Hofmann pictured each stage as complete lithium layers, as the figure draws them; in the Daumas–Hérold picture the guest forms islands within the layers instead. Full lithiation swells the graphite by about 10 %, mostly by the sheets moving apart, not by the sheets stretching.

LiCoO2: lithium between oxide sheets

LiCoO2 is a layered compound too: cobalt surrounded by oxygen in CoO2 sheets, with the lithium in the layer between two of them. The idea came from sodium, which Hagenmuller and co-workers had put between such sheets in the 1970s; in 1979 and 1980 Goodenough’s group made the lithium compound, at twice the voltage of TiS2. On charge only about half of its lithium is taken out.

The sheets are what makes this work. Lithium comes and goes; the framework of carbon or of cobalt oxide stays, so the electrodes keep their structure and shape and the reaction can run back and forth many times.

Two layered solids and one guest: charging moves lithium from between the oxide sheets to between the carbon sheets, and discharging moves it back.

The first charge

A lithium-ion cell is assembled discharged, with all its lithium in the positive electrode. On the first charge the graphite is driven down to about 0.2 V vs Li/Li+, below the lower edge of the window in which the carbonate electrolyte is stable (module 3). The electrolyte is reduced at the graphite surface, and its products form a passivating layer, the solid-electrolyte interphase or SEI. Forming it consumes lithium from the positive electrode irreversibly, and the layer adds to the interfacial impedance; it also changes as the cell cycles.

Among the solvents, ethylene carbonate is reduced first, from about 0.8 V vs Li/Li+ (with LiPF6 the anion starts earlier; see Go deeper). Two EC molecules, two lithium ions and two electrons give lithium ethylene dicarbonate (Li2EDC) and ethylene gas; the layer that forms has a dense inorganic inner part next to the graphite (Li2CO3, LiF, Li2O) and a porous organic outer part.

A crystalline layer only 2 to 3 nm thick would already stop electrons tunnelling through, but on graphite the SEI soon grows past 10 nm and reaches about 50 nm after cycling.

Why the layer helps

What makes the SEI useful is its selectivity: it is electronically insulating, so it stops further reduction, yet it conducts Li+, so the graphite keeps filling. Of the main solvents, only ethylene carbonate forms a protective layer on graphite, which is why it is in almost every commercial electrolyte; propylene carbonate does not, and in 2001 Tarascon and Armand still called the reason unclear.

The explanation usually given, which goes back to Dahn and co-workers in 1990, is that EC decomposes first and builds an SEI that stops PC from slipping into the graphite together with the lithium; PC holds on to its Li+ more strongly, goes into the graphite with it, and peels the graphite layers apart.

Brandt’s 1994 review put it simply: the layer forms during the first insertion, and after it intercalation is completely reversible. Module 10 shows that this is only nearly true. An electrode that works inside the window needs no SEI: lithium titanate at 1.5 V forms none, and Zaghib and co-workers reported electrodes of lithium titanate and LiFePO4 that survived 30 000 cycles at 5C.

Steering the SEI on purpose

Today the SEI is also steered on purpose, with small amounts of additives that decompose first and leave a thin, controlled layer. The best known is vinylene carbonate (VC): reduced at the graphite above 1 V vs Li/Li+, it forms a passivation layer, and it suppresses the decomposition of PC.

Why new cells are “formed”. Because the SEI decides how the cell will age, a factory does not leave its growth to chance: near the end of making a cell, it is cycled slowly under precisely defined conditions, for up to a day, and let the gas out before the cell is sealed for good. It is expensive, about a third (32 %) of the cost of making a cell, mostly for keeping cells warm while they condition. And the first cycle is where the big loss happens: in one recent laboratory cell the first cycle returned about 82.5 % of the charge put in, the second about 97.5 %.

Not only on graphite. A positive electrode pushed above the upper edge of the window, about 4.3 V, oxidizes the electrolyte instead; LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4 at about 4.75 V is protected by a passivation layer of its own, which Goodenough and Park also call an SEI.

The SEI costs most of its lithium on the first charge, and then lets the cell work outside its electrolyte’s window.

The host decides how the ion can move once it is inside. In the layered oxides lithium travels within two-dimensional planes; the spinel LiMn2O4 offers a three-dimensional network; in olivine LiFePO4 the paths are one-dimensional channels.

Inside the solid the ion moves by diffusion, down its concentration gradient, as Fick’s first law describes; in a time t it typically gets about (2Dt)1/2 from where it started, where D is its diffusion coefficient. Turned around, emptying a particle of size L takes a time of order L2/2D: halve the particle and it empties four times faster. Module 8 returns to that when the particles shrink to nanometres.

The pieces arrived one at a time. In 1972, at a conference at Belgirate, Steele singled out layered dichalcogenides such as TiS2 as positive electrodes and Armand set out what an intercalation electrode needs; in 1976 lithium went reversibly into TiS2 against a lithium-metal electrode. Exxon’s cell then failed on dendrites that shorted it and ignited the electrolyte. Armand’s answer in the late 1970s was the rocking chair, and Lazzari and Scrosati built the first one, LiₓWO2 against TiS2, at about 2 V.

Around 1980 Goodenough’s group showed that over half the lithium could be taken out of LiCoO2 reversibly, at about 4 V, so that a cell could be built discharged. In 1983 Yoshino found that petroleum coke cycled in a carbonate electrolyte and proposed carbon against LiCoO2; Sony’s cell of June 1991 delivered about 80 Wh/kg and 200 Wh/L, more than any other rechargeable of the time.

With the LiPF6 in EC/DMC electrolyte proposed in 1993 and decades of refinement, the same chemistry reached about 250 Wh/kg and 600 Wh/L by 2020. Since its launch the lithium-ion cell has gained a factor of three to four in energy density while its price fell by a factor of 18.

You can now explain where the lithium sits in graphite and in LiCoO2, why the cell is called a rocking chair, why a new cell loses lithium on its first charge and what it gets in return, how the host shapes the ion’s path, and how the cell came together between 1972 and 1996.

The ion had to cross something to get there.

Go deeper: why the SEI forms where it does

Goodenough and Park draw the electrolyte as having its own window: an energy gap between its lowest unoccupied and highest occupied molecular orbitals. A negative electrode whose electrochemical potential μA lies above the lowest unoccupied orbital reduces the electrolyte; a positive electrode whose μC lies below the highest occupied orbital oxidizes it, unless a passivating layer blocks the electron transfer. For the carbonate electrolytes the lowest unoccupied orbital sits about 1.1 eV below the lithium level and the practical limit at the top is about 4.3 eV below it, which on the voltage scale of module 3 is the window from about 1.1 to about 4.3 V vs Li/Li+. Graphite, at about 0.2 V, is outside it, and so works only behind its SEI.

The orbital picture is a simplification. Real reduction and oxidation potentials depend on the salt and on the molecules around each one: estimates from isolated molecules can be far off: oxidizing EC next to PF6−, with a hydrogen passed to a neighbour, takes almost 2 V less than oxidizing an isolated EC molecule, and in an EC/EMC electrolyte reduction is measured to start at about 1.7 V with LiPF6 but about 0.8 V with LiBF4. Most solvents and salt anions are thermodynamically unstable where lithium inserts into graphite; the electrolyte survives there only kinetically, behind the insulating interphase it has built.

What a good SEI has to do comes down to four things: block electrons, so that no more electrolyte is reduced; let Li+ through without much hindrance; stay chemically stable, neither reacting with nor dissolving into the electrolyte; and stay mechanically intact while the electrode under it swells and shrinks. Peled introduced the idea of an electronically insulating, ionically conducting interphase in 1979.

Module 7

The electrolyte

How does an ion travel through a liquid, a polymer or a solid?

Where we are. Module 6 put our ion in its two hosts. Between them it crosses the electrolyte, the part of the cell that must carry ions and stop electrons. This module starts from nothing: what the liquid in a lithium-ion cell is made of, how its molecules sit around each ion, how an ion moves through it, what the electric field inside it looks like, and how the arrangement changes, layer by layer, as you approach an electrode. Then it compares the liquid with polymers and ceramics, and shows why the anion matters as much as the lithium.

Everything in this module is made of four kinds of object, so meet them first. A molecule is a group of atoms held together by chemical bonds; the figure draws each atom as a ball and each bond as a stick. The two solvents of a lithium-ion cell are both carbonates, built round a carbon atom bonded to three oxygen atoms. In ethylene carbonate, EC, the carbonate closes into a ring; in dimethyl carbonate, DMC, it stays an open chain: EC is a cyclic carbonate and DMC a linear one.

In both, one oxygen is double-bonded to the carbon and sticks out of the molecule: the carbonyl oxygen. A lithium ion is held by a solvent through the spare pairs of electrons on such a donor atom. The salt brings the other two objects: the lithium ion, Li+, a lithium atom that has lost one electron, and the hexafluorophosphate anion, PF6−, six fluorine atoms around a phosphorus atom, carrying one negative charge.

Electron clouds and charge

A molecule is not a set of hard balls. Its electrons form a cloud, densest at the atoms and thinning out over a fraction of a nanometre, and the figure shows that cloud as calculated for this page. The calculation also shows where each molecule is slightly negative and where slightly positive. EC has a negative end, at the carbonyl oxygen, and a positive end, at its CH2 groups: a strongly polar molecule, with a computed dipole moment of about 5 debye. In DMC the two halves almost cancel, about 0.3 debye. The same contrast shows up in the two liquids’ dielectric constants, 90 for EC against 3.1 for DMC, in the next figure; the simulations quoted below likewise call EC the polar solvent and DMC the less polar one.

Four objects to recognize: EC, a ring; DMC, a chain; PF6−, a ball of fluorine; and Li+, the smallest of all.

The recipe

An electrolyte has one job that defines it: to provide purely ionic conduction between the two electrodes. It is a solution: a salt dissolved in a liquid, the solvent. Most commercial lithium-ion cells use roughly 1 mol of the salt lithium hexafluorophosphate, LiPF6, per litre of a mixture of carbonate solvents: a cyclic one such as ethylene carbonate (EC) or propylene carbonate (PC), and a linear one such as dimethyl carbonate (DMC), ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) or diethyl carbonate (DEC).

Water, the obvious solvent, is out: it would be split at the voltage of a lithium cell (module 3). Water-based electrolytes conduct best, about 1 S/cm; the organic liquids reach 10−2 to 10−3 S/cm, because they dissolve salts less well and let the ions pair up.

Why two solvents

Why two solvents? Because each does one job well and the other badly. A good solvent needs a high dielectric constant (relative permittivity), to pull the salt apart into separate ions, and a high fluidity, so that the ions can move. EC has a relative permittivity of 90 but is thick, 1.9 mPa s, and melts at about 36 °C, so on its own it is a solid at room temperature. DMC flows easily, 0.59 mPa s, but its relative permittivity is only 3.1.

Mixed, they stay liquid far lower: a mixture of carbonates has a eutectic, the composition with the lowest melting point, at −8.6 °C for EC with DMC and −64.7 °C for EC with DMC and EMC (the solvents alone, without salt), and blends near the eutectic are preferred to work in the cold. EC is usually kept below 50 % by volume, preferably 30 %, because more of it comes out as a solid at low temperature.

A pinch of additives

Real electrolytes also carry minute amounts of additives, each with a job: some decompose on purpose to build a thin, controlled SEI on the graphite (module 6), others protect the positive electrode or stop overcharge (module 12); no single additive can do everything, and finding the right combination is now central to electrolyte work.

Why LiPF6

Why LiPF6? It is used almost exclusively in commercial lithium-ion cells because it has shown the best balance of the properties a salt needs: its solutions are among the most conductive known, it forms a stable surface on the aluminium current collector at high potential, and with carbonate solvents it forms a stable SEI on graphite (module 6). Its weakness is its P–F bond, which is easily broken: the salt reacts readily with water and is not very stable to heat, and hydrofluoric acid, HF, in LiPF6 electrolytes is one of the principal concerns about using it. It became the standard only when carbon replaced lithium metal and the solvents were optimized for the lithium-ion cells Sony introduced in 1991.

Worked example. Counting the crowd: 1 mol of LiPF6 dissolved in 1 litre of EC : DMC 3 : 7 by volume (the salt’s own volume neglected). EC: 300 cm3 × 1.32 g/cm3 ÷ 88.1 g/mol = 4.5 mol DMC: 700 cm3 × 1.06 g/cm3 ÷ 90.1 g/mol = 8.2 mol so for each Li+ and each PF6−: about 13 solvent molecules each Li+ has a cube of liquid about (1 L ÷ 6.02 × 1023)^1/3 = 1.2 nm on a side to itself, on average A lithium ion is never more than a few molecules from the next one, and most of what surrounds it is solvent.

The liquid in a lithium-ion cell is mostly solvent: a dozen or so carbonate molecules for every lithium ion, two kinds of solvent because neither does both jobs.

Choosing a salt

A salt for a lithium-ion electrolyte has a list of jobs. It must give a conductive solution, stay stable within the cell’s voltage window and at high temperature, help form a good SEI, protect the aluminium current collector of the positive electrode above about 3.6 V vs Li, and resist water. It should also be cheap and not toxic. Failing any one of these rules a salt out of practical use, and every one of them depends on the rest of the recipe.

Line the candidates up and no salt wins everywhere. LiClO4 conducts well and is stable, but its anion is a strong oxidant, which makes the salt a potential explosive. LiAsF6 behaves much like LiPF6, but reduction could turn its arsenic into highly toxic forms. LiBF4 resists water and heat better than LiPF6 but conducts significantly less.

LiTFSI and lithium triflate shrug off water and heat, but in ordinary dilute solutions they corrode the aluminium foil at high potential. In PC:DME the aluminium repassivation potential, above which corrosion pits keep growing, is 3.7 V vs Li with LiTFSI, against above 5 V with LiPF6. So LiPF6, with its known weaknesses, keeps its place in the recipe of figure 7.2.

Choosing the solvents

The five carbonates of commercial electrolytes split into two families. The cyclic ones, EC and PC, have relative permittivities of 65 to 90 and flash points above 130 °C, but they are viscous, and EC is a solid below 36 °C. The linear ones, DMC, EMC and DEC, flow two and a half to four times more easily and, apart from DMC, stay liquid far below 0 °C. But their permittivities are only about 3, and they flash at 17 to 25 °C, near room temperature.

All five resist oxidation on glassy carbon to above 6 V vs Li. Henderson warns that a window measured on inert carbon is not a clear guide to stability against real electrode materials.

Every salt and every solvent fails at something; a commercial electrolyte is the mixture whose weaknesses matter least.

What does it mean for a salt to dissolve? Solid LiPF6 is made of Li+ cations and PF6− anions, which attract each other because their charges are opposite (module 0). In a carbonate solvent the Li+ ions are pulled out of it by the solvent molecules: each solvent molecule has an oxygen atom with spare pairs of electrons (in a carbonate, the carbonyl oxygen), and it binds to the lithium through them. The anions are, in general, left bare, because these solvents have no acidic hydrogen to hold them: in water an anion is held by hydrogen bonds, in a carbonate it is not. The anions can bind to Li+ in the same way, so solvent and anion compete for the places around each lithium ion, and that competition decides what is actually present in the liquid.

And the anion?

So does a PF6− anion get a shell of its own? Not in the way Li+ does. Solvent molecules do come close to it, and simulations treat a PF6− next to an EC molecule as a complex, but nothing binds there the way the oxygens bind to lithium, and the neighbours keep changing. In the figure they are drawn faint, with their slightly positive CH ends (blue in figure 7.1) towards the anion.

Dissolving is a tug of war for the lithium ion, and in a good solvent the solvent wins.

The shell

How many solvent molecules fit round one lithium ion? Few. Simulations find about four solvent molecules bound strongly around a lithium ion, arranged as a tetrahedron, and about 4.2 to 4.4 partners, solvent and anions together, in EC : DMC and EC : PC with LiPF6; neutron diffraction on LiPF6 in PC finds 4.5 PC molecules on average, each oxygen 0.204 nm from the lithium.

This group is the ion’s first solvation shell. In the liquid, simulations find EC and DMC in it about equally often, even though an isolated lithium ion in the gas phase prefers EC. The shell is not a fixed object: a given EC molecule stays in it for about half a nanosecond, an anion partner for 2 to 3 ns.

Pairs and clusters

When an anion wins a place, the two ions form a contact ion pair; when solvent stays between them, they are a solvent-separated ion pair; larger clusters are aggregates. The anion matters: the tendency to pair up rises in the order LiAsF6 and LiPF6, then LiTFSI, LiClO4, LiBF4 and lithium triflate.

The solvent matters too: in simulations of EC : DMC the fraction of free ions rises nearly linearly with the share of EC; and measured with LiTFSI, the conductivity reaches 62 % of what fully independent ions would give in PC, but only 11 % in DMC. Not every cluster is lost to conduction: charged clusters carry current too.

A lithium ion never travels alone: it carries a shell of about four solvent molecules, and sometimes an anion.

Wandering

Even with no current, nothing in the liquid is still. Every ion is bumped continually by the molecules around it and wanders at random, in every direction alike. On average it goes nowhere; it only spreads out, a distance that grows as the square root of time, (2Dt)1/2, where D is its diffusion coefficient. This is diffusion, met inside the electrode particles in module 6.

Drifting in a field

Draw current and a small electric field appears across the liquid. It does not replace the random wandering; it biases it: each step is pushed slightly one way, cations down the potential and anions up it. That steady bias is migration, and the Nernst–Planck equation adds the two together (module 0).

What moves is the ion together with whatever stays bound to it, which is why a bigger moving object, or a thicker liquid, conducts less: u = |z|e/(6πηr). The lithium ion moves in two ways: it carries its first shell along with it, or it is handed on, leaving some solvent molecules behind and gaining others. In EC : DMC with LiPF6 the two contribute roughly equally; in short ethylene-oxide chains (oligoethers) the ion travels a distance of three to four solvent molecules before its shell is renewed, so there carrying dominates.

A picture to keep: ionic conduction is a crowd crossing a corridor. More people, or people who move more freely, carry more across per second. The picture breaks in two places: the ions also push on each other through the field, and in a polymer the corridor itself has to keep moving for anyone to get through.

Worked example. How strong is the field, and how fast do the ions go? A current density of 1 mA/cm2 through a liquid of conductivity κ = 10−2 S/cm, with 1 mol/L of Li+. field in the liquid, from Ohm’s law: E = i/κ = 10−3 A/cm2 ÷ 10−2 S/cm = 0.1 V/cm in the steady state, when all the current is carried by lithium: mean velocity v = i/(Fc) = 10−3 ÷ (96 485 × 10−3) cm/s ≈ 0.1 µm per second to cross a 25 µm layer of liquid: about 4 minutes The field in the liquid is gentle and each ion drifts slowly; the current is large because there are so many ions, about 6 × 1020 in every cubic centimetre.

An ion in a liquid wanders at random; a current only nudges the wandering, gently and all the same way.

The bulk and the diffuse layer

The electrolyte is not the same all the way across. Far from either electrode, in the bulk, every small volume holds as much positive as negative charge: a separation of charge there would set up a field large enough to erase it at once. The molecules point every way, and with no current the potential is flat. With current, the only field is the gentle ohmic one of the worked example above, about 0.1 V/cm.

A few nanometres from a charged electrode this changes. Ions of the opposite sign crowd towards the surface and solvent molecules turn: together they cancel the electrode’s charge, so its field does not reach the bulk. The excess of ions thins out over the diffuse layer, less than about 10 nm thick above 0.01 M; closest to the surface is the compact layer, one solvent shell thick.

The field inside that thin step can reach 107 V/cm, a hundred million times the field in the bulk of the worked example. This is the electrical double layer of module 3, now seen molecule by molecule.

The first molecular layer

Simulations show what the innermost layer looks like in EC : DMC with LiPF6. When the electrode is charged, either way, the more polar EC replaces DMC at the surface, an effect confirmed by experiment on LiCoO2. At a negative electrode the carbonyl groups are pushed away from the surface, and at the most negative potentials lithium ions sit against it, solvated mainly by EC, which fits the observation that EC reduction dominates the SEI formed in these mixtures. At a positive electrode the layer fills with carbonyl groups and PF6− anions accumulate, where they are expected to take part in oxidation reactions.

Getting in

To enter graphite a lithium ion has to leave its shell. In preliminary simulations of bare graphite, with no SEI, a lithium ion rests about 0.5 nm from the surface, still solvated; closer than about 0.4 nm the free energy climbs steeply as it is forced to shed its solvent, consistent with DMC going first and EC last. The size of that climb agrees with measured activation energies of 16.4 to 17 kcal/mol, about 70 kJ/mol. The two contributions usually discussed for the resistance of this interface are the SEI and this desolvation, and experiments show that at low temperature the interface can resist more than the whole bulk electrolyte.

Uniform in the middle, ordered in the last nanometre: the electrolyte’s structure, and almost all of its field, change within a few molecules of the electrode.

So far, a liquid. Two other kinds of electrolyte carry lithium differently: a polymer, where there is no solvent at all, and a ceramic, where the ion moves through a solid framework.

A dry polymer such as poly(ethylene oxide), PEO, dissolves a lithium salt with no solvent at all. Wright and co-workers first saw ionic conduction in PEO complexes of sodium and potassium salts, and in 1978 Armand proposed the first dry PEO-lithium electrolyte for lithium batteries, conducting about 10−4 S/cm at 40 to 60 °C.

The Li+ ion is held by the oxygen atoms of the chain and hops from one coordinating site to the next, helped by the segmental motion of the chain; the motion happens in the amorphous, not the crystalline, regions of the polymer. That is the weakness of the idea: no chain motion, no conduction.

An electrolyte must carry ions and block electrons; how it carries them decides how fast, and at what temperature.

The polymer route improved by steps. Changing the salt in PEO from lithium triflate, LiCF3SO3, to LiTFSI, a salt Armand introduced, gains about an order of magnitude, its bulky anion acting as a plasticizer; adding 10 to 25 % of a plasticizer gains another, and such lightly plasticized polymers still suit a lithium-metal electrode. Gels, 60 to 95 % liquid held in a polymer, come within 2 to 5 times of the liquid’s conductivity but need a lithium-ion configuration. A dry-polymer lithium cell runs at up to 80 °C.

LiTFSI itself is a good salt, conductive and safe, with one catch: above about 4 V (3.7 V in Henderson’s PC:DME data) it corrodes the aluminium current collector unless a passivating salt is added. Liquid or polymer, a nonaqueous electrolyte is thermodynamically stable over a window of about 3.5 V but is used, kinetically, up to 5.5 V.

The neighbours matter: a single EC molecule is computed to resist oxidation to about 6.9 V vs Li/Li+; next to a PF6− anion, which can take a hydrogen from it as HF, the limit falls, and when the hydrogen passes to a neighbouring EC it falls to about 5 to 5.2 V, almost 2 V lower. In the usual mixture, the cyclic EC dissolves the salt and builds the SEI, while a linear carbonate such as DMC thins the liquid so it conducts better in the cold.

Worked example. What the conductivity costs, for a 25 μm electrolyte layer (an illustrative thickness) of 1 cm2, using R = l/(κA) from module 0. liquid, κ = 10−2 S/cm: R = 0.0025 cm ÷ (10−2 S/cm × 1 cm2) = 0.25 Ω a solid at κ = 10−4 S/cm: 25 Ω, a hundred times more at 1 mA: ohmic drop 0.25 mV against 25 mV This is why 10−4 S/cm is the bar a solid electrolyte has to clear, and why it has to be thin.

Both ions move, but only one is wanted. The transference number t+ is the fraction of the ionic conductivity carried by Li+; in a dilute electrolyte it is the ratio of the lithium diffusion coefficient to the sum of both. (Tarascon and Armand call it the transport number.) In the organic liquid electrolytes of 1994 it was typically below 0.5.

The rest of the current is carried by anions that no electrode can take in, so in a working cell salt builds up at one electrode and runs short at the other, and the steady-state difference grows with (1 − t+).

That gradient is the concentration polarization of module 5, seen from the electrolyte. In polymer electrolytes t+ is about 0.3; about 10 % of ceramic nanofillers lifts it to about 0.6, raises the conductivity severalfold at 60 to 80 °C, and gives a stable interface with lithium.

The logical end point is a single-ion conductor, in which the anions are tied to the polymer so that t+ approaches 1 and no salt gradient builds up; one block copolymer of this kind reached t+ above 0.85, though at only 1.3 × 10−5 S/cm at 60 °C, and conducting 10−4 S/cm at room temperature remains the challenge.

A solid separator that blocks dendrites would make a lithium-metal negative electrode possible (module 9), and Goodenough and Park list what a solid electrolyte must do: conduct Li+ at above 10−4 S/cm, block dendrites without being reduced by lithium, stay chemically stable, and form a robust, flexible thin membrane. The garnet Li7−ₓLa3Zr2−ₓTaₓO12 conducts above 10−3 S/cm and is stable against lithium; meeting all four at once, they suggested, points to ceramic-polymer composites. A polymer above 10−4 S/cm with good electrode contact had, in 2013, yet to be demonstrated.

Even a good solid conductor has to keep touching the electrodes. Electrode particles swell and shrink as lithium goes in and out, and against an inorganic solid electrolyte the contact is lost. In a liquid, the electrolyte simply flows after them.

Go deeper: conductivity from the ions themselves

The conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of what each ion contributes: κ = F Σ|zj|ujCj, with uj the mobility and Cj the concentration of ion j. The share carried by one ion is its transference number, tj = |zj|ujCj / Σ|zk|ukCk. For a salt of one cation and one anion this reduces to t+ = |z+|u+/(|z+|u+ + |z−|u−), and in the dilute limit to DLi/(DLi + D−). Module 0 gave the mobility of a sphere in a viscous liquid, u = |z|e/(6πηr): a bigger ion, or a more viscous liquid, conducts less.

How independent are the ions? Measure each ion’s self-diffusion coefficient, D+ and D−, and compute the conductivity the ions would give if each moved on its own, λNE = e2(n+D+ + n−D−)/(VkBT), for n ions of each kind in a volume V. The measured conductivity divided by it is the ionicity, αd = λ/λNE: 0.62 for LiTFSI in PC and 0.11 in DMC, both at 20 solvent molecules per lithium and 298 K. Why about 1 mol per litre in the first place? Enough salt has to dissolve to provide plenty of charge carriers, and the conductivity is frequently found to peak near 1 M.

Even water can be stretched. Water splits over a window of 1.23 V (module 3), but in “water-in-salt” electrolytes, with far more salt than water by weight, the salt crowds the electrode surfaces and its decomposition builds a LiF-rich interphase; with further measures to keep water off the negative electrode, aqueous electrolytes have reached stability windows above 4 V.

Module 8

Electrodes are composites

Why is an electrode not just a block of the good stuff?

Where we are. Our ion has crossed the electrolyte (module 7) and has to get into a particle of the positive electrode (module 6). Module 2 showed that an electrode is a coating of particles, carbon, binder and pores. This module asks what each of those is for, and what happens when one of them fails.

Most battery electrodes are porous composites: particles of the active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon or a metal powder, and a polymer binder, coated on a current collector. About 30 % of the coating is pore space, so the surface where the reaction can happen is far larger than the electrode’s geometric area. A large surface lowers the current density at each point of it, and with it the polarization.

Each ingredient keeps one road open. The carbon carries electrons from the foil to every particle; the electrolyte in the pores carries ions from the separator to every particle; the binder holds the coating together and onto the foil. A particle that one of the two roads cannot reach stores nothing and only adds mass.

A picture to keep: a town where every house needs two roads, one for each of two deliveries that must arrive together, and is only lived in if both reach it. The picture breaks because here the roads also have tolls: the farther a delivery travels through the coating, the more voltage it costs, which is the next figure.

A particle that the electrons or the ions cannot reach contributes nothing.

Inside a porous electrode the reaction is not spread evenly. Where it happens depends on the conductivities of the solid matrix and of the electrolyte, the exchange current, diffusion, the thickness of the electrode, its porosity, pore size and tortuosity; and a nonuniform current distribution wastes active material.

The reaction concentrates where the electrons and the ions together have the shortest, cheapest way to meet. In the simplest theory the profile is a hyperbolic cosine with a characteristic depth; Pathak and Bazant find this reaction-limited picture most realistic for thin electrodes, of order 10 μm, and in need of corrections beyond about 100 μm.

The electrolyte inside the pores conducts worse than in bulk, because the ions have less room and a winding path. Boz and co-workers give the usual correction, κeff = κεα, with ε the porosity and α = 1.5 (1.53 for LiFePO4 electrodes). Ionic transport is far slower than electronic transport, which is also why cells use thin electrodes of large area.

Worked example. How well does the electrolyte conduct inside a 30 % porous electrode? κeff/κ = 0.301.5 = 0.164 for a liquid of 10−2 S/cm: κeff = 1.6 × 10−3 S/cm, about a sixth Every micrometre of coating the ions must cross costs six times the voltage it would in the open electrolyte; another reason the reaction in figure 8.2 crowds toward the separator.

An active material that conducts electrons poorly works only where it touches the conductive additive, and the reacted zone spreads outward from that contact; Winter and Brodd show it for MnO2. LiFePO4 is the modern case: its poor electronic conductivity was overcome by coating the particles with carbon and making them small.

The coating Armand and co-workers introduced is made by decomposing a polymer precursor on the particles, which leaves a uniform layer of carbon; the once dead-end material is now produced in thousands of tonnes a year.

Small particles bring the ions closer to every atom: the time to cross a particle falls with the square of its size (module 6). But carbon-coated nanoparticles have a lower tap density, so the electrode holds less energy per litre, and coating them evenly gets harder as they shrink. Highly porous aerogel electrodes pay the same price, at a tap density of about 0.2 g/cm3.

The composite of this module is made in a few industrial steps. The powders, about 90 % active material by mass with conductive carbon and a polymer binder, are mixed into a slurry, coated on the current-collector foil, dried so that the pores form, and pressed between rollers to an even, denser layer; the electrodes are then cut, stacked or wound with the separator, filled with electrolyte, and cycled for the first time to form the SEI.

Every step leaves its mark on the roads of figure 8.1: where the carbon and binder end up, how open the pores stay, how well the electrolyte wets them.

Module 9

Lithium metal, sodium and organic electrodes

If lithium metal is the best negative electrode, why is it not in your phone?

Where we are. Act B followed our ion through a lithium-ion cell that works. Act C is about how that journey goes wrong and what is being done about it. It starts with the materials beyond today’s cell: the lithium metal that the rocking chair replaced, the materials that store more than graphite, and the ions and molecules that might one day replace lithium and cobalt.

A lithium-metal negative electrode would give the highest energy of all, but lithium does not plate back the way it left: it redeposits as a mossy, dendritic layer. In 2004 that limited such cells to about 100 to 150 cycles, against the 300 a commercial cell must reach, and raised the risk of a short circuit.

Each fresh, high-surface deposit also builds new SEI and loses more lithium. Tarascon and Armand show the dendrites growing in situ in the electron microscope. The SEI that forms on lithium lets Li+ through, yet it does not make the lithium plate back evenly.

The routes tried since include special electrolytes, such as dioxolane with LiAsF6, protective coatings on the lithium, glassy thin-film electrolytes that have reached 50 000 cycles, and cells assembled with no lithium metal at all. A solid separator that blocks the dendrites is the route behind the solid-state cells of module 7. Lithium metal stays indispensable in the laboratory, where half-cells against it are the standard way to test a new positive electrode.

Worked example. Why early lithium-metal cells carried far more lithium than they used. Brandt gives the usual definition of the lithium cycling efficiency E from the charge stripped each cycle and the excess of lithium used up; the relative excess needed for N cycles is R = N(1 − E). LiClO4 in propylene carbonate, E ≈ 60 %: for just 100 cycles, R = 100 × 0.40 = an excess of 40 times the lithium cycled Moli Energy, late 1980s, E > 99 %: about 300 cycles, but only by carrying three times the lithium: R = 300 × 0.01 = an excess of 3 times so the 3861 mAh/g of lithium metal becomes 3861 ÷ (1 + 3) ≈ 965 mAh/g for a rechargeable negative electrode Still well above graphite’s 372 mAh/g (module 4).

Lithium metal stores the most, but it plates back unevenly, and that, not the chemistry of storage, is what limits it.

The negative electrodes that store more than graphite pay in volume. Lithium alloys, at 0.2 to 0.8 V vs Li/Li+, can swell by up to 200 %, and silicon by about 300 %. Swelling and shrinking on every cycle, the particles crack, lose electrical contact and stop working, unless a buffer matrix around them takes up the strain and keeps the electrical pathway.

Worked example. What a 300 % volume change means for the size of a particle. volume after = 1 + 3 = 4 times the volume before diameter after = 41/3 = 1.59 times the diameter before a 100 nm particle grows to about 160 nm, and back, on every cycle The coating around it, the binder and the SEI on its surface all have to follow that.

A third family abandons the host altogether. In a conversion electrode a transition-metal oxide reacts with lithium as lithium goes in and turns into metal nanoparticles embedded in Li2O; as lithium comes out, the oxide forms again. Such nano-sized oxides store two to three times the capacity of carbon. The price is in efficiency: the oxide reforms only partly, much lithium goes into the SEI, the first cycle loses about 30 %, and charge and discharge are separated by almost a volt, which wastes energy.

Transition-metal fluorides work the same way as positive electrodes, with theoretical capacities of 500 to 1500 mAh/g. Sulfur, at 2.4 V, also works by conversion rather than insertion.

Put together, the materials form a small family tree: lithium metal; the insertion hosts of today’s cells; the alloys; the conversion electrodes; and organic electrodes, in which the redox centre is part of a molecule. In Tarascon and Armand’s 2001 review the organic examples were disulfides, whose S–S bonds break into thiolates and reform, at up to 3 V, held back by their solubility and by self-discharge. In 2013 Goodenough and Park ended their perspective by calling for organic electrodes with multiple-electron redox centres.

Organic electrodes have since become a field of their own. Their redox centres sit in molecules or polymers, and they come in three kinds: n-type, reduced to a negative state whose charge a cation such as Li+ balances; p-type, oxidized to a positive state balanced by an anion of the electrolyte; and bipolar, which can go either way.

Unlike inorganic insertion hosts, they are not limited by the size of the ion, so one material can serve in lithium, sodium, potassium or magnesium cells. They are softer and swell less than crystalline hosts, can be made from abundant, even bio-sourced, elements, and their chemistry can be tuned; the weak points of some of them are a low working voltage, possible dissolution in the electrolyte, low volumetric capacity and poor electronic conductivity. One benchmark, lithium terephthalate, works as a negative electrode at 0.8 V vs Li/Li+ with 300 mAh/g.

A sodium-ion cell would rock sodium ions instead of lithium ions, on the principle of module 6. But Na+ is larger than Li+ and needs a larger interstitial space than many of the hosts that serve lithium offer, so sodium-ion cells need hosts of their own.

The older sodium cells used molten electrodes instead: the sodium-sulfur battery, invented at Ford in 1967 after Kummer and Weber found fast Na+ conduction in a ceramic above 300 °C, runs at 300 to 350 °C, and the ZEBRA cell is a variant with NaCl and iron. Sodium-ion is the first of the “beyond lithium” chemistries to become commercially viable, so far mainly for stationary storage, where energy density matters less.

Module 10

Why batteries age

Where did the capacity go?

Where we are. Module 9 showed materials that fail fast and loudly. A well-made lithium-ion cell fails slowly and quietly: a little capacity gone each week, whether it is used or not. This module names the thieves and shows how each one works.

Capacity is lost in two ways. Some is only hidden: at a high current the cell reaches its cut-off early, and the capacity comes back at a gentler rate. The rest is gone for good, to changes in the volume of the electrodes, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes; the cycle life ends when the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value. For lithium-ion cells Tarascon and Armand put it plainly: poor lifetimes are rooted mainly in side reactions at the electrode-electrolyte interface.

Sorted by what they take, these causes are the three thieves of figure 10.1. Side reactions lock up lithium that could have carried charge. Cracking, contact loss and dissolution remove active material, the room the lithium needs. And thicker interfaces raise the resistance, so that at a given current the cell runs out of voltage before it runs out of charge.

A battery rarely dies of one cause; diagnosing it means separating the causes.

The SEI of module 6 does not stop at the first charge. It keeps growing, more slowly, adding to the interfacial impedance as it evolves. Von Kolzenberg and co-workers show that its thickness follows a power law in time, L ∝ tb, and so does the capacity lost to it: b = 1/2 when the supply through the layer is limited by diffusion, the usual assumption; b = 1 when the reaction itself, or migration during charging, is the bottleneck; b = 0 for migration during discharge. It grows fastest when the cell sits at a high state of charge and is charged fast. Too thick a layer means lost capacity and higher impedance at once.

How much does it matter? In storage, most of the capacity a lithium-ion cell loses goes into the SEI, and in a well-engineered cell more than half of all capacity loss can be traced to its growth. The layer grows while the graphite is being filled, not while it is being emptied, and in a charged cell it keeps growing at rest, more slowly; in the standard models only a fully discharged cell stops feeding it. On silicon the picture changes: the large volume change keeps cracking the SEI, and the growth becomes linear in time.

Worked example. The square-root law, with an illustrative start: suppose the SEI costs 2 % of the capacity in the first month. after 4 months: 2 % × √4 = 4 % after 1 year: 2 % × √12 = 6.9 %; after 4 years: 2 % × √48 = 13.9 % The loss slows down, but it never stops. With b = 1 the same start would have cost 24 % in a year.

The thieves can work together. In a cell with a spinel LiMn2O4 positive electrode, manganese dissolves as Mn(II), crosses the electrolyte and poisons the SEI on the negative electrode. The positive electrode loses active material; the negative electrode’s protection weakens, and with it more lithium is lost. Particles that crack and lose contact, as alloys do (figure 9.2), are the other route to losing active material.

Cells age on the shelf too. Self-discharge, the slow loss of charge with nothing connected, differs enormously between chemistries: a nickel-metal hydride cell can lose up to 30 % a month, while a lithium-MnO2 primary cell keeps 90 % after eight years. It rises with temperature. Overcharging or overdischarging a cell is another way to age it fast: both drive irreversible reactions that form new compounds and cost capacity.

Go deeper: paying back the first-charge loss

The lithium a new cell locks into its SEI on the first charge is gone before the cell is ever used, and in conversion electrodes the first-cycle loss reaches about 30 %. Shanmukaraj and co-workers describe a remedy proposed by Armand: sacrificial salts, lithium salts with an oxidizable anion mixed into the positive electrode. On the first charge the anion is oxidized and gives up its lithium, which pays for the SEI; what is left of the salt leaves pores behind that help the electrolyte wet the electrode. The salt is chosen by its oxidation potential to suit the cell, and the idea extends to sodium and potassium cells.

Module 11

Testing and diagnosis

How would you find out which thief it was?

Where we are. Module 10 named the ways a cell loses capacity. From the outside they all look alike: the cell holds less. This module is about the measurements that tell them apart, which ones leave the cell intact, and what each one actually answers.

Every measurement answers one question, so the question comes first. Cycling at constant current gives the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the state of health; switching the current off and timing the recovery separates the three polarizations (figure 5.4). Impedance spectroscopy separates resistances by their speed; the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique, GITT, measures how fast lithium diffuses inside the electrode material. Measuring the open-circuit voltage at several temperatures gives the thermodynamics of the reaction itself.

Every technique answers one question; choosing the question comes first.

Impedance spectroscopy applies a small alternating voltage and records the current at many frequencies, from fast to slow. Each process answers at its own speed, the same three clocks as in figure 5.4, read with an alternating current instead of a switch. The ohmic parts are independent of frequency; charge transfer with its double-layer capacitance draws a semicircle; diffusion draws a 45° line, the Warburg impedance. Real cells show capacitances of farads and resistances of milliohms (the figure uses small laboratory values). The top of the semicircle lies at the angular frequency ω = 1/(RctCd), and its diameter is the charge-transfer resistance.

That is why impedance is used to follow ageing: a semicircle that grows from one check-up to the next is an interface getting thicker. Recording how a cell’s resistance evolves during operation is one of the ways its state of health is tracked, and the state of charge is estimated from a combination of impedance and resistance measurements, current pulses, counting the charge in and out, and the open-circuit voltage.

Worked example. Where is the top of the semicircle for Rct = 20 Ω and Cd = 20 μF? ω = 1/(RctCd) = 1/(20 Ω × 20 × 10−6 F) = 2500 rad/s f = ω/2π = 398 Hz; Rct = 40 Ω gives 199 Hz Doubling Rct doubles the diameter of the semicircle and halves the frequency at its top.

GITT turns the voltage curve into a diffusion measurement. A short current pulse changes the lithium content of the electrode by a known amount; a rest lets it settle. The settled change, ΔEs, is a step along the open-circuit curve; the change during the pulse, ΔEt, without the IR drop, reflects the lithium content at the particle surface running ahead of the interior, because diffusion cannot keep up. Weppner and Huggins turned the two into the chemical diffusion coefficient, valid as long as the pulse is short compared with L2/D, the time lithium needs to diffuse across the sample (for a porous electrode, S is the contact area between active material and electrolyte).

Charge-discharge and impedance measurements are nondestructive; the full picture often needs a tear-down, opening the cell and examining its parts with Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy, NMR, electron microscopy and X-ray absorption, while accelerating-rate calorimetry finds the conditions for thermal runaway. Plastic lithium-ion cells opened the way to watching the electrodes while they work, with X-ray absorption, NMR, Mössbauer spectroscopy and the electron microscope. Sensing is moving towards the cell itself: an external pressure sensor can already follow the SEI growing, through the irreversible swelling it causes, and sensors inside the cell would add where it happens.

The cell itself has to be chosen too. The standard way to test a new positive electrode is a half-cell against lithium metal, but Murray, Hall and Dahn show that half-cells can fail to predict how the same electrode behaves in a full cell, and that small assembly errors, such as a misaligned stack in a coin cell, change the result.

Telling the two electrodes apart inside one cell needs a stable reference electrode placed where it does not disturb the current, which is still hard to build.

Module 12

Safety, use and end of life

How do you keep a cell safe, and what happens to it at the end?

Where we are. Module 11 showed how to find out what is wrong with a cell. This last module is about the two ends of its working life that matter most to the person holding it: keeping the stored energy from coming out all at once, and what becomes of the materials when the cell is finished.

A charged cell holds its energy in two materials kept apart. Let them meet through a short circuit and the whole stored energy comes out as heat. Thermal runaway is the worst case: the reaction of an electrode with the electrolyte becomes self-sustaining and enters an autocatalytic mode, the heat it releases speeding it up. Because the heat is released inside, at the electrode surfaces, cells for high currents must be built to shed it. The SEI plays a part here too: its decomposition promotes thermal runaway.

The history is written in fires. Exxon’s rechargeable lithium-metal cell of the 1970s grew dendrites across the electrolyte to the positive electrode; the internal short ignited the flammable electrolyte and the programme was abandoned. In 1989 the first lithium-metal rechargeable cell sold for consumer use, Moli Energy’s Li/MoS2, was recalled after cells vented with flame in the field: the porous, high-surface lithium deposited by cycling reacts with the electrolyte strongly enough to do that, and cycling makes cells less tolerant of abuse.

A cell is safe as long as its heat can leave faster than its reactions make it.

Electronics and separators

Lithium cells have no built-in chemical way of shedding overcharge, unlike nickel-cadmium, and in a battery of cells in series small differences in capacity and self-discharge push individual cells out of balance. So the end of charge and of discharge is controlled by watching the voltage, cell by cell; Sony’s first lithium-ion batteries carried a circuit that did exactly that and also limited the current.

Inside the cell, a separator that loses its porosity when it gets hot stops the ions in a fault that heats the cell evenly, such as a short outside it, but helps much less against a local internal short. A ceramic Al2O3 coating on the separator helps keep dendrites from getting through.

The electrolyte as a safeguard

The electrolyte can be a safeguard too. An overcharged cell loses too much lithium from its positive electrode, whose crystal starts to release oxygen and becomes unstable, while lithium metal deposits on the negative electrode; the electrodes then decompose the solvents, and a sudden exothermic reaction follows. Additives in small quantities answer this in three main ways: redox shuttles consume the excess current through a redox process and keep the voltage from rising; aromatic compounds such as biphenyl are believed to polymerize above the maximum operating voltage into an insulating layer on the positive electrode, raising the cell’s resistance; and compounds such as cyclohexylbenzene decompose and give off hydrogen, so that a pressure-operated current interrupter cuts the charging current. None is free: additives always have side effects, and with biphenyl, cycle life at 40 °C up to 4.3 V got worse the more of it there was.

A nonflammable liquid is a further aim: phosphate esters such as triethyl phosphate have very high flash points, but trimethyl phosphate is unstable against graphite, which led some to judge such electrolytes impractical.

Safety built into the chemistry

The lithium-metal polymer cells that Bolloré has used in electric cars and buses since 2011 are a case of safety built into the chemistry: solid polymer electrolytes, more than 3000 cycles without dendrites.

Consumer cells are designed to work between about 0 and 40 °C and to be stored between −20 and 85 °C; military and automotive cells must work from −50 to 85 °C. Within those limits the sources point the same way. Self-discharge rises with temperature. The SEI grows fastest when the cell sits at a high state of charge and is charged fast. And at high current and low temperature, lithium can plate on the graphite instead of entering it.

What that means in your pocket. Read together, those statements favour keeping a cell cool, not leaving it full for long periods, and not fast-charging it in the cold. This is the page’s reading of the sources quoted, not a separate rule from any of them.

Recycling a lithium-ion battery means recovering above all its cobalt, lithium and nickel, besides aluminium and copper. Two chemical routes dominate: pyrometallurgy, which smelts the cells at about 1000 °C into a copper-nickel-cobalt alloy while lithium and manganese go to the slag, and hydrometallurgy, which dissolves the electrode materials in acid and separates the metals, with higher recovery and lower energy use but more complex processing. Direct recycling, which keeps the electrode materials whole, is still immature.

Why it matters: cobalt is scarce, costly and toxic, which is one reason the positive electrode has moved from LiCoO2 toward materials with less of it. Lead-acid batteries show what a closed loop can look like: Winter and Brodd reported in 2004 that about 98 % of them were recycled in the United States.

For lithium batteries, a 2020 European proposal would raise the collection target for portable batteries from 45 % to 65 % by 2025 and 70 % by 2030, and require all batteries from electric vehicles and stationary storage to be collected and recycled.

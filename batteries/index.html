Module 1 of 12

What a battery does

Why does the lamp light when you close the switch, and why not when it is open?

Close the switch. Zinc atoms at the left electrode give up two electrons each and dissolve into the liquid as zinc ions; at the right electrode copper ions take two electrons each and settle as copper metal. The electrons cannot cross the liquid, so they take the wire, and the lamp lights on the way.1,2 Inside the cell the current is carried by ions: cations drift toward the copper side and anions toward the zinc side, so no charge piles up anywhere.1,3

Open the switch and everything stops, but nothing is lost. The push is still there: it is a property of the two materials and the electrolyte between them. The voltage you measure with nothing connected is called the open-circuit voltage,1 and for this pair of electrodes it is 1.10 V.2 The wire only lets the push act.

That is the whole trick, in Goodenough and Park’s words: the electrolyte “conducts the ionic component of the chemical reaction between the anode and the cathode, but it forces the electronic component to traverse an external circuit where it does work”.3

A battery is a chemical reaction that is only allowed to happen through a wire.

A battery keeps its reacting materials inside, so it is a closed system: storage and conversion happen in the same box. A fuel cell is an open system, fed with fuel and oxidant from outside, so its energy is stored in a tank and converted in the cell. A supercapacitor stores charge in the electrical double layers at its electrode surfaces, without a chemical reaction.1

One thing they share sets them apart from engines: none of them turns heat into work, so none is bound by the Carnot limit that caps every heat engine.1

Strictly, one of these units is a cell, and several connected together are a battery, although the two words are used loosely.1 Connecting cells in series adds their voltages; connecting them in parallel adds their current and their run time.3 Everything in this story happens inside a single cell.

You can now explain why a battery has a voltage even when nothing is connected, why the wire matters, and what separates a battery from a fuel cell and a supercapacitor.

So what is inside the box that makes the push?

Go deeper: primary and secondary cells

A primary battery is assembled in the charged state and used until exhausted. A secondary, or rechargeable, battery can be restored to its charged condition by a current flowing in the opposite direction to the discharge current; since secondary batteries are usually assembled in the discharged state, they must be charged before their first use.1

Which reactions can be run backwards, and which cannot, is the subject of module 6.

Module 2 of 12

Anatomy of a cell

If you cut a cell open, what would you find, and what does each part do?

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide.1 The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.1 The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching.1 If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.1

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words. In battery usage the anode is the negative electrode, where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, and the cathode is the positive electrode, where reduction takes them back.1 Because the roles reverse when the cell is charged, this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and lithium cobalt oxide on aluminium foil, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell.3 Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal.3

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal. So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals.3 A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.1,4

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.1 A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; module 8 returns to what happens where one of them does not.

The same four parts, in different materials, give very different cells. The table behind this chart lists the systems on the market in 2004: zinc and manganese dioxide in the 1.5 V cells of a torch, lead and lead oxide in the 2.0 V cells of a car battery, and graphite with lithium cobalt oxide in the 4.0 V cells of a phone.1 Where the voltage comes from is the next question.

You can now explain the four parts of a cell and their jobs, why the separator must never let the electrodes touch, and why cells are built from thin, wide layers.

Two of those parts decide how hard the push is.

Go deeper: half cells and full cells in the laboratory

New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell”, a coin cell with a piece of lithium foil as the negative electrode; lithium is a convenient reference, but a half cell can fail to predict how the material behaves against a graphite negative electrode in a full cell, which is why full coin cells are also built.5,3

Module 3 of 12

Where the voltage comes from

Why does a lithium-ion cell give over 3.6 V and a torch battery 1.5 V?

The open-circuit voltage of a cell is the difference between the electrochemical potentials of its two electrodes.3 Picture each material as a rung on a ladder: the voltage is the gap between the two rungs you choose. Battery scientists measure the rungs against lithium metal, which is why every value here reads “versus Li/Li⁺”.

Lithium sits at the bottom. In Tarascon and Armand’s words it is “the most electropositive” metal, meaning the one with the most negative standard potential, −3.04 V against the standard hydrogen electrode, and it is also the lightest metal, at 6.94 g/mol and 0.53 g/cm³.4 Any positive electrode paired with lithium, or with a host that holds lithium near its own potential, gets the largest possible gap.

Think of two reservoirs at different heights: the pressure at the pipe between them depends on the height difference, not on how wide the reservoirs are. The picture breaks as soon as water flows: the height stays, but the flow costs pressure. Module 5 is about that cost.

Voltage is a property of the pair of materials, not of the size of the cell.

The gap has a ceiling. An electrolyte is stable only between two energy levels: the energy of its lowest unoccupied orbital and of its highest occupied one. A negative electrode whose electrons sit above the first will reduce the electrolyte, and a positive electrode whose empty states sit below the second will oxidize it, unless a passivating layer forms at the surface and blocks the reaction.3

Water is thermodynamically stable over only 1.23 V, and thanks to slow kinetics about 2 V in practice,1 which is why cells with an aqueous electrolyte and a long shelf life stop near 1.5 V.3 The organic carbonate electrolytes of lithium-ion cells reach about 4.3 V versus lithium before their filled orbitals are attacked,3 and about 4.6 V in practice.1 That is the difference between the torch battery and the phone battery: not a stronger reaction, but an electrolyte that tolerates a wider gap.

Graphite sits about 0.2 V above lithium, below the carbonate electrolyte’s limit, so a layer must form on it during the first charge; lithium titanate at 1.5 V sits inside the window and needs none.3 Module 6 follows that layer as it forms.

Why do oxide positive electrodes reach 4 V when the sulfide of the first rechargeable lithium cell reached only 2.2 V? The redox couple of the host cannot be pushed below the top of the host’s anion band. In the layered sulfides that top sits about 2.5 eV below the lithium level; in the layered oxides it sits about 4.0 eV below, which is why oxide hosts are used in today’s lithium-ion cells.3 Polyanions such as phosphate pull the band lower still through what chemists call the inductive effect, which is how LiFePO₄ reaches 3.5 V.3,4

In the language of thermodynamics the same gap reads ΔG = −nFE: the electrical energy available from a reaction is the number of electrons it moves per formula unit, times the Faraday constant, times the cell voltage. So the voltage is the reaction’s free energy per unit of charge, and nF, the total charge available, is its capacity factor.1 The Faraday constant is the charge carried by one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 coulombs.1 Module 4 turns that constant into the capacity of your cell.

You can now explain why lithium cells have the highest voltages, why oxide hosts beat sulfides, and what sets the ceiling.

You have chosen a voltage. How much charge can your cell hold?

Go deeper: reading “versus Li/Li⁺”

A potential is always a difference, so every value needs a reference. Textbook tables use the standard hydrogen electrode, against which lithium sits at −3.04 V;4 battery papers use lithium metal itself as the zero, which is why the same LiCoO₂ electrode reads about 4.0 V “versus Li/Li⁺” and about 1 V versus hydrogen.3,4 In a lithium-ion cell the negative electrode is not lithium metal but a host that holds lithium near lithium’s potential, so the cell voltage is a little less than the positive electrode’s ladder value: graphite costs about 0.2 V.3

Module 4 of 12

Capacity, energy and power

How long would your cell run a lamp?

Capacity is a count of electrons. Faraday’s law ties the amount of material transformed to the charge that flows, mass = I·t·M/(nF), and it has “no known exceptions”.1 Turn it around: a gram of a material of molar mass M that gives up n electrons per formula unit holds nF/M coulombs, which the battery world writes in milliampere-hours per gram, Q = nF/(3.6 M). The Faraday constant is the charge of one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C or 26.8015 Ah.1

Two conventions hide in that formula. Graphite’s 372 mAh/g refers to the mass of carbon, the host as assembled, not to the mass of LiC₆: Tarascon and Armand give 372 mAh/g for the end compound LiC₆ and about 350 mAh/g in practice.4 Positive-electrode materials are referred to their lithiated mass: LiFePO₄ holds 170 mAh/g in theory3 and is used at about 90 % of it,4 while LiCoO₂ is limited to about half of its lithium for safety, 140 mAh/g in practice.4

Light elements and many electrons per formula unit give high capacity; high voltage turns capacity into energy.

Energy is the integral of the voltage over the charge delivered: on a discharge curve, the area under the line, which is also the average voltage times the capacity.3 Per kilogram it is called specific energy (Wh/kg), per litre energy density (Wh/L); how fast it can be delivered is specific power (W/kg) and power density (W/L).1

The capacity itself depends on how hard you pull. At high current the transfer of ions across the interfaces becomes diffusion-limited and the cell reaches less of its charge before the cut-off; that part of the loss is reversible and comes back at a gentler rate.3 Module 5 takes the curve apart.

To compare the two abilities, energy and power, engineers use the Ragone plot. On it, supercapacitors are high-power systems, fuel cells high-energy systems, and batteries sit between them, overlapping both; no single electrochemical system matches the combustion engine on both axes at once, which is why the review suggests combining them.1

As a rule of thumb, a rechargeable battery delivers about 25 % of its theoretical energy, and a primary battery more than 50 %. The difference has three causes: the inert parts a cell needs, such as conductive diluents, current collectors and containers; the internal resistances of electrodes, electrolyte and contacts; and incomplete use of the active masses.1

To give the number a feel, Winter and Brodd note that a specific energy of 30 Wh/kg means one kilogram of lead-acid battery can power a 60 W lamp for only half an hour.1 The first commercial lithium-ion cell of 1991 gave over 3.6 V and 120 to 150 Wh/kg, two to three times the usual nickel-cadmium cell of the day.4

You can now explain where a capacity in mAh/g comes from, why energy is an area, what the Ragone plot shows, and why practice gives a quarter of theory.

Now let us watch your cell do it.

Go deeper: the mass basis, and 372 against 339

Run Faraday’s law on the lithiated compound LiC₆ (79.0 g/mol) and you get 339 mAh/g; run it on the carbon host C₆ (72.07 g/mol) and you get 372 mAh/g. Both are correct; they answer different questions. The convention in the field is to refer negative-electrode hosts to their delithiated mass and positive-electrode materials to their lithiated mass, which is how each is weighed when the cell is built.4,3 Whenever you compare capacities, check the basis first.

Module 5 of 12

Charging and discharging

Why does the voltage sag when you draw current, and where does the lost energy go?

A cell has an internal resistance, and the current has to push through it. On discharge the terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization η; on charge it is the open-circuit voltage plus η, because the charger must overcome the same resistance in the other direction.3 So the charge curve always lies above the discharge curve, and the gap between them is paid twice per cycle.

The polarization has three parts. Activation polarization comes from the kinetics of the charge-transfer reactions at the two electrode-electrolyte interfaces; ohmic polarization from the resistance of the cell’s components and their contacts; concentration polarization from the limits of mass transport during operation.1 All three grow with current, and the third grows fastest near the end of a discharge, which is why a hard-driven cell gives up early.3

The gap between charge and discharge is energy lost as heat; the shrinking width over cycles is ageing.

The three parts can be told apart by their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears instantaneously, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² s. Concentration polarization is slow, taking 10⁻² s or longer to appear and to fade.1 Interrupt the current and watch the voltage recover: the step is ohmic, the fast curve is activation, the slow drift is concentration.1 Module 11 uses the same clocks with an alternating current.

Why are some curves flat and others sloping? Winter and Brodd answer with the Gibbs phase rule. When the reaction converts one solid phase into another, the number of phases equals the number of components, and once pressure and temperature are fixed no degree of freedom is left: the voltage must stay constant, and the curve is a plateau. When lithium dissolves into a single host phase, one degree of freedom remains and the voltage can slope.1 LiFePO₄ is the two-phase case with its flat 3.5 V plateau;3 the spinel LiMn₂O₄ shows steps near 4 V and a drop to 3 V as the lithium changes sites.3

Which is better depends on the use: electronics prefer a flat, unchanging voltage, while a sloping curve makes it easy to read how much charge is left.1

Two numbers summarize a cycle. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial reversible value.3 A commercial rechargeable cell must survive at least 300 full cycles while losing under 20 % of its capacity, and most need three to eight hours to charge; fast charging stresses the electrodes and shortens cycle life.1

Some capacity loss is only borrowed: what a high rate hides comes back at a low rate. What does not come back, the irreversible loss, comes from electrode volume changes, reactions between electrode and electrolyte, and decomposition of the electrodes.3 Module 10 names the thieves.

The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage: if dE/dT is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as in nickel-cadmium; lead-acid has a negative dE/dT and does the opposite. An irreversible part, Joule heat, comes from the polarization and grows with current; in operation it usually dwarfs the entropic part. The heat is released inside the cell at the electrode surfaces, so high-rate cells must be built to shed it.1

You can now explain why the charge curve sits above the discharge curve, what the three polarizations are and how fast each relaxes, why some curves are flat, and why 99.9 % per cycle is not enough.

Go deeper: the shape of the polarization curve

Near equilibrium the current is proportional to the overpotential, and the ratio defines the charge-transfer resistance, R_ct = RT/(nF i₀), where i₀ is the exchange current density: a reaction with a large exchange current is more reversible and polarizes less for a given current. Battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10⁻² A/cm² at room temperature are favoured.2,1 At large overpotentials one branch of the Butler-Volmer equation dominates and the overpotential grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation, which holds once the back reaction contributes under 1 % of the current, beyond about 118 mV at 25 °C.2

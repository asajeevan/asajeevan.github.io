Module 0 · the foundation

Charge, field, potential, voltage

A torch cell says 1.5 V on its side. What is a volt, and what exactly is being pushed?

Where we are. Before the first cell, four words that every later screen uses: charge, the thing that pushes and is pushed; field, the push per unit charge at a place; potential, the energy per unit charge at a place; voltage, a difference of potential. Each gets one figure, one equation and one number.

Everything on this page moves because charges push on one another. Charge comes in two signs, and the names are a convention older than the electron: Benjamin Franklin called an excess of what we now call electronic charge negative and a deficiency of it positive.1 Between two charges q and q′ a distance r apart there is a force F = qq′/(4πε₀r²), Coulomb’s law, where ε₀ is a measured constant, the permittivity of free space.1 Read the formula: the force grows with either charge, falls with the square of the distance, and its sign follows the product of the charges, so like charges push apart and unlike charges pull together.

The unit of charge is the coulomb, and one electron carries 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C of it. Put two electrons one nanometre apart, about the width of three water molecules, and the formula gives 0.23 nN pushing them apart: tiny in newtons, enormous for something that weighs 10⁻³⁰ kg.

Charges push on each other with a force that falls as the square of the distance.

Take the second charge away and ask what is left at its place. The answer is the electric field: the force that would act on a unit charge put there, a vector with a direction and a strength at every point in space.1 Around a positive charge it points outward and falls as 1/r², because that is how the force falls.

Now bring a unit positive charge in from very far away, pushing against that field all the way. The work you do is the electric potential φ at the point you reach, and it does not depend on the path you took: φ = −∫E·dl.1 Only differences of φ between two points can be measured; the value at one point is a bookkeeping choice, and the usual choice sets φ = 0 infinitely far away.1 For a single elementary charge the integral gives φ = q/(4πε₀r), which is 1.44 V one nanometre out and half that at two.

The two pictures are one: the field is the slope of the potential. Where φ falls steeply the push is strong; where φ is flat there is no push at all. That last case is about to matter.

Field is force per unit charge; potential is energy per unit charge; the field is the slope of the potential.

A voltage is a difference of potential between two points, and its unit, the volt, is one joule per coulomb: the energy available to move each coulomb of charge from one point to the other.1 Move a charge q across a potential difference Δφ and its energy changes by qΔφ.1 For one electron across one volt that change is one electron-volt, and per mole of electrons it is 96.5 kJ, comparable to the energies of chemical bonds and reactions; which is why a few volts are enough to drive chemistry.1

Metals are conductors: they contain charges that are free to move. Leave a metal alone and those charges settle until there is no field left inside it, because any field would move them further; so a conductor at rest sits at a single potential throughout, and any excess charge it carries lies on its surface.1 The plates in the figure are two such flat regions of the potential; between them, where there is no conductor, the potential falls in a straight line and the field is simply V divided by the gap.1

A picture to keep: potential is height, field is slope, and a positive charge rolls downhill while a negative one is pushed uphill. The picture breaks in one place: charges also push on each other and rearrange, so the hill is not fixed scenery. Module 3 is about how a cell builds its own hill.

Worked example. How much charge does it take to raise a metal by one volt? Bard, Faulkner and White give the answer for a mercury drop of 0.5 mm radius in vacuum: about 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ C per volt.1 electrons per volt = 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ C ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 3 × 10⁵ Three hundred thousand electrons, out of the 10²⁰ or so in the drop, shift its potential by a full volt. Potentials are set by minute charge imbalances, which is why they are so easy to change and so hard to see.

A volt is a joule per coulomb; a conductor at rest is all at one potential.

A cell keeps its two terminals at a fixed potential difference; module 1 shows how. Join them with a wire and electrons flow through the wire from the more negative terminal to the more positive one.1 The amount of charge passing a point each second is the current, i = dQ/dt, measured in amperes: one ampere is one coulomb per second.1

A wire carrying current is no longer at one potential. Between any two points on it there must be a potential difference to drive the current through the resistance between them.1 That is Ohm’s law, E = IR: the same current through twice the resistance needs twice the voltage.2 The resistance of a piece of conductor grows with its length and shrinks with its cross-section, R = ρl/A, where ρ is the resistivity of the material.1 Metals have a resistance too, but it is so much smaller than that of an electrolyte that on the scale of a cell the drop along the copper is invisible.1 In the figure, nearly the whole voltage is spent across the lamp.

Energy per coulomb times coulombs per second is energy per second: the power delivered is P = IV, in watts.3

Worked example. A 1.5 V cell drives a lamp of 3 Ω. I = V / R = 1.5 V / 3 Ω = 0.5 A, that is 0.5 C every second electrons per second = 0.5 C ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 3.1 × 10¹⁸ P = I V = 0.5 A × 1.5 V = 0.75 W Each electron gives up 1.5 eV on its way round; three billion billion of them a second add up to three quarters of a watt of light and heat.

Current needs a potential difference to drive it through resistance; the power is current times voltage.

In a liquid the mobile charges are not electrons but ions, atoms or molecules that have lost or gained electrons; free electrons in a solution are so short-lived that they carry no current worth counting.1 Put an ion of charge number z in a field E and it feels the force |z|eE. As it speeds up the liquid drags on it, with a drag that grows with speed; when the drag balances the push the ion stops accelerating and drifts at a steady terminal velocity.1 The velocity per unit field is the ion’s mobility, u = |z|e/(6πηr): a smaller ion in a thinner liquid moves faster.1

When current flows through a liquid the potential slopes across it, exactly as it did along the wire, and the slope is the field: cations are driven down the slope and anions up it.1 Both carry current, and the liquid’s conductivity adds up every kind of ion, its charge, its mobility and its concentration: κ = FΣ|z|uC.1 One more rule holds everywhere in the liquid: on any scale you can see, positive and negative charge balance. An imbalance cannot build up, because it would create an enormous field that would move ions until it was gone.1

The constant F in that formula is the Faraday constant, the charge carried by one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C.1,2 It is the bridge between the chemist’s moles and the physicist’s coulombs, and it returns in module 4 to count how much charge a gram of material can hold.

You can now explain what a volt is, why a field makes charges move and which way, why a current needs a potential difference to drive it, and why an ion in a liquid drifts at a steady speed rather than accelerating.

Now put two different metals into a liquid and see where a potential difference comes from.

Go deeper: Gauss’s law, and why the charge sits on the surface

Draw an imaginary closed surface anywhere. Gauss’s law says that the net charge inside it equals ε₀ times the integral of the field over that surface, q = ε₀∮E·dS.1 Inside a conductor at rest the field is zero everywhere, so the integral over any surface drawn inside the conductor is zero, so the charge inside is zero: whatever excess charge the conductor carries must lie on its boundary.1 In an electrolyte the same argument leaves the charge at the boundary too, but thermal motion smears it into a layer that can be tenths of a nanometre to thousands of nanometres thick, the space charge region; in a metal that layer is negligibly thin.1 Module 3 uses this to build the double layer at an electrode.

Bring an electrolyte next to the metal and the coulombic field of the metal’s surface charge is largely cancelled by the polarization of the liquid: the same mercury drop then needs about 10⁻⁶ C, some 6 × 10¹² electrons, for a one-volt change, more than 10⁷ times what it needed in vacuum.1

Module 1 of 12

What a battery does

Why does the lamp light when you close the switch, and why not when it is open?

Where we are. Module 0 gave you the volt (a joule per coulomb), the field (the push per unit charge), the conductor at rest (one potential throughout) and the two kinds of carrier: electrons in metals, ions in liquids. Now two different metals go into a liquid, and a potential difference appears between them.

Close the switch. Zinc atoms at the left electrode give up two electrons each and dissolve into the liquid as Zn²⁺ ions; at the right electrode Cu²⁺ ions take two electrons each and settle as copper metal.2,1 Each of those is a half-reaction, and neither can happen alone: each must be coupled, through a cell, to a second half-reaction running the other way.1 The electrons cannot cross the liquid, so they take the wire, from the more negative electrode to the more positive, and the lamp lights on the way.1 Inside the cell the current is carried by ions: cations drift toward the copper and sulfate toward the zinc, so no charge piles up anywhere.2,1

Open the switch and everything stops, but nothing is lost. The voltage you measure with nothing else connected is the open-circuit voltage,2 and a high-impedance voltmeter can read it without drawing any appreciable current.1 For this pair of metals it is 1.10 V: the difference between the standard potentials of the two half-reactions, +0.340 V for copper and −0.763 V for zinc.1 Where a potential sits inside the cell is the strip under the figure: it jumps at each metal|liquid boundary, in a zone so narrow that a very strong field exists there, and stays flat through the liquid; the voltmeter reads the sum of the jumps along the path from one electrode to the other.1

That is the whole trick, in Goodenough and Park’s words: the electrolyte “conducts the ionic component of the chemical reaction between the anode and the cathode, but it forces the electronic component to traverse an external circuit where it does work”.3

Worked example. Where does 1.10 V come from, and what does it buy? E = E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) − E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = 0.340 V − (−0.763 V) = 1.10 V energy per electron round the circuit = 1.10 eV; two electrons per zinc atom per mole of zinc: 2 × 96 485 C × 1.10 V = 213 kJ Module 3 shows that this 213 kJ is the free energy of the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, and module 4 turns the “per mole” into “per gram”.

A battery is a chemical reaction that is only allowed to happen through a wire.

Why do the electrons choose the wire? Because they have no other road. In a metal, charge is carried by electrons moving through a lattice of fixed ion cores; in an electrolyte it is carried by ions, and the “free” electrons a liquid might briefly hold are so short-lived that they contribute nothing to its conductivity.1 At the boundary between the two, charge can pass only if the two kinds of carrier are linked by an electrode reaction: electrons consumed or produced in the metal, ions produced or consumed in the liquid.1

The field does the rest. One field points from the positive side to the negative side; it pushes a positive ion along itself and an electron against itself, so the cations in the liquid and the electrons in the wire go round the circuit in opposite senses and meet again at the electrodes.1 In the wire, the potential difference that drives the electrons through the lamp is the one you met in figure 0.4; in the liquid, the potential slopes gently and drives the ions.1

Electrons travel in metals, ions travel in liquids, and only a reaction can hand charge from one to the other.

Strictly, one of these units is a cell, and several connected together are a battery, although the two words are used loosely.2 Connecting cells in series adds their voltages; connecting them in parallel adds their current and their run time.3 The strip says why: a potential difference between two points is the sum of the drops and rises along any path between them,1 so three cells in a chain give three rises, while three cells side by side each give the same single rise but share the current.

A battery keeps its reacting materials inside, so it is a closed system: storage and conversion happen in the same box. A fuel cell is an open system, fed with fuel and oxidant from outside, so its energy is stored in a tank and converted in the cell. A supercapacitor stores charge in the electrical double layers at its electrode surfaces, without a chemical reaction.2 None of the three turns heat into work, so none is bound by the Carnot limit that caps every heat engine.2 Everything on this page happens inside a single cell.

You can now explain why a cell has a voltage even when nothing is connected, where inside the cell that voltage sits, why the electrons take the wire and the ions the liquid, and why voltages add in series.

So what is inside the box, part by part, and what does each part do?

Go deeper: primary and secondary cells

A primary battery is assembled in the charged state and used until exhausted. A secondary, or rechargeable, battery can be restored to its charged condition by a current flowing in the opposite direction to the discharge current; since secondary batteries are usually assembled in the discharged state, they must be charged before their first use.2 To charge, a power supply is connected across the cell and its voltage is adjusted; the interfacial potential differences shift, especially at the electrode|electrolyte boundaries, and current can be driven in either direction.1 Which reactions can be run backwards, and which cannot, is the subject of module 6.

Module 2 of 12

Anatomy of a cell

If you cut a cell open, what would you find, and what does each part do?

Where we are. Module 1 showed the four jobs in a cell: give electrons, take electrons, carry ions, keep the two electrodes apart. Now the parts that do those jobs in a real cell, and why they have the shapes they have.

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide.2 The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.2 The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching.2 If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.2

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words. In battery usage the anode is the negative electrode, where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, and the cathode is the positive electrode, where reduction takes them back.2 Because the roles reverse when the cell is charged, this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and the lithium host on aluminium, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell.3 Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal.3 The electrolyte is a liquid, added drop by drop during assembly: in Murray, Hall and Dahn’s research cells, about 38 mg of a 1 mol/L solution of LiPF₆ in a carbonate solvent, enough to soak the separator and wet both electrodes.4 New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell” against a piece of lithium foil; such cells are easy to make but can fail to predict how the material behaves against graphite in a full cell, which is why full coin cells like this one are built.4

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal (module 0 showed why: an ion drags its way through a liquid at a terminal speed set by its mobility). So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals.3 A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.2,5

Worked example. Why area matters: the resistance of a layer is R = ρl/A (module 0).1 Keep the electrolyte layer as thin as the separator allows (small l) and make the electrodes as wide as will fit (large A), and the ionic resistance of the whole cell falls with both. A wound strip is the way to put a large A inside a small can.

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.2 The porous structure extends the surface area where the reaction can happen and lowers the current density, so the cell works more efficiently.2 A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; module 8 returns to what happens where one of them does not.

The same four parts, in different materials, give very different cells. The table behind this chart lists the systems on the market in 2004: zinc and manganese dioxide in the 1.5 V cells of a torch, lead and lead oxide in the 2.0 V cells of a car battery, and graphite with lithium cobalt oxide in the 4.0 V cells of a phone.2 Two patterns are already visible: every system with a water-based electrolyte stays at or below 2 V, and every system above 2 V is built on lithium with a nonaqueous electrolyte.2 Where the voltage comes from, and why water sets a ceiling, is the next question.

You can now explain the parts of a cell and their jobs, why the separator must never let the electrodes touch, why cells are built from thin, wide layers, and why an electrode is a porous coating rather than a block.

Two of those parts decide how hard the push is.

Go deeper: what a research coin cell taught its makers

Murray, Hall and Dahn built full coin cells with graphite and a nickel-rich oxide and found that three “identical” cells kept 95 %, 90 % and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. Taking them apart showed the cause: the positive and negative discs were slightly misaligned, and lithium had plated on the parts of the positive electrode not covered by the negative one. The spring and spacer had been dropped in at an angle from tweezers; placing them from directly above with a vacuum pen, and using a thicker, more compliant separator, brought the cells to the reproducibility of machine-made pouch cells.4 Module 9 explains why plated lithium is lost.

Module 3 of 12

Where the voltage comes from

Why does a lithium-ion cell give over 3.6 V and a torch battery 1.5 V?

Where we are. Module 1 measured 1.10 V between zinc and copper and showed that it sits in two jumps, one at each metal|liquid boundary. Module 2 named the parts. Now the physics of a jump: what a potential means for the electrons in a metal, what happens at the boundary, and why the two electrodes of your cell sit where they do on a ladder.

In module 0 a potential was energy per unit charge. For an electrode that has a sharp meaning: to observe or control the potential of an electrode is to observe or control the energy of its transferable electrons.1 Drive an electrode to a more negative potential and the energy of its electrons is raised; for each volt, each electron gains one electron-volt, 96.5 kJ per mole of electrons, comparable to the energies of chemical bonds.1

The energy of those transferable electrons has a name: the Fermi energy, or Fermi level. In a metal the electron states form a continuum, filled from the bottom up; the Fermi level marks the division between filled and empty, and because empty states lie just above it, an electron can move with almost no activation, which is what makes a metal conduct.1 Raise the Fermi level above an empty orbital of a species in the electrolyte and electrons flow from the electrode into the solution, a reduction current; lower it below a filled orbital and electrons flow from the solution onto the electrode, an oxidation current.1

This is the same fact as the potential ladder further down: a more negative electrode holds its electrons at a higher energy, so it is the stronger reducing agent. Lithium, at the bottom of every ladder, holds them highest of all.

Electrode potential is electron energy in disguise: more negative means higher-energy electrons, one electron-volt per volt.

Now zoom into one of the jumps of figure 1.1. Put a metal into an electrolyte and the two phases exchange a little charge until their electron energies match; equality of the Fermi energies of metal and solution is the condition for equilibrium, and electrons flow from the phase with the higher Fermi energy to the one with the lower until it is reached.1 The amount of charge that moves is tiny, so the composition of the solution does not noticeably change.1

That charge cannot spread through the metal: a conductor at rest keeps its excess charge on its surface (module 0), and on an electrode it lies in a layer thinner than a nanometre.1 The liquid answers with an equal and opposite charge right at the boundary, an excess of cations or of anions.1 The whole array, charged metal surface, oriented solvent molecules, a compact layer of ions one solvent shell out and a diffuse layer beyond, is the electrical double layer.1 Above 0.01 M the diffuse part is less than about 10 nm thick.1

Two sheets of opposite charge a molecular distance apart: the potential steps across them, and the field inside the step can reach 10⁷ V/cm, strong enough to distort the molecules that sit in it.1 The arrangement behaves like a capacitor, q = C·E, with 10 to 40 µF for every square centimetre of electrode, and it forms and relaxes in about 10⁻⁸ s.1,2 A cell’s voltage is the sum of such steps, taken along the path from one electrode to the other.1

A cell’s voltage lives in two double layers, each a few nanometres thick.

Chemists carry the same idea one step further. The energy of a charged species in a phase has two parts: a chemical part, from its surroundings on the scale of a molecule, and an electrical part, its charge times the local potential. Their sum is the electrochemical potential, μ̄ = μ + zFφ, and a species is at equilibrium between two phases when its electrochemical potential is the same in both.1 Apply that to the electrons in the two electrodes of a cell and the open-circuit voltage falls out: it is the difference between the electrochemical potentials of the electrons in the two electrodes, divided by the electron charge, VOC = (μA − μC)/e.3,1

So picture each material as a rung on a ladder of electron energy, drawn as a potential: the cell voltage is the gap between the two rungs you choose. Battery scientists measure the rungs against lithium metal, which is why every value here reads “versus Li/Li⁺”. Lithium sits at the bottom. In Tarascon and Armand’s words it is “the most electropositive” metal, the one with the most negative standard potential, −3.04 V against the standard hydrogen electrode, and it is also the lightest metal, at 6.94 g/mol and 0.53 g/cm³.5

The gap has a ceiling, and the ceiling is the electrolyte. Water is thermodynamically stable over only 1.23 V, between the potential at which it gives off hydrogen and the potential at which it gives off oxygen;1 slow kinetics stretch that to about 2 V in practice,2 which is why cells with an aqueous electrolyte and a long shelf life stop near 1.5 V.3 But where that 1.23 V sits matters as much as its width: at pH 0 it runs from 3.05 to 4.27 V versus lithium, and it slides 59 mV lower for every pH unit.1 Every rung below about 3 V, which is every negative electrode on the ladder and most of the positive ones, is simply out of reach for water. The organic carbonate electrolytes of lithium-ion cells are stable from about 1.1 V to about 4.3 V versus lithium,3 about 4.6 V in practice,2 and there is the answer to the hook: not a stronger reaction, but an electrolyte that tolerates a wider gap, in the right place.

Two reservoirs at different heights: the pressure at the pipe between them depends on the height difference, not on how wide the reservoirs are. The picture breaks as soon as water flows: the height stays, but the flow costs pressure. Module 5 is about that cost.

Voltage is a property of the pair of materials, and the electrolyte sets how far apart they may be.

The ceiling has a mechanism. An electrolyte is a collection of molecules, and each has a highest filled and a lowest empty energy level; the gap between them is the electrolyte window. A negative electrode whose electrons sit above the empty level will reduce the electrolyte, and a positive electrode whose empty states sit below the filled level will oxidize it, unless a passivating layer forms at the surface and blocks the reaction.3 Graphite sits about 0.2 V above lithium, outside the carbonate window, so a layer must form on it during the first charge; lithium titanate at 1.5 V sits inside the window and needs none.3 Module 6 follows that layer as it forms.

Notice the path of the electron in the figure. It gives up its e·VOC only by travelling round the external circuit, from the negative electrode to the positive one; it never crosses the electrolyte, which has no free electrons (module 1). Inside the cell it is our ion that crosses, keeping every region neutral.3,1

Why do oxide positive electrodes reach 4 V when the sulfide of the first rechargeable lithium cell reached only 2.2 V? In a solid the electron levels of the many atoms merge into bands, continuous ranges of energy, filled from the bottom up.1 The redox couple of the host’s transition metal cannot be pushed below the top of the host’s filled anion band. In the layered sulfides that top sits about 2.5 eV below the lithium level; in the layered oxides it sits about 4.0 eV below, which is why oxide hosts are used in today’s lithium-ion cells.3 Polyanions such as phosphate pull the band lower still through what chemists call the inductive effect, which is how LiFePO₄ reaches 3.5 V.3,5

In the language of thermodynamics the same gap reads ΔG = −nFE: the free energy of a reaction, the energy it can deliver as work, equals the charge it moves per mole, nF, times the cell voltage, with the minus sign saying that a spontaneous reaction has a positive voltage.1,2 So the voltage is the reaction’s free energy per unit of charge, and nF, the total charge available, is its capacity factor.2 The Faraday constant is the charge carried by one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 coulombs.2,1

Worked example. The Daniell cell of module 1, Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, moves two electrons per zinc atom at 1.103 V. ΔG = −nFE = −2 × 96 485 C/mol × 1.103 V = −213 kJ/mol per electron: 1.103 eV; per zinc atom: 2.21 eV That 213 kJ per mole of zinc is what the lamp of figure 1.1 can get, at most, from 65 g of zinc; module 4 turns “per mole” into “per gram” and “per hour”.

You can now explain why a more negative electrode is the stronger reducing agent, where a cell’s voltage physically sits, why lithium cells have the highest voltages, why water caps a cell near 1.5 V and where on the lithium scale that cap lies, why oxide hosts beat sulfides, and what ΔG = −nFE means.

You have chosen a voltage. How much charge can your cell hold?

Go deeper: the electrochemical potential, from two metals in contact to the cell voltage

For electrons in a metal the electrochemical potential is μ̄e = μ°e − Fφ, with no activity term because the electron concentration never changes appreciably.1 Touch a copper wire to a zinc electrode and electrons move between them until their electrochemical potentials are equal, which leaves a potential difference φZn − φCu = (μ°eZn − μ°eCu)/F between the two metals: a contact potential, one of the steps in figure 1.1’s profile.1 Write the equilibrium of the whole cell reaction in electrochemical potentials and the terms in φ cancel everywhere except at the two copper leads, giving −nFE = n(μ̄eCu′ − μ̄eCu) and, on expanding the chemical potentials, the Nernst equation.1 Per electron, the same statement is Goodenough and Park’s VOC = (μA − μC)/e.3

A potential is always a difference, so every value needs a reference. Textbook tables use the standard hydrogen electrode, against which lithium sits at −3.045 V;1 battery papers use lithium metal itself as the zero, which is why the same LiCoO₂ electrode reads about 4.0 V “versus Li/Li⁺” and about 1 V versus hydrogen.3,5 In a lithium-ion cell the negative electrode is not lithium metal but a host that holds lithium near lithium’s potential, so the cell voltage is a little less than the positive electrode’s ladder value: graphite costs about 0.2 V.3

Module 4 of 12

Capacity, energy and power

How long would your cell run a lamp?

Where we are. Module 3 gave your cell a voltage: the gap between two rungs, which is the free energy of its reaction per unit of charge. Now the other factor: how much charge the materials hold. Charge times voltage is energy; energy per second is power.

Capacity is a count of electrons. Faraday’s law ties the amount of material transformed to the charge that flows, Q = nFN for N moles of reactant, and it has “no known exceptions”.1,2 Turn it around: a gram of a material of molar mass M that gives up n electrons per formula unit holds nF/M coulombs, which the battery world writes in milliampere-hours per gram, Q = nF/(3.6 M). The Faraday constant is the charge of one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C or 26.8015 Ah.2

Two conventions hide in that formula. Graphite’s 372 mAh/g refers to the mass of carbon, the host as assembled, not to the mass of LiC₆: Tarascon and Armand give 372 mAh/g for the end compound LiC₆ and about 350 mAh/g in practice.5 Positive-electrode materials are referred to their lithiated mass: LiFePO₄ holds 170 mAh/g in theory3 and is used at about 90 % of it,5 while LiCoO₂ is limited to about half of its lithium for safety, 140 mAh/g in practice.5

Worked example. Graphite, one lithium per six carbons, referred to the carbon. M(C₆) = 6 × 12.011 = 72.07 g/mol, n = 1 Q = 1 × 96 485.3 C/mol ÷ (3.6 × 72.07 g/mol) = 372 mAh/g at 3.8 V: 372 mAh/g × 3.8 V = 1414 mWh/g = 1414 Wh/kg of graphite, graphite alone The same calculation on the mass of LiC₆ (79.0 g/mol) gives 339 mAh/g. Both are right; they answer different questions.

Light elements and many electrons per formula unit give high capacity; high voltage turns capacity into energy.

Energy is the integral of the voltage over the charge delivered: on a discharge curve, the area under the line, which is also the average voltage times the capacity.3 Per kilogram it is called specific energy (Wh/kg), per litre energy density (Wh/L); how fast it can be delivered is specific power (W/kg) and power density (W/L).2

The capacity itself depends on how hard you pull. At high current the transfer of ions across the interfaces becomes diffusion-limited and the cell reaches less of its charge before the cut-off; that part of the loss is reversible and comes back at a gentler rate.3 Module 5 takes the curve apart.

Worked example. A cell of capacity 2 Ah discharged at an average of 3.6 V. energy = 3.6 V × 2 Ah = 7.2 Wh a 6 W lamp (P = IV, module 0) runs for 7.2 Wh ÷ 6 W = 1.2 hours, drawing 6 W / 3.6 V = 1.7 A At that current the curve would sag and the capacity would fall short of 2 Ah; the figure shows by how much, schematically.

To compare the two abilities, energy and power, engineers use the Ragone plot. On it, supercapacitors are high-power systems, fuel cells high-energy systems, and batteries sit between them, overlapping both: a battery with thin-film electrodes reaches the power of a supercapacitor.2 No single electrochemical system matches the combustion engine on both axes at once, which is why the review suggests combining them: batteries or supercapacitors for the power, fuel cells for the energy.2

As a rule of thumb, a rechargeable battery delivers about 25 % of its theoretical energy, and a primary battery more than 50 %. The difference has three causes: the inert parts a cell needs, such as conductive diluents, current collectors and containers; the internal resistances of electrodes, electrolyte and contacts; and incomplete use of the active masses.2 Module 2 showed the inert parts; module 0 showed why resistance costs voltage, and therefore energy, whenever current flows.

To give the number a feel, Winter and Brodd note that a specific energy of 30 Wh/kg means one kilogram of lead-acid battery can power a 60 W lamp for only half an hour.2 The first commercial lithium-ion cell of 1991 gave over 3.6 V and 120 to 150 Wh/kg, two to three times the usual nickel-cadmium cell of the day.5

You can now explain where a capacity in mAh/g comes from and which mass it refers to, why energy is an area, what the Ragone plot shows, and why practice gives a quarter of theory.

Go deeper: the mass basis, and 372 against 339

Run Faraday’s law on the lithiated compound LiC₆ (79.0 g/mol) and you get 339 mAh/g; run it on the carbon host C₆ (72.07 g/mol) and you get 372 mAh/g. Both are correct; they answer different questions. The convention in the field is to refer negative-electrode hosts to their delithiated mass and positive-electrode materials to their lithiated mass, which is how each is weighed when the cell is built.5,3 Whenever you compare capacities, check the basis first.

Module 5 of 12

Charging and discharging

Why does the voltage sag when you draw current, and where does the lost energy go?

A cell has an internal resistance, and the current has to push through it. On discharge the terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization η; on charge it is the open-circuit voltage plus η, because the charger must overcome the same resistance in the other direction.3 So the charge curve always lies above the discharge curve, and the gap between them is paid twice per cycle.

The polarization has three parts. Activation polarization comes from the kinetics of the charge-transfer reactions at the two electrode-electrolyte interfaces; ohmic polarization from the resistance of the cell’s components and their contacts; concentration polarization from the limits of mass transport during operation.2 All three grow with current, and the third grows fastest near the end of a discharge, which is why a hard-driven cell gives up early.3

The gap between charge and discharge is energy lost as heat; the shrinking width over cycles is ageing.

The three parts can be told apart by their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears instantaneously, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² s. Concentration polarization is slow, taking 10⁻² s or longer to appear and to fade.2 Interrupt the current and watch the voltage recover: the step is ohmic, the fast curve is activation, the slow drift is concentration.2 Module 11 uses the same clocks with an alternating current.

Why are some curves flat and others sloping? Winter and Brodd answer with the Gibbs phase rule. When the reaction converts one solid phase into another, the number of phases equals the number of components, and once pressure and temperature are fixed no degree of freedom is left: the voltage must stay constant, and the curve is a plateau. When lithium dissolves into a single host phase, one degree of freedom remains and the voltage can slope.2 LiFePO₄ is the two-phase case with its flat 3.5 V plateau;3 the spinel LiMn₂O₄ shows steps near 4 V and a drop to 3 V as the lithium changes sites.3

Which is better depends on the use: electronics prefer a flat, unchanging voltage, while a sloping curve makes it easy to read how much charge is left.2

Two numbers summarize a cycle. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial reversible value.3 A commercial rechargeable cell must survive at least 300 full cycles while losing under 20 % of its capacity, and most need three to eight hours to charge; fast charging stresses the electrodes and shortens cycle life.2

Some capacity loss is only borrowed: what a high rate hides comes back at a low rate. What does not come back, the irreversible loss, comes from electrode volume changes, reactions between electrode and electrolyte, and decomposition of the electrodes.3 Module 10 names the thieves.

The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage: if dE/dT is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as in nickel-cadmium; lead-acid has a negative dE/dT and does the opposite. An irreversible part, Joule heat, comes from the polarization and grows with current; in operation it usually dwarfs the entropic part. The heat is released inside the cell at the electrode surfaces, so high-rate cells must be built to shed it.2

You can now explain why the charge curve sits above the discharge curve, what the three polarizations are and how fast each relaxes, why some curves are flat, and why 99.9 % per cycle is not enough.

Go deeper: the shape of the polarization curve

Near equilibrium the current is proportional to the overpotential, and the ratio defines the charge-transfer resistance, R_ct = RT/(nF i₀), where i₀ is the exchange current density: a reaction with a large exchange current is more reversible and polarizes less for a given current. Battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10⁻² A/cm² at room temperature are favoured.1,2 At large overpotentials one branch of the Butler-Volmer equation dominates and the overpotential grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation, which holds once the back reaction contributes under 1 % of the current, beyond about 118 mV at 25 °C.1

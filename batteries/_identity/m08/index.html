Module 8

Electrodes are composites

Why is an electrode not just a block of the good stuff?

Where we are. In module 7 we carried our ion across the electrolyte. On the far side it has to get into a particle of the positive electrode, one of the two hosts of module 6. In module 2 we saw that an electrode is not a solid block of that host but a coating of particles, carbon, binder and pores. In this module we ask what each of those ingredients is for, and what happens when one of them fails.

Let us look at the coating our ion is about to enter. Most battery electrodes are porous composites: particles of the active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon or a metal powder, and a polymer binder, coated on a current collector. About 30 % of the coating is pore space, so the surface where the reaction can happen is far larger than the electrode’s geometric area.1 A large surface lowers the current density at each point of it, and with it the polarization.1

Each ingredient, then, keeps one road open. The carbon carries electrons from the foil to every particle; the electrolyte in the pores carries ions from the separator to every particle; the binder holds the coating together and onto the foil. A particle that one of the two roads cannot reach stores nothing and only adds mass.

A picture to keep: a town where every house needs two roads, one for each of two deliveries that must arrive together, and is only lived in if both reach it. The picture breaks because here the roads also have tolls: the farther a delivery travels through the coating, the more voltage it costs.

A particle that the electrons or the ions cannot reach contributes nothing.

The tolls decide where in the coating the work gets done. Inside a porous electrode the reaction is not spread evenly. Where it happens depends on the conductivities of the solid matrix and of the electrolyte, the exchange current, diffusion, the thickness of the electrode, its porosity, pore size and tortuosity; and a nonuniform current distribution wastes active material.1

The reaction concentrates where the electrons and the ions together have the shortest, cheapest way to meet. In the simplest theory the profile is a hyperbolic cosine with a characteristic depth.2 This reaction-limited picture is most realistic for thin electrodes, of order 10 μm, and needs corrections beyond about 100 μm.2

Part of the toll is paid in the pores themselves. The electrolyte inside the pores conducts worse than in bulk, because the ions have less room and a winding path. The usual correction is κeff = κεα, with ε the porosity and α = 1.5 (1.53 for LiFePO4 electrodes).3 Ionic transport is far slower than electronic transport, which is also why cells use thin electrodes of large area.4

Worked example. Let us put a number on it: how well does the electrolyte conduct inside a 30 % porous electrode? κeff/κ = 0.301.5 = 0.164 for a liquid of 10−2 S/cm: κeff = 1.6 × 10−3 S/cm, about a sixth Every micrometre of coating the ions must cross costs six times the voltage it would in the open electrolyte; another reason the reaction in figure 8.2 crowds toward the separator.1,3

So far we have assumed that once electrons reach a particle, they reach all of it. For many active materials that is not so. An active material that conducts electrons poorly works only where it touches the conductive additive, and the reacted zone spreads outward from that contact, as in MnO2.1 LiFePO4 is the modern case: its poor electronic conductivity was overcome by coating the particles with carbon and making them small.4

The coating, introduced by Armand and co-workers, is made by decomposing a polymer precursor on the particles, which leaves a uniform layer of carbon.5 With it, a material that was once a dead end is now produced in thousands of tonnes a year.5,6

The other half of that remedy, making the particles small, has its own price. Small particles bring the ions closer to every atom: the time to cross a particle falls with the square of its size (module 6). But carbon-coated nanoparticles have a lower tap density, so the electrode holds less energy per litre, and coating them evenly gets harder as they shrink.4,7 Highly porous aerogel electrodes pay the same price, at a tap density of about 0.2 g/cm3.8

So far we have taken the coating as given. Let us see how it is made, because the making decides how well the two roads work. The composite of this module is made in a few industrial steps. The powders, about 90 % active material by mass with conductive carbon and a polymer binder, are mixed into a slurry, coated on the current-collector foil, dried so that the pores form, and pressed between rollers to an even, denser layer; the electrodes are then cut, stacked or wound with the separator, filled with electrolyte, and cycled for the first time to form the SEI.9

Every step leaves its mark on the roads of figure 8.1: where the carbon and binder end up, how open the pores stay, how well the electrolyte wets them.9

You can now explain what each ingredient of an electrode coating is for, why a particle without both roads is dead weight, where the reaction happens inside a thick electrode and why, why poorly conducting materials need carbon, and what small particles gain and cost.

Our ion now has a round trip that works: across the electrolyte, into a particle, and back. Now let us push the electrodes further, starting with the negative one, and see how the round trip fails.

Go deeper: the porous electrode in one equation

Let us write the current in the solid i1 and in the electrolyte i2; their sum is the cell current, and the electrolyte current grows through the thickness by whatever the reaction converts, di2/dx = a j, with a the reacting area per volume. Ohm’s law in each phase, dφ1/dx = −i1/σ and dφ2/dx = −i2/κ, and the linearized kinetics of module 5, j = η/Rct, with η = φ1 − φ2 − U the local overpotential, give d2η/dx2 = (ν/L)2η, with ν2 = aL2(1/σ + 1/κ)/Rct, the same balance of current that underlies the porous-electrode equations.3,7 Its solution is the cosh profile of figure 8.2, and 1/ν is the reaction depth as a fraction of the thickness. This reaction-limited case and its depth scale have been analysed in full; when the depth is much larger than the electrode the overpotential is uniform.2

Where Act B leaves you

We have followed one ion through a full cycle: the voltage curve its journey draws and what current costs it (module 5), the two hosts it leaves and enters and the layer the first charge builds (module 6), the electrolyte it crosses and the anions that cross the other way (module 7), and the composite it has to find its way into (module 8). Your cell, Graphite | LiCoO2, has done all of this.

In Act C we push the same journey further and watch how it goes wrong: lithium that plates as metal, hosts that crack, interfaces that keep growing, and what a cell does at the end of its life.

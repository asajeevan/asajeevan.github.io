Module 9

Electrode materials: from graphite to lithium metal, from LiCoO2 to organics

If lithium metal is the best negative electrode, why is it not in your phone?

Where we are. In module 8 we built real electrodes out of particles, carbon, binder and pores, and our ion made the round trip through them. The round trip works; now let us push it further. We begin with the negative electrode, the side that holds the lithium when your cell is charged: first graphite, which made the first lithium-ion cell possible, then the materials that might replace it, silicon, conversion materials and lithium metal itself, and the ions that might one day replace lithium. Then we cross to the positive electrode: the oxides in today’s cells, why nickel-rich NMC trades stability for capacity, what comes next, and the organic molecules that do without transition metals. Along the way we answer the question above.

Let us start with what the negative electrode has to do. It stores the lithium while the cell is charged and gives it back on discharge. It was the negative electrode that made the first lithium-ion cell possible: graphite held lithium reversibly and stayed stable in carbonate electrolytes, where lithium metal did not.1 Graphite’s 372 mAh/g now limits how much energy a cell can hold, and the search for something better has produced four families of material, named for how they store lithium: intercalation hosts, alloys, conversion materials and lithium metal.1

What would a better material have to offer? A good negative electrode needs a high reversible capacity, a low potential, fast kinetics, low cost, stability against the electrolyte, a stable SEI and a light environmental footprint.1 On the first two counts lithium metal and silicon stand out; both still face serious problems at the level of the material, the electrode and the cell.1

Worked example. Ten times the capacity, but not ten times the energy. Take a positive electrode like NMC811, which gives about 200 mAh/g,2 at an illustrative average of 3.8 V. The same charge passes through both electrodes, so a gram of the pair passes Q = QposQneg/(Qpos + Qneg). graphite: Q=200×372200+372=130 mAh/g, at 3.8 − 0.15 = 3.65 V: 475 Wh/kg lithium metal: Q=200×3860200+3860=190 mAh/g, at 3.8 V: 723 Wh/kg A factor of 1.5, because the positive electrode now limits the cell. The gain from lithium metal over today’s lithium-ion cells is put at up to a factor of two.1

A negative electrode should sit low and store a lot, but past a point the positive electrode sets the limit.

The families on the map differ in what happens to the material when lithium enters it, and there are three ways for that to go. In intercalation, lithium slips between the layers of a host that keeps its structure, as in graphite, Li+ + e− + xC ⇌ LiCx, or in titanium oxides; that is why these materials last for many cycles. In alloying, lithium forms an alloy with an element such as silicon, germanium or tin, xLi+ + xe− + M ⇌ LixM. In conversion, a compound is taken apart into metal and a lithium compound and rebuilt on the way back. Alloys and conversion materials tend to break into smaller particles as they cycle.1

All three pay a price on the very first charge: they lose a large part of it, mostly to the SEI, which takes lithium from the positive electrode and lowers the first-cycle Coulombic efficiency. Alloys and conversion materials also show a large gap between the voltage at which lithium goes in and the voltage at which it comes out, which lowers the energy a full cell can deliver.1

Graphite, still the workhorse

Of the three mechanisms, intercalation is the one we met in module 6, so let us look at graphite first. Sony’s first lithium-ion cell of 1991 paired graphite with LiCoO2, and three decades later graphite, alone or with some silicon, is still the negative electrode of high-energy cells, now facing nickel-rich layered oxides.1 It is cheap, conducts electrons well, and lithium goes in at about 0.15 V with only 10 to 50 mV of hysteresis.1

Its one real weakness is that it works below the potential at which the electrolyte is stable. On the first charge the electrolyte is reduced on its surface and leaves the SEI (module 6), consuming lithium; once the layer is complete it protects the graphite and lets it cycle.1 Changes at this interface are a major cause of ageing in commercial cells, through five routes: exfoliation and cracking of the graphite, an SEI that keeps forming and dissolving, SEI conversion and growth, SEI dissolution, and lithium plating.1 Graphite particles also crack along their grain boundaries as they expand and shrink, exposing new surface that builds more SEI.1

Temperature decides which route dominates: hot, the SEI grows thicker and consumes lithium and electrolyte; cold, lithium plates on the graphite instead of entering it.1 We follow the growth of the SEI over time in module 10.

Where graphite comes from. Natural graphite costs about $8000 to $11 000 per tonne, synthetic graphite about $20 000; China produces nearly 75 % of the world’s graphite, which is why the United States and Europe are building their own supply. Demand is expected to reach about 1800 kt by 2030.1

Charging fast

Plating, which we just met in the cold, is also a risk when we charge quickly. Electric cars, drones, electric aircraft and grid storage all want faster charging; industry aims to reach 80 % of the charge in under 15 minutes without losing cycle life or safety.1 On the negative electrode three things go wrong. Lithium can arrive faster than it can enter the graphite and plate as metal, a risk made worse by graphite’s low potential; the particles are stressed and crack; and the heat destabilizes the SEI.1

The remedies run from material to cell: smaller particles and porous structures; thin, porous electrodes of low tortuosity; electrolytes with a LiFSI salt or an FEC additive, which improve the SEI and reduce plating; and, at cell level, the right ratio of negative to positive capacity (the N/P ratio), the charging protocol and cooling.1 Silicon-based negative electrodes are being developed for fast charging too: one company reports silicon nanowires charged from 0 to 80 % in under 6 minutes, another columnar silicon grown on copper charged to 80 % in 7 minutes at 1350 Wh/L.1

The other route is to move away from 0 V. Lithium titanate, Li4Ti5O12, works at about 1.55 V, where little SEI forms, and changes volume by only about 0.2 %, a “zero-strain” material: it is safe, long-lived and fast, but stores only 175 mAh/g, conducts poorly and can give off gas (H2, CO2, CO) by decomposing the electrolyte on its surface, a mechanism not yet well understood.1 Niobium oxides such as Nb2O5, TiNb2O7 and Nb14W3O44 also work at 1 to 2 V, store more (about 200 to 350 mAh/g), change little in volume and charge very fast; they are already being made at industrial scale for high-power cells.1

Worked example. What the safe voltage costs. Keep the same positive electrode, like NMC811, which gives about 200 mAh/g,2 at an illustrative average of 3.8 V: graphite: 3.65 V × 130 mAh/g = 475 Wh/kg of the pair Li4Ti5O12: Q=200×175375=93 mAh/g, at 3.8 − 1.55 = 2.25 V: 210 Wh/kg Less than half the energy, in exchange for a volt and a half of margin against plating. That is why titanate serves where power and life matter more than range.

Silicon: ten times the capacity, four times the volume

Titanate trades energy for margin. Silicon makes the opposite trade. It stores 3579 mAh/g, close to ten times graphite, at a low potential, and it is cheap. Its problem is volume: it swells by about 280 % when fully lithiated. The particles pulverize, lose contact with each other, and the electrode can come away from its current collector; the SEI breaks on every swelling and forms again on the fresh surface, using up lithium and electrolyte, so the first-cycle efficiency is low and the capacity fades fast. Silicon also conducts electrons poorly.1

Nanoparticles, porous and hollow spheres, nanowires, yolk–shell and pomegranate-like structures all help, though small particles cost volumetric energy. Binders that hold the contact, perhaps self-healing polymers, could be the breakthrough. Today’s best cells already use graphite with 5 to 10 wt% silicon; more than 20 % is the industry’s next step, trading energy against cycle life.1 The market for silicon negative electrodes, about $279 million in 2023, is projected to reach $15 billion by 2035.1

Worked example. What 280 % does to a particle, and what a little silicon does for a blend. volume after = 1 + 2.8 = 3.8 times; diameter after = 3.81/3 = 1.56 times: a 100 nm particle grows to about 156 nm and back on every cycle 8 wt% silicon in graphite: 0.92×372+0.08×3579=629 mAh/g, 1.7 times graphite The coating, the binder and the SEI on each silicon particle all have to follow that breathing.

The third mechanism goes further still. A conversion electrode is not a host at all. Lithium reduces the compound to metal grains embedded in a lithium compound, for an oxide MxOy + 2yLi+ + 2ye− ⇌ yLi2O + xM, and on the way back the compound forms again. Oxides, sulfides, phosphides and fluorides work this way, with high capacities: about 1000 mAh/g for Fe2O3, 1230 for MnO2, 670 for MoS2 and 900 for FeP.1 Nano-sized transition-metal oxides were shown to store two to three times the capacity of carbon in 2000.3

The drawbacks are four: the particles swell and pulverize; they conduct poorly (the Li2O formed is an insulator); the SEI is unstable; and the voltage hysteresis is about 1 V, because several solid phases must form and dissolve and oxygen and metal atoms must rearrange, not just lithium.1 On top of that, the first cycle loses about 30 %, and the wasted energy is the main obstacle.4 Nano-sized particles shorten the paths and spread the stress, but increase the surface that reacts with the electrolyte.1

The same chemistry also appears on the other side of the cell. Transition-metal fluorides work the same way as positive electrodes, with theoretical capacities of 500 to 1500 mAh/g, and sulfur, at 2.4 V, is also a conversion electrode.3,4

Lithium metal

We now reach the far corner of the map. Lithium metal is the most attractive negative electrode of all: 3860 mAh/g, the lowest potential, and nothing in it but active material. It was studied long before graphite, and kept out of commercial cells by dendrites.1 In 2004 lithium-metal cells reached about 100 to 150 cycles, against the 300 a commercial cell needed.5 Its problems are dendrites, an unstable SEI, low Coulombic efficiency, dead lithium, volume changes and pulverization, and reaction with the electrolyte.1

Why does lithium plate back so unevenly? Several models explain the dendrites. In the space-charge model, when plating outruns ion conduction at high current, the Li+ concentration near the surface falls to zero and dendritic growth begins. Cracks in the SEI, tips and surface defects concentrate the electric field, and lithium grows fastest there. On stripping, pieces break off the dendrites and lose contact: dead lithium, which in one study thickened from about 2 µm to about 100 µm within a few dozen cycles, lowering the efficiency and raising the impedance. Whiskers that pierce the separator short the cell, with a risk of fire.1

Most of these problems start at the interface, not in the bulk metal, which is why coatings, artificial SEI layers and new electrolytes are where the work is.1 The interface is studied with surface spectroscopy (XPS, soft X-ray absorption), optical and electron microscopy, cryo-electron microscopy, which preserves fragile interphase compounds, and synchrotron X-ray diffraction and pair-distribution analysis, which identified the much-debated LiH in the interphase on lithium metal.1

Lithium metal stores the most, but it plates back unevenly, and that, not the chemistry of storage, is what limits it.

If every cycle loses a little lithium, how much lithium should the cell carry in reserve? An anode-free cell carries none: it is assembled with no lithium on the negative side at all, only a copper current collector. On the first charge the lithium in the positive electrode is plated onto the copper, and on discharge it goes back. That is the highest energy a lithium-metal cell can reach, and the simplest to make; but with no lithium in reserve, every loss shows directly as lost capacity.1

Worked example. Why early lithium-metal cells carried far more lithium than they used. Define the lithium cycling efficiency E from the charge stripped each cycle and the excess of lithium used up; Brandt’s relation then gives the relative excess needed for N cycles, R = N(1 − E). LiClO4 in propylene carbonate, E ≈ 60 %: for just 100 cycles, R = 100 × 0.40 = an excess of 40 times the lithium cycled Moli Energy, late 1980s, E > 99 %: about 300 cycles, but only by carrying three times the lithium: R = 300 × 0.01 = an excess of 3 times and for an anode-free cell to keep 80 % for 1000 cycles: E1000≥0.8, so E≥0.81/1000=99.978 % The efficiency, not the capacity, decides whether lithium metal can be used.6

The efficiency, then, is what we must raise, and three kinds of fix are being tried. An artificial SEI, made beforehand on the copper or formed in the cell, guides the lithium to deposit evenly. Electrolyte engineering: additives that build a better SEI, two-salt electrolytes, localized high-concentration electrolytes that mix a dissolving solvent with a diluent that does not dissolve the salt, and fluorinated solvents that are stable against both lithium and nickel-rich positive electrodes. And modified current collectors, such as three-dimensional porous copper that spreads the current and gives the lithium room.1

Lithium metal was long thought too reactive to cycle in a liquid electrolyte. The new electrolytes have changed that: they now allow promising cycling with little excess lithium and heavily loaded positive electrodes.1

Negative electrodes for solid-state cells

So far our lithium metal has faced a liquid. The alternative is to put a solid electrolyte against it, and the main reason for moving to solid electrolytes, which we met in module 7, is to use lithium metal. Graphite and titanate work in solid-state cells but store too little to be worth it; silicon is attractive but its swelling is hard on a rigid electrolyte.1 A solid electrolyte should be stiffer than the electrode to hold back dendrites, and lithium is soft; yet dendrites still grow through ceramic electrolytes, probably along their grain boundaries, and this is not yet understood.1

The lithium/ceramic interface has three problems: poor wetting, an unstable SEI and dendrites. Interlayers of Al2O3, LiNbO3 or ZrO2, often made by atomic layer deposition, a polymer-gel layer, or an additive such as dimethyl sulfate that forms a stiff Li2S/Li2O interphase, all improve it.1 Two other designs avoid the problem: columnar silicon, whose columns change volume anisotropically and so build up less stress, kept 92 % of its capacity after 50 cycles with a sulfide electrolyte against 50.6 % in a liquid one, at a Coulombic efficiency of about 99.95 %; and Samsung’s silver–carbon layer, which needs no excess lithium and gave a prototype pouch cell of more than 900 Wh/L that lasted 1000 cycles.1

Worked example. An interface of 1710 Ω cm2 at a current density of 1 mA/cm2: ΔV=Ri=1710 Ωcm2×0.001 A/cm2=1.71 V with the Al2O3 interlayer, 1 Ω cm2: 1 mV Without the interlayer the interface alone would eat most of a lithium-metal cell’s voltage.

Beyond lithium

Every electrode in this module so far has stored lithium. Must it always be lithium? Lithium itself is a critical raw material, so its supply and sustainability matter too.1 A sodium-ion cell would rock sodium ions instead of lithium ions, on the principle of module 6. But Na+ is larger than Li+ and needs a larger interstitial space than many of the hosts that serve lithium offer, so sodium-ion cells need hosts of their own.7 The older sodium cells used molten electrodes instead: the sodium-sulfur battery, invented at Ford in 1967 after Kummer and Weber found fast Na+ conduction in a ceramic above 300 °C, runs at 300 to 350 °C.7,8 Sodium-ion is the first of the “beyond lithium” chemistries to become commercially viable, so far mainly for stationary storage, where energy density matters less.9

The other side: positive electrodes

So far we have changed only the negative electrode. Let us now cross to the other side, because in today’s cells it is the positive electrode that holds the cell back: its capacity lags behind that of the negative electrode, and its materials account for around half the cost of a cell.2

Almost every commercial positive electrode belongs to one of three families of oxide, identified in Goodenough’s group in the 1980s: layered oxides such as LiCoO2, spinels such as LiMn2O4, and polyanion oxides such as LiFePO4. Oxides rather than sulfides, because the top of the oxide-ion band lies lower in energy, which lets a cell reach about 4 V instead of under 2.5 V.12

The three differ in how the lithium moves and how tightly the oxygen is held. In a layered oxide, lithium moves in planes between sheets of transition-metal oxide; these materials conduct well and pack densely. The spinel gives lithium three-dimensional paths, which favours power, but in practice it is limited to LiMn2O4, whose manganese dissolves and poisons the graphite.12 In a polyanion oxide, the strongly covalent P–O or S–O bonds weaken the covalency of the metal–oxygen bonds, lower the energy of the metal’s redox couple and so raise the voltage, the inductive effect: Fe2+/Fe3+ works below 2.5 V in Fe2O3 but at 3.6 V in Fe2(SO4)3. Oxygen held by phosphorus makes these materials thermally stable and safe, and iron makes them abundant; but they conduct poorly, so their particles must be small and coated with carbon, and they pack less densely.12

From LiCoO2 to nickel-rich NMC

LiCoO2 gives up only about half its lithium, because beyond that the Co3+/Co4+ band overlaps the top of the oxide band and oxygen leaves the lattice.12 Replacing cobalt with nickel and manganese gives NMC, LiNi1−y−zMnyCozO2, and each metal brings something. Nickel brings the capacity: it can be oxidized from Ni2+ all the way to Ni4+ without much loss of oxygen. Manganese, as Mn4+, stabilizes the structure without taking part in the redox. Cobalt keeps the layers ordered and the kinetics fast; in NCA, aluminium in place of manganese adds thermal stability.10,12

More nickel means that more lithium can be taken out within the same voltage window,13 and NMC811 gives about 200 mAh/g.2 The price is stability. Ni4+ is unstable against the electrolyte; nickel and manganese dissolve, migrate to the graphite and thicken its SEI, and the phase changes at high charge crack the particles and open new surface.12 In one comparison of full cells against graphite, NMC111 and NMC622 cycled stably when charged to 4.4 V and NMC811 only to 4.0 V; above those limits the impedance of the positive electrode grew, from the point where each began to release oxygen from its surface, about 4.7 V vs Li for NMC111 and NMC622 and about 4.3 V for NMC811. The best stable result came from NMC622 charged to 4.4 V, about 650 mWh per gram of NMC (figure 9.12).13 Nickel-rich materials are also less thermally stable and more sensitive to air and moisture, which makes them harder and costlier to manufacture.2,10

Worked example. What does a higher cut-off buy, and what does it cost? Take NMC622 in the full cells of figure 9.12. to 4.2 V: 155.4 mAh/g at cycle 5, 147.3 at cycle 300: kept 147.3/155.4= 95 % to 4.4 V: 177.8 → 166.8 mAh/g: 166.8/155.4=1.07, still 7 % more than the 4.2 V cell gave at cycle 5 to 4.6 V: 203.1 → 79.9 mAh/g: kept 79.9/203.1= 39 % Each extra 0.2 V first buys capacity; past the oxygen-release point it destroys it.

Which material wins depends on the use. Nickel-rich NMC and NCA serve long-range and high-performance cars; LFP, cheaper and longer-lived, has come back strongly, its uptake in electric vehicles doubling since 2020, and it serves grid storage and shorter-range cars.2 Cell and pack design have narrowed its energy gap: state-of-the-art LFP cells rose from 90 Wh/kg in 2010 to 160 Wh/kg in 2022, against about 300 Wh/kg for cells with NMC-type positive electrodes.2

What comes next

Several routes lead on. Single-crystal NMC particles avoid the cracks between primary grains that shorten the life of nickel-rich materials.2 Manganese-rich chemistries balance cost against range: LMFP, LiFePO4 with manganese added, reaches cells of 210 to 240 Wh/kg; LNMO works at about 4.7 V but waits for an electrolyte that survives there; lithium- and manganese-rich NMC offers about 280 mAh/g but loses voltage as it cycles.2,12 In 2023, none of these high-manganese cells had clearly passed technology readiness level 5.11 Conversion materials such as FeF3 and FeS2 promise 700 to 900 mAh/g at 1 to 2.5 V vs Li, at the price of large hysteresis, sluggish kinetics and strain.11 And molecules can take the transition metal out altogether, which is where we end this module.

A positive electrode is a compromise between capacity, voltage, stability and cost; more nickel buys capacity and spends margin.

Organic electrodes

Every positive electrode so far stores its charge on a transition metal, which is mined and then processed at high temperature.14 Organic electrodes put the redox centre in a molecule or a polymer made of abundant light elements such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur; they can be made by environmentally friendly synthesis, and some could even be extracted from plants.15 Their potential can be tuned by molecular design, through the bonds or through space.15

They are sorted by the charge the molecule takes on (figure 9.13). An n-type molecule is reduced, and a cation such as Li+ balances it; a p-type molecule is oxidized, and an anion from the electrolyte balances it; a bipolar one does both, in different windows of potential.15 The p-type materials work at relatively high potential but store the anion, so they suit dual-ion or lithium-free cells rather than the rocking chair; n-type materials work with the lithium ion itself.14,15

The redox centre is a functional group: most often a conjugated carbonyl, which becomes an enolate when reduced, but also imines, organosulfur groups, nitroxide radicals and, recently, conjugated sulfonamides and oximates.14,15 Its potential follows the energy of the orbital that gives or takes the electron: groups that draw electron density away from the redox centre lower that orbital and raise the voltage. Most conventional n-type organic materials work below 3 V vs Li.15

A lithium-ion cell adds a condition. Its graphite arrives empty, so the positive electrode must bring the lithium, as LiCoO2 does. An n-type organic made in its oxidized form brings none, and needs lithium metal as the negative electrode; the answer is to make it in its reduced, lithiated form.14,15 But a lithiated organic working below about 2.91 V vs Li is thermodynamically prone to oxidation by air, so most cannot even be handled in a dry room.15

Small molecules also dissolve in the electrolyte, the main route of degradation of organic electrodes; making the material a polymer, building it into a framework, or replacing the liquid with a solid electrolyte are the usual remedies.15 And organic electrodes are usually mixed with a large share of conductive carbon, a quarter to 40 % of the electrode by mass in the studies cited here (a third in most of their half-cell tests).14,16

The p-DHT family

One family shows how far molecular design can go. Its parent is 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalic acid, written p-DHT: the p, for para, marks that the two hydroxy groups sit opposite each other on the ring. The fully lithiated salt, dilithium (2,5-dilithium-oxy)-terephthalate or Li4-p-DHT, carries two kinds of redox group on one benzene ring: two phenolates (–OLi) that react at about 2.6 V and two carboxylates (–CO2Li) that react at about 0.8 V, so the same salt can serve as a positive or a negative electrode. The carboxylates also make it nearly insoluble in carbonate electrolytes.16 It can also be derived entirely from renewable resources.16

Its 2.6 V is too low for a modern cell, and here a subtle change works: two of the four lithium ions are replaced by one magnesium ion. The magnesium stays in place while lithium comes and goes, so it is called a spectator cation.16,17 The salt, magnesium (2,5-dilithium-oxy)-terephthalate or Mg(Li2)-p-DHT, has a name that reflects its synthesis: magnesium hydroxide first neutralizes the two carboxylic acid groups of 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalic acid, and two equivalents of lithium hydroxide then neutralize the two phenol groups.16 The name might suggest that magnesium sits only at the carboxylates; the crystal structure, published in 2025, shows otherwise. Each Mg2+ sits in an octahedron of six oxygen atoms, four from phenolates and two from carboxylates. It is chelated by two p-DHT4− ions, each through a phenolate oxygen and the neighbouring carboxylate oxygen of that ion, closing two six-membered rings; two more phenolate oxygens complete the octahedron. Each phenolate oxygen, the redox centre, is bonded to one Li+ and two Mg2+ (see the structures below).17

Mg2+ is small and doubly charged: it has a high ionic potential, its charge divided by its radius. Its bonds to oxygen are therefore more covalent. At the phenolate oxygen, bonded to two Mg2+, they draw electron density from that oxygen and through it from the ring; together with its bonds to the carboxylates, they weaken the electron donation (the donor inductive effect) of the –O− and –CO2− groups into the ring. The phenolate becomes harder to oxidize, and the potential rises by about 0.8 V, to about 3.4 V, in the range of LiFePO4; a calculation from the solved structure and the predicted half-delithiated one gives 3.45 V, in line with the measurement.16,17 Across the barium, lithium, calcium and magnesium salts, measured in the same way, the average potential rises almost linearly with the ionic potential of the spectator cation.16

Once dehydrated, the magnesium salt is stable in air, and it cycles stably near 100 mAh/g; but, like bulk Li4-p-DHT, it gives only one of its two possible electrons, whereas exfoliated nanosheets of Li4-p-DHT have been reported to reach the full two-electron capacity.16,17 The 2025 study explains why. After one electron is removed, each ring carries a radical; the rings, stacked face to face, pair up into dimers whose spins cancel, a Peierls distortion, and electron spin resonance finds only about 1.2 % of one unpaired spin per formula unit. Calculations indicate that removing the second electron would weaken or even break the C=O bonds of the carboxylates; charging bulk material beyond 4.0 V instead starts an irreversible reaction, attributed to Kolbe electrolysis.17

Changing the organic ion instead also works. In the ortho isomer, dilithium (2,3-dilithium-oxy)-terephthalate or Li4-o-DHT, the two –OLi groups sit side by side, and the potential rises by about 0.3 V; like the para salt, it is oxidized by air.16 Carboxylates held off the ring by a methylene (CH2) spacer, in tetralithium 2,5-dioxido-1,4-benzenediacetate (Li4-p-DOBDA), are no longer conjugated with it; the potential still rises, to 3.35 V, which the authors attribute to a through-space charge modulation in the solid, with both electrons exchanged, 215 mAh/g, though with a large polarization on cycling.17

The structures in 3D: the p-DHT family, and where the cations sit

From my own work. My doctorate took Mg(Li2)-p-DHT as its benchmark positive electrode and built it into soft solid-state cells against lithium metal, with a solid polymer electrolyte that limits the dissolution of the organic material; it also developed a route to recover the organic precursor from the cycled electrode material.

Beyond carbonyl chemistry: conjugated sulfonamides and oximates

Most lithiated organic positive electrodes, the p-DHT family included, rest on enolate and carbonyl chemistry.14 A newer line uses a different redox-active unit: a p-phenylenediamine core whose two nitrogen atoms each carry an electron-withdrawing group, methanesulfonyl (–SO2CH3) in the conjugated sulfonamides, trifluoromethanesulfonyl (–SO2CF3) in the conjugated triflimides and cyano (–C≡N) in the cyanamides. Conjugation spreads the negative charge of the lithiated form over the whole core and its withdrawing groups. On charge the core gives up two electrons in two steps, through a radical anion, to a neutral quinone diimine form: the Wurster-type redox of p-phenylenediamines.15 Because its charge is spread out, the anion is less nucleophilic, so the lithiated material resists water and oxygen in ambient air and still reacts reversibly at high potential.14,15

Trifluoromethanesulfonyl withdraws electrons more strongly than methanesulfonyl, and the swap raised the potential by 0.5 V: the conjugated triflimides (the authors’ name for the family) work at 3.26 to 3.8 V, the highest reported for n-type organics by 2024, and are stable in moist ambient air. The simplest cyanamide, Li2-PDCA, stores more, 320 mAh/g in theory, but its fully charged form dissolves in the electrolyte.15 A third class, the conjugated oximates, carries its charge on C=N–O− groups; one of them, Li2-BQDO, reaches about 350 mAh/g at about 3.0 V, close to 1 kWh/kg at the material level.14

Cycling stability remains a concern for most organic electrode materials, and it is too early to think of commercializing the newest of them.14 But their voltages now reach those of LiFePO4 and beyond, without cobalt or nickel.15,16

In an organic electrode the molecule is the host; its voltage is set by the groups around the redox centre, and even by the cation beside it.

You can now explain where each family of negative electrode sits on the map of capacity and potential and why extra capacity has diminishing returns; the three ways to store lithium; how graphite’s SEI forms and how graphite ages hot and cold; why fast charging risks plating and how titanate and niobium oxides avoid it; why silicon and conversion materials are hard to cycle; what limits lithium metal and anode-free cells, and the fixes; why the lithium/ceramic interface matters in solid-state cells; why sodium needs hosts of its own; the three families of positive electrode and what nickel, manganese and cobalt each bring; why nickel-rich NMC trades stability for capacity; and how organic electrodes store charge and how their voltage is tuned.

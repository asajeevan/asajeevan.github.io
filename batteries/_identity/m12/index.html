Module 12

Safety, use and end of life

How do you keep a cell safe, and what happens to it at the end?

Where we are. In module 11 we learned to measure a cell and to find out what has gone wrong inside it. Two questions remain, and they matter most to the person holding the cell. What happens when it is abused, so that its stored energy comes out all at once? And what becomes of its materials when it is finished?

Let us begin with the energy itself. A charged cell holds its energy in two materials kept apart. If they meet through a short circuit, the whole stored energy comes out as heat.1 The worst case is thermal runaway: the reaction of an electrode with the electrolyte becomes self-sustaining and enters an autocatalytic mode, the heat it releases speeding it up. Because this heat is released inside the cell, at the electrode surfaces, cells meant for high currents must be built to shed it.1 The SEI we met in module 6 plays a part here too: its decomposition promotes thermal runaway.2

The history of the rechargeable lithium cell is written in fires. Exxon’s rechargeable lithium-metal cell of the 1970s grew dendrites across the electrolyte to the positive electrode; the internal short ignited the flammable electrolyte and the programme was abandoned.3 In 1989 the first lithium-metal rechargeable cell sold for consumer use, Moli Energy’s Li/MoS2, was recalled after cells vented with flame in the field: the porous, high-surface lithium deposited by cycling reacts with the electrolyte strongly enough to do that, and cycling makes cells less tolerant of abuse.4

A cell is safe as long as its heat can leave faster than its reactions make it.

Electronics and separators

If a cell is safe only while its heat leaves faster than its reactions make it, the first task is to stop the faults that start those reactions. Let us go through the safeguards from the outside in. Lithium cells have no built-in chemical way of shedding overcharge, unlike nickel-cadmium, and in a battery of cells in series small differences in capacity and self-discharge push individual cells out of balance. So the end of charge and of discharge is controlled by watching the voltage, cell by cell; Sony’s first lithium-ion batteries carried a circuit that did exactly that and also limited the current.1,4

Inside the cell, the next safeguard is the separator. A separator that loses its porosity when it gets hot stops the ions in a fault that heats the cell evenly, such as a short outside it, but helps much less against a local internal short.4 A ceramic Al2O3 coating on the separator helps keep dendrites from getting through.5

The electrolyte as a safeguard

The electrolyte can be a safeguard too. To see how, consider what overcharge does to the cell. An overcharged cell loses too much lithium from its positive electrode, whose crystal starts to release oxygen and becomes unstable, while lithium metal deposits on the negative electrode; the electrodes then decompose the solvents, and a sudden exothermic reaction follows. Additives in small quantities answer this in three main ways: redox shuttles consume the excess current through a redox process and keep the voltage from rising; aromatic compounds such as biphenyl are believed to polymerize above the maximum operating voltage into an insulating layer on the positive electrode, raising the cell’s resistance; and compounds such as cyclohexylbenzene decompose and give off hydrogen, so that a pressure-operated current interrupter cuts the charging current. None is free: additives always have side effects, and with biphenyl, cycle life at 40 °C up to 4.3 V got worse the more of it there was.6

The electrolyte itself could also be made safer. A nonflammable liquid is a further aim: phosphate esters such as triethyl phosphate have very high flash points, but trimethyl phosphate is unstable against graphite, which led some to judge such electrolytes impractical.6

Safety built into the chemistry

The innermost safeguard is the chemistry itself. The lithium-metal polymer cells that Bolloré has used in electric cars and buses since 2011 are an example: solid polymer electrolytes, more than 3000 cycles without dendrites.7

So far we have looked at abuse. Ordinary use matters too: temperature and state of charge decide how a cell ages, and we take temperature first. Consumer cells are designed to work between about 0 and 40 °C and to be stored between −20 and 85 °C; military and automotive cells must work from −50 to 85 °C.1 Within those limits, three trends matter. Self-discharge rises with temperature.1 The SEI grows fastest when the cell sits at a high state of charge and is charged fast.8 And at high current and low temperature, lithium can plate on the graphite instead of entering it.9

What that means in your pocket. Read together, those statements favour keeping a cell cool, not leaving it full for long periods, and not fast-charging it in the cold. This is our own reading of the three statements above, not a rule stated in any of the works cited.

Even a cell that is used kindly reaches the end of its life. What becomes of it then? Recycling a lithium-ion battery means recovering above all its cobalt, lithium and nickel, besides aluminium and copper. Two chemical routes dominate: pyrometallurgy, which smelts the cells at about 1000 °C into a copper-nickel-cobalt alloy while lithium and manganese go to the slag, and hydrometallurgy, which dissolves the electrode materials in acid and separates the metals, with higher recovery and lower energy use but more complex processing. Direct recycling, which keeps the electrode materials whole, is still immature.9

Why does this matter? Cobalt is scarce, costly and toxic, which is one reason the positive electrode has moved from LiCoO2 toward materials with less of it.7,10 Lead-acid batteries show what a closed loop can look like: by a 2004 account, about 98 % of them were recycled in the United States.1

A loop can only close if the spent cells are collected in the first place. For lithium batteries, a 2020 European proposal would raise the collection target for portable batteries from 45 % to 65 % by 2025 and 70 % by 2030, and require all batteries from electric vehicles and stationary storage to be collected and recycled.9

From my own work. The organic electrode materials of my doctorate were designed with their end of life in mind: I developed a route that recovers the organic precursor from a cycled electrode material, Mg(Li2)-p-DHT, and separates the streams containing lithium and magnesium (manuscript in preparation).

You can now explain what thermal runaway is and what breaks the loop, why lithium cells need electronics as well as chemistry to stay safe, why temperature and state of charge decide how a cell ages, and the three ways its materials can go back into a new cell.

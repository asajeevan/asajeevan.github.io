Chapter 15

Sodium-ion and organic electrode materials

Can sodium, or molecules made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, do lithium's job?

Beyond lithium

Every electrode in this module so far has stored lithium. Must it always be lithium? Lithium itself is a critical raw material, so its supply and sustainability matter too.1 A sodium-ion cell would rock sodium ions instead of lithium ions, on the principle of module 6. But Na+ is larger than Li+ and needs a larger interstitial space than many of the hosts that serve lithium offer, so sodium-ion cells need hosts of their own.2 The older sodium cells used molten electrodes instead: the sodium-sulfur battery, invented at Ford in 1967 after Kummer and Weber found fast Na+ conduction in a ceramic above 300 °C, runs at 300 to 350 °C.2,3 Sodium-ion is the first of the “beyond lithium” chemistries to become commercially viable, so far mainly for stationary storage, where energy density matters less.4

Organic electrodes

Every positive electrode so far stores its charge on a transition metal, which is mined and then processed at high temperature.7 Organic electrodes put the redox centre in a molecule or a polymer made of abundant light elements such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur; they can be made by environmentally friendly synthesis, and some could even be extracted from plants.8 Their potential can be tuned by molecular design, through the bonds or through space.8

They are sorted by the charge the molecule takes on (figure 9.13). An n-type molecule is reduced, and a cation such as Li+ balances it; a p-type molecule is oxidized, and an anion from the electrolyte balances it; a bipolar one does both, in different windows of potential.8 The p-type materials work at relatively high potential but store the anion, so they suit dual-ion or lithium-free cells rather than the rocking chair; n-type materials work with the lithium ion itself.7,8

The redox centre is a functional group: most often a conjugated carbonyl, which becomes an enolate when reduced, but also imines, organosulfur groups, nitroxide radicals and, recently, conjugated sulfonamides and oximates.7,8 Its potential follows the energy of the orbital that gives or takes the electron: groups that draw electron density away from the redox centre lower that orbital and raise the voltage. Most conventional n-type organic materials work below 3 V vs Li.8

A lithium-ion cell adds a condition. Its graphite arrives empty, so the positive electrode must bring the lithium, as LiCoO2 does. An n-type organic made in its oxidized form brings none, and needs lithium metal as the negative electrode; the answer is to make it in its reduced, lithiated form.7,8 But a lithiated organic working below about 2.91 V vs Li is thermodynamically prone to oxidation by air, so most cannot even be handled in a dry room.8

Small molecules also dissolve in the electrolyte, the main route of degradation of organic electrodes; making the material a polymer, building it into a framework, or replacing the liquid with a solid electrolyte are the usual remedies.8 And organic electrodes are usually mixed with a large share of conductive carbon, a quarter to 40 % of the electrode by mass in the studies cited here (a third in most of their half-cell tests).7,9

The p-DHT family

One family shows how far molecular design can go. Its parent is 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalic acid, written p-DHT: the p, for para, marks that the two hydroxy groups sit opposite each other on the ring. The fully lithiated salt, dilithium (2,5-dilithium-oxy)-terephthalate or Li4-p-DHT, carries two kinds of redox group on one benzene ring: two phenolates (–OLi) that react at about 2.6 V and two carboxylates (–CO2Li) that react at about 0.8 V, so the same salt can serve as a positive or a negative electrode. The carboxylates also make it nearly insoluble in carbonate electrolytes.9 It can also be derived entirely from renewable resources.9

Its 2.6 V is too low for a modern cell, and here a subtle change works: two of the four lithium ions are replaced by one magnesium ion. The magnesium stays in place while lithium comes and goes, so it is called a spectator cation.9,10 The salt, magnesium (2,5-dilithium-oxy)-terephthalate or Mg(Li2)-p-DHT, has a name that reflects its synthesis: magnesium hydroxide first neutralizes the two carboxylic acid groups of 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalic acid, and two equivalents of lithium hydroxide then neutralize the two phenol groups.9 The name might suggest that magnesium sits only at the carboxylates; the crystal structure, published in 2025, shows otherwise. Each Mg2+ sits in an octahedron of six oxygen atoms, four from phenolates and two from carboxylates. It is chelated by two p-DHT4− ions, each through a phenolate oxygen and the neighbouring carboxylate oxygen of that ion, closing two six-membered rings; two more phenolate oxygens complete the octahedron. Each phenolate oxygen, the redox centre, is bonded to one Li+ and two Mg2+ (see the structures below).10

Mg2+ is small and doubly charged: it has a high ionic potential, its charge divided by its radius. Its bonds to oxygen are therefore more covalent. At the phenolate oxygen, bonded to two Mg2+, they draw electron density from that oxygen and through it from the ring; together with its bonds to the carboxylates, they weaken the electron donation (the donor inductive effect) of the –O− and –CO2− groups into the ring. The phenolate becomes harder to oxidize, and the potential rises by about 0.8 V, to about 3.4 V, in the range of LiFePO4; a calculation from the solved structure and the predicted half-delithiated one gives 3.45 V, in line with the measurement.9,10 Across the barium, lithium, calcium and magnesium salts, measured in the same way, the average potential rises almost linearly with the ionic potential of the spectator cation.9

Once dehydrated, the magnesium salt is stable in air, and it cycles stably near 100 mAh/g; but, like bulk Li4-p-DHT, it gives only one of its two possible electrons, whereas exfoliated nanosheets of Li4-p-DHT have been reported to reach the full two-electron capacity.9,10 The 2025 study explains why. After one electron is removed, each ring carries a radical; the rings, stacked face to face, pair up into dimers whose spins cancel, a Peierls distortion, and electron spin resonance finds only about 1.2 % of one unpaired spin per formula unit. Calculations indicate that removing the second electron would weaken or even break the C=O bonds of the carboxylates; charging bulk material beyond 4.0 V instead starts an irreversible reaction, attributed to Kolbe electrolysis.10

Changing the organic ion instead also works. In the ortho isomer, dilithium (2,3-dilithium-oxy)-terephthalate or Li4-o-DHT, the two –OLi groups sit side by side, and the potential rises by about 0.3 V; like the para salt, it is oxidized by air.9 Carboxylates held off the ring by a methylene (CH2) spacer, in tetralithium 2,5-dioxido-1,4-benzenediacetate (Li4-p-DOBDA), are no longer conjugated with it; the potential still rises, to 3.35 V, which the authors attribute to a through-space charge modulation in the solid, with both electrons exchanged, 215 mAh/g, though with a large polarization on cycling.10

From my own work. My doctorate took Mg(Li2)-p-DHT as its benchmark positive electrode and built it into soft solid-state cells against lithium metal, with a solid polymer electrolyte that limits the dissolution of the organic material; it also developed a route to recover the organic precursor from the cycled electrode material.

Beyond carbonyl chemistry: conjugated sulfonamides and oximates

Most lithiated organic positive electrodes, the p-DHT family included, rest on enolate and carbonyl chemistry.7 A newer line uses a different redox-active unit: a p-phenylenediamine core whose two nitrogen atoms each carry an electron-withdrawing group, methanesulfonyl (–SO2CH3) in the conjugated sulfonamides, trifluoromethanesulfonyl (–SO2CF3) in the conjugated triflimides and cyano (–C≡N) in the cyanamides. Conjugation spreads the negative charge of the lithiated form over the whole core and its withdrawing groups. On charge the core gives up two electrons in two steps, through a radical anion, to a neutral quinone diimine form: the Wurster-type redox of p-phenylenediamines.8 Because its charge is spread out, the anion is less nucleophilic, so the lithiated material resists water and oxygen in ambient air and still reacts reversibly at high potential.7,8

Trifluoromethanesulfonyl withdraws electrons more strongly than methanesulfonyl, and the swap raised the potential by 0.5 V: the conjugated triflimides (the authors’ name for the family) work at 3.26 to 3.8 V, the highest reported for n-type organics by 2024, and are stable in moist ambient air. The simplest cyanamide, Li2-PDCA, stores more, 320 mAh/g in theory, but its fully charged form dissolves in the electrolyte.8 A third class, the conjugated oximates, carries its charge on C=N–O− groups; one of them, Li2-BQDO, reaches about 350 mAh/g at about 3.0 V, close to 1 kWh/kg at the material level.7

Cycling stability remains a concern for most organic electrode materials, and it is too early to think of commercializing the newest of them.7 But their voltages now reach those of LiFePO4 and beyond, without cobalt or nickel.8,9

In an organic electrode the molecule is the host; its voltage is set by the groups around the redox centre, and even by the cation beside it.

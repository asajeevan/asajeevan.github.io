Chapter 18

End of life: recycling of lithium-ion batteries

What happens to a battery when it is finished?

Even a cell that is used kindly reaches the end of its life. What becomes of it then? Recycling a lithium-ion battery means recovering above all its cobalt, lithium and nickel, besides aluminium and copper. Two chemical routes dominate: pyrometallurgy, which smelts the cells at about 1000 °C into a copper-nickel-cobalt alloy while lithium and manganese go to the slag, and hydrometallurgy, which dissolves the electrode materials in acid and separates the metals, with higher recovery and lower energy use but more complex processing. Direct recycling, which keeps the electrode materials whole, is still immature.1

Why does this matter? Cobalt is scarce, costly and toxic, which is one reason the positive electrode has moved from LiCoO2 toward materials with less of it.2,3 Lead-acid batteries show what a closed loop can look like: by a 2004 account, about 98 % of them were recycled in the United States.4

A loop can only close if the spent cells are collected in the first place. For lithium batteries, a 2020 European proposal would raise the collection target for portable batteries from 45 % to 65 % by 2025 and 70 % by 2030, and require all batteries from electric vehicles and stationary storage to be collected and recycled.1

From my own work. The organic electrode materials of my doctorate were designed with their end of life in mind: I developed a route that recovers the organic precursor from a cycled electrode material, Mg(Li2)-p-DHT, and separates the streams containing lithium and magnesium (manuscript in preparation).

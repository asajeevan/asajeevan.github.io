Chapter 12

Negative electrode materials: graphite, silicon, lithium metal and anode-free cells

What could replace graphite, and why has it not happened yet?

Where we are. In chapter 11 we built real electrodes out of particles, carbon, binder and pores, and our ion made the round trip through them. The round trip works; now let us push it further. We begin with the negative electrode, the side that holds the lithium when your cell is charged: first graphite, which made the first lithium-ion cell possible, then the materials that might replace it, silicon, conversion materials and lithium metal itself, and the ions that might one day replace lithium. Then we cross to the positive electrode: the oxides in today’s cells, why nickel-rich NMC trades stability for capacity, what comes next, and the organic molecules that do without transition metals. Along the way we answer the question above.

Let us start with what the negative electrode has to do. It stores the lithium while the cell is charged and gives it back on discharge. It was the negative electrode that made the first lithium-ion cell possible: graphite held lithium reversibly and stayed stable in carbonate electrolytes, where lithium metal did not.1 Graphite’s 372 mAh/g now limits how much energy a cell can hold, and the search for something better has produced four families of material, named for how they store lithium: intercalation hosts, alloys, conversion materials and lithium metal.1

What would a better material have to offer? A good negative electrode needs a high reversible capacity, a low potential, fast kinetics, low cost, stability against the electrolyte, a stable SEI and a light environmental footprint.1 On the first two counts lithium metal and silicon stand out; both still face serious problems at the level of the material, the electrode and the cell.1

Worked example. Ten times the capacity, but not ten times the energy. Take a positive electrode like NMC811, which gives about 200 mAh/g,2 at an illustrative average of 3.8 V. The same charge passes through both electrodes, so a gram of the pair passes Q = QposQneg/(Qpos + Qneg). graphite: Q=200×372200+372=130 mAh/g, at 3.8 − 0.15 = 3.65 V: 475 Wh/kg lithium metal: Q=200×3860200+3860=190 mAh/g, at 3.8 V: 723 Wh/kg A factor of 1.5, because the positive electrode now limits the cell. The gain from lithium metal over today’s lithium-ion cells is put at up to a factor of two.1

A negative electrode should sit low and store a lot, but past a point the positive electrode sets the limit.

A 2026 review gives the theoretical capacities by class (the strip at the top of figure 12.1): nanostructured carbons such as nanotubes, graphene and porous carbons up to 1750 mAh/g; metal oxides 375 to 1170, nitrides 400 to 1300, sulfides 447 to 1230 and phosphides 700 to 1800, all by conversion; and, among alloying elements, tin 960, indium 1012, germanium 1384, phosphorus 2596 and silicon 4200 mAh/g.3 Its value for silicon is higher than the 3579 mAh/g on our map, a reminder to check which lithiated end-state a quoted capacity assumes. The review also notes that Li4Ti5O12 cycles well because it neither expands nor contracts as lithium goes in and out, at the price of its relatively high potential, and that composites of carbon nanotubes with silicon or metal oxides have shown over 1000 mAh/g with good retention over hundreds of cycles.3

Charging fast

Plating, which we just met in the cold, is also a risk when we charge quickly. Electric cars, drones, electric aircraft and grid storage all want faster charging; industry aims to reach 80 % of the charge in under 15 minutes without losing cycle life or safety.1 On the negative electrode three things go wrong. Lithium can arrive faster than it can enter the graphite and plate as metal, a risk made worse by graphite’s low potential; the particles are stressed and crack; and the heat destabilizes the SEI.1

The remedies run from material to cell: smaller particles and porous structures; thin, porous electrodes of low tortuosity; electrolytes with a LiFSI salt or an FEC additive, which improve the SEI and reduce plating; and, at cell level, the right ratio of negative to positive capacity (the N/P ratio), the charging protocol and cooling.1 Silicon-based negative electrodes are being developed for fast charging too: one company reports silicon nanowires charged from 0 to 80 % in under 6 minutes, another columnar silicon grown on copper charged to 80 % in 7 minutes at 1350 Wh/L.1

The other route is to move away from 0 V. Lithium titanate, Li4Ti5O12, works at about 1.55 V, where little SEI forms, and changes volume by only about 0.2 %, a “zero-strain” material: it is safe, long-lived and fast, but stores only 175 mAh/g, conducts poorly and can give off gas (H2, CO2, CO) by decomposing the electrolyte on its surface, a mechanism not yet well understood.1 Niobium oxides such as Nb2O5, TiNb2O7 and Nb14W3O44 also work at 1 to 2 V, store more (about 200 to 350 mAh/g), change little in volume and charge very fast; they are already being made at industrial scale for high-power cells.1

Worked example. What the safe voltage costs. Keep the same positive electrode, like NMC811, which gives about 200 mAh/g,2 at an illustrative average of 3.8 V: graphite: 3.65 V × 130 mAh/g = 475 Wh/kg of the pair Li4Ti5O12: Q=200×175375=93 mAh/g, at 3.8 − 1.55 = 2.25 V: 210 Wh/kg Less than half the energy, in exchange for a volt and a half of margin against plating. That is why titanate serves where power and life matter more than range.

Silicon: ten times the capacity, four times the volume

Titanate trades energy for margin. Silicon makes the opposite trade. It stores 3579 mAh/g, close to ten times graphite, at a low potential, and it is cheap. Its problem is volume: it swells by about 280 % when fully lithiated. The particles pulverize, lose contact with each other, and the electrode can come away from its current collector; the SEI breaks on every swelling and forms again on the fresh surface, using up lithium and electrolyte, so the first-cycle efficiency is low and the capacity fades fast. Silicon also conducts electrons poorly.1

Nanoparticles, porous and hollow spheres, nanowires, yolk–shell and pomegranate-like structures all help, though small particles cost volumetric energy. Binders that hold the contact, perhaps self-healing polymers, could be the breakthrough. Today’s best cells already use graphite with 5 to 10 wt% silicon; more than 20 % is the industry’s next step, trading energy against cycle life.1 The market for silicon negative electrodes, about $279 million in 2023, is projected to reach $15 billion by 2035.1

Worked example. What 280 % does to a particle, and what a little silicon does for a blend. volume after = 1 + 2.8 = 3.8 times; diameter after = 3.81/3 = 1.56 times: a 100 nm particle grows to about 156 nm and back on every cycle 8 wt% silicon in graphite: 0.92×372+0.08×3579=629 mAh/g, 1.7 times graphite The coating, the binder and the SEI on each silicon particle all have to follow that breathing.

The third mechanism goes further still. A conversion electrode is not a host at all. Lithium reduces the compound to metal grains embedded in a lithium compound, for an oxide MxOy + 2yLi+ + 2ye− ⇌ yLi2O + xM, and on the way back the compound forms again. Oxides, sulfides, phosphides and fluorides work this way, with high capacities: about 1000 mAh/g for Fe2O3, 1230 for MnO2, 670 for MoS2 and 900 for FeP.1 Nano-sized transition-metal oxides were shown to store two to three times the capacity of carbon in 2000.4

The drawbacks are four: the particles swell and pulverize; they conduct poorly (the Li2O formed is an insulator); the SEI is unstable; and the voltage hysteresis is about 1 V, because several solid phases must form and dissolve and oxygen and metal atoms must rearrange, not just lithium.1 On top of that, the first cycle loses about 30 %, and the wasted energy is the main obstacle.5 Nano-sized particles shorten the paths and spread the stress, but increase the surface that reacts with the electrolyte.1

The same chemistry also appears on the other side of the cell. Transition-metal fluorides work the same way as positive electrodes, with theoretical capacities of 500 to 1500 mAh/g, and sulfur, at 2.4 V, is also a conversion electrode.4,5

Lithium metal

We now reach the far corner of the map. Lithium metal is the most attractive negative electrode of all: 3860 mAh/g, the lowest potential, and nothing in it but active material. It was studied long before graphite, and kept out of commercial cells by dendrites.1 In 2004 lithium-metal cells reached about 100 to 150 cycles, against the 300 a commercial cell needed.6 Its problems are dendrites, an unstable SEI, low Coulombic efficiency, dead lithium, volume changes and pulverization, and reaction with the electrolyte.1

Why does lithium plate back so unevenly? Several models explain the dendrites. In the space-charge model, when plating outruns ion conduction at high current, the Li+ concentration near the surface falls to zero and dendritic growth begins. Cracks in the SEI, tips and surface defects concentrate the electric field, and lithium grows fastest there. On stripping, pieces break off the dendrites and lose contact: dead lithium, which in one study thickened from about 2 µm to about 100 µm within a few dozen cycles, lowering the efficiency and raising the impedance. Whiskers that pierce the separator short the cell, with a risk of fire.1

Most of these problems start at the interface, not in the bulk metal, which is why coatings, artificial SEI layers and new electrolytes are where the work is.1 The interface is studied with surface spectroscopy (XPS, soft X-ray absorption), optical and electron microscopy, cryo-electron microscopy, which preserves fragile interphase compounds, and synchrotron X-ray diffraction and pair-distribution analysis, which identified the much-debated LiH in the interphase on lithium metal.1

Lithium metal stores the most, but it plates back unevenly, and that, not the chemistry of storage, is what limits it.

If every cycle loses a little lithium, how much lithium should the cell carry in reserve? An anode-free cell carries none: it is assembled with no lithium on the negative side at all, only a copper current collector. On the first charge the lithium in the positive electrode is plated onto the copper, and on discharge it goes back. That is the highest energy a lithium-metal cell can reach, and the simplest to make; but with no lithium in reserve, every loss shows directly as lost capacity.1

Worked example. Why early lithium-metal cells carried far more lithium than they used. Define the lithium cycling efficiency E from the charge stripped each cycle and the excess of lithium used up; Brandt’s relation then gives the relative excess needed for N cycles, R = N(1 − E). LiClO4 in propylene carbonate, E ≈ 60 %: for just 100 cycles, R = 100 × 0.40 = an excess of 40 times the lithium cycled Moli Energy, late 1980s, E > 99 %: about 300 cycles, but only by carrying three times the lithium: R = 300 × 0.01 = an excess of 3 times and for an anode-free cell to keep 80 % for 1000 cycles: E1000≥0.8, so E≥0.81/1000=99.978 % The efficiency, not the capacity, decides whether lithium metal can be used.7

The efficiency, then, is what we must raise, and three kinds of fix are being tried. An artificial SEI, made beforehand on the copper or formed in the cell, guides the lithium to deposit evenly. Electrolyte engineering: additives that build a better SEI, two-salt electrolytes, localized high-concentration electrolytes that mix a dissolving solvent with a diluent that does not dissolve the salt, and fluorinated solvents that are stable against both lithium and nickel-rich positive electrodes. And modified current collectors, such as three-dimensional porous copper that spreads the current and gives the lithium room.1

Lithium metal was long thought too reactive to cycle in a liquid electrolyte. The new electrolytes have changed that: they now allow promising cycling with little excess lithium and heavily loaded positive electrodes.1

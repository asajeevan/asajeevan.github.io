Chapter 5

Galvanostatic cycling and polarization: overpotentials, hysteresis and heat generation

What happens when we charge a cell and use it, and why does it get warm?

Where we are. In chapter 4 we counted the charge your cell can hold and turned it into energy, with the cell mostly at rest. Now we let the current flow and ask what that costs. We start at the test bench, with how a charge and a discharge are actually done and recorded. Then we follow what current costs (voltage, capacity and heat), why some curves are flat and others slope, what happens inside real electrodes made of many particles, how a curve can be read for signs of ageing, and how many cycles a cell survives. From here on we follow one lithium ion, our ion, through the cell.

Let us connect your cell to something that uses it. A discharge is the cell doing work: current flows out through a lamp, a phone or a motor. A charge is the reverse: a charger pushes current back in, in the opposite direction, and restores the cell to its charged condition.1 On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced; charging runs both reactions backwards.1 In a lithium-ion cell that means lithium ions leave one electrode, cross the electrolyte and enter the other, while their electrons go round through the wire (chapter 2).

The test bench

To watch this happen we put the cell on a test bench. Cells are studied, and their capacities measured, on a cycler: an instrument that sets the current and records the voltage at regular intervals (once a second in one set of tests). Most tests hold the current constant. This is galvanostatic cycling, and its record, the voltage against the charge passed, is the raw material of everything else in this module.2

At constant current the charge passed is simply current times time, Q=It. Currents are quoted as C-rates: 1C is the current that passes the cell’s full nominal charge in one hour, so C/20 takes twenty hours. In one study, for example, a 210 mAh cell was recorded at C/20, that is at 10.5 mA.2

A step cannot run for ever. Each step stops at a cut-off voltage. Overcharging or overdischarging drives unwanted reactions that cost capacity.1 Charging often ends with a constant-voltage step: the cycler holds the upper cut-off, the current falls away by itself, and the step ends when it reaches a small limit. In two published protocols the cycler held 4.2 V until the current had fallen to C/20 in one and to C/25 in the other.2,3

Worked example. A 2.0 Ah cell discharged at 1C for 30 minutes. I=1C×2.0 Ah=2.0 A Q=It=2.0 A×1800 s=3600 C = 1.0 Ah, half the cell At C/20 the same half takes ten hours, at 0.10 A.
building a coin cell and running a cycling test
  1. Punch discs of both electrodes (1.27 cm in one published guide, the same size for both), dry the coated discs under vacuum overnight at 110 °C in the antechamber of an argon glove box, and assemble the cell inside it.4
  2. Stack from the bottom: the can, the positive electrode, the separator, the negative electrode (lithium foil in a half cell, graphite in a full cell), a spacer, a spring, the gasket and the cap.4
  3. Add the electrolyte drop by drop between the layers, a fixed amount for every cell: in that guide 19 drops, about 38 mg, with two thin separators, or 21 drops, about 42 mg, with one thick one.4
  4. Keep the discs exactly on top of each other. Place the spacer and spring from directly above, with a vacuum pen rather than tweezers: a slightly misaligned disc let lithium plate where the positive electrode was not covered by the negative, and three “identical” cells kept about 95, 90 and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. One thick, compliant separator gave more even pressure than two thin ones.4
  5. Crimp the cell, then cycle it in a temperature-controlled box (30.0 ± 0.1 °C there): a slow formation cycle and then repeated cycles, C/10 followed by C/5 or C/5 followed by C/3, between fixed cut-offs, 3.0 to 4.3 V for NMC622 against graphite.4
  6. Charge at constant current, hold the upper cut-off until the current falls to a small limit such as C/20, and discharge at constant current; record the capacity and Coulombic efficiency of every cycle.2,5
  7. Read half-cell results with care: their excess of lithium masks the side reactions that consume lithium in a full cell. A lithium foil over 50 µm thick holds more than 10 mAh/cm2: against a positive electrode of about 3 mAh/cm2, a negative-to-positive capacity ratio above 3.3, against about 1.1 in a full cell.4,5
Setting the current: a positive electrode disc of 1.27 cm with a loading of 21.3 mg/cm2, taken here as the whole coating, of which 96 % is active material, holds 21.3×π(0.635)2×0.96=25.9 mg of active material.4 At an illustrative 170 mAh/g that is 4.40 mAh, so C/10 is 0.44 mA and C/3 is 1.47 mA.

A test is a recipe of currents, limits and rests; the cell answers with its voltage.

The cycler shows us only the voltage at the terminals. Let us look along the cell to see where that voltage goes. At rest, a cell’s voltage is the sum of the potential jumps at its two electrode-electrolyte boundaries (chapter 3). Draw a current and two things change. The ions must be driven through the electrolyte, which has a resistance, so the potential now slopes across it, cations running down the slope and anions up it: the ohmic drop, IR.6 And each boundary has to be pushed off its equilibrium before its reaction keeps pace with the current; that extra push is the electrode’s overpotential.6

For the whole cell this comes down to two lines. On discharge the terminals give the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization η; on charge the charger must supply the open-circuit voltage plus it:7

Vdis=VOC−η,Vch=VOC+η

The polarization itself has three parts: activation, from the charge-transfer reactions at the interfaces; ohmic, from the resistance of the cell’s components and contacts; and concentration, from the limits of mass transport.1

Polarization or overpotential? You will meet both. Here polarization belongs to the whole cell, η=EOCV−ET, the open-circuit voltage minus the voltage under load; overpotential, η=E−Eeq, belongs to one electrode and has its own sign. We keep them apart.1,6

Current costs voltage: a working cell gives less than its open-circuit voltage, and charging it takes more.

Now let us record the voltage while the current runs, all the way from full to empty: that record is the discharge curve. Its typical shape carries all three polarizations, and it tells us the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the health of the cell.1 The upper end of the curve has its own story. A LiCoO2 electrode, for instance, gives up only about half its lithium.8,9 In practice that is about 135 to 140 mAh/g,10,11 because its structure degrades at high states of charge.9 Consumer cells have been pushing the upper cut-off voltage higher to win more capacity.10

Push harder and the curve pays twice. The polarization grows with the current, so the two branches move apart; and the discharge reaches its cut-off sooner, before all the charge is out. That missing capacity is not lost for good: at high current the transfer of ions becomes diffusion-limited, and what the cell could not deliver comes back at a gentler rate.7

Worked example. Let us price the ohmic part on its own. A 2 Ah cell with VOC = 3.8 V and an internal resistance of 50 mΩ, run at 1C, that is 2 A, counting only the ohmic part. η=IRb=2 A×0.050 Ω=0.10 V discharge: 3.8−0.1=3.7 V; charge: 3.8+0.1=3.9 V EoutEin=3.7×2 Ah3.9×2 Ah=0.95; the other 5 % leaves as heat Double the current and the ohmic part alone doubles: 0.2 V each way, and the share returned falls to 90 %.

The gap between the charge and discharge branches is energy paid as heat on every cycle, and it widens with current.

If the three parts all add to one gap, how can we tell them apart? By their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears almost instantly, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10−4 to 10−2 s. Concentration polarization is slow: 10−2 s or longer.1 So let us switch the current off and time the recovery: the step is ohmic, the fast relaxation activation, the slow drift concentration. Interrupting the current in this way is how the polarizations are separated in practice.1

The middle clock is set by the charge-transfer reaction itself. A reaction that runs fast in both directions at equilibrium, one with a large exchange current, needs only a small overpotential to carry a given current, and battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10−2 A/cm2 are favoured.1,6 We meet the same clocks again in Lab 3, read with an alternating current.

Ohmic, activation, concentration: instant, milliseconds, tens of milliseconds and longer.

One cost of current is still to be followed: the energy lost on every cycle. The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. An irreversible part, often loosely called Joule heat, comes from the whole polarization: it is released whichever way the current flows. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction, and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage, dE/dT. If it is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as nickel-cadmium does; lead-acid has a negative coefficient and does the opposite.1 Bernardi’s energy balance writes the two side by side:12

Q˙=I(U−V)−ITdUdT

The heat is released inside the cell, at the electrode surfaces, so a cell meant for high currents has to be built to shed it.1 Because the entropic term changes sign with the current, the heat it takes in on one half of a cycle it gives back on the other; the irreversible term only ever adds up.12

You can now explain how a cycler charges and discharges a cell and what a C-rate is, why a working cell gives less than its open-circuit voltage and why charging takes more, what the three polarizations are and how fast each relaxes, how a battery’s curve differs from a capacitor’s, why some curves are flat and others slope, why a flat curve tilts at high rate and can remember its past, why fast discharges leave material unused, how slopes of the curve reveal ageing, why 99.9 % per cycle is not enough, and where the lost energy goes.

So far we have watched the cell from outside, through its voltage. To see why the curve has the shape it has, let us follow our ion inside the cell.

Go deeper: the exchange current, the Butler-Volmer equation and the Tafel line

For one electrode, the current and the overpotential η=E−Eeq are linked by the Butler-Volmer equation. Written with reduction current positive, it reads6

i=i0[exp(−αfη)−exp((1−α)fη)],f=FRT

with α the transfer coefficient (between 0.3 and 0.7 in most cases) and i0 the exchange current. It holds while the current stays below about 10 % of the limiting current, so that mass transport does not interfere.6 Near equilibrium the exponentials can be expanded and the current is simply proportional to η: the electrode behaves like a resistor, the charge-transfer resistance Rct=RT/(Fi0) for a one-electron reaction. A large exchange current means a small Rct.6

Far from equilibrium one exponential wins and η grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation η=a+blogi, which holds once |η| exceeds about 118 mV at 25 °C; the same empirical form is quoted for batteries.1,6 In the battery literature it is written with oxidation current positive (the printed equation lost a bracket and a sign); the physics is the same with the roles of α and 1−α swapped.1,6

One caution about the heat equation. It takes the open-circuit voltage U from a slow measurement and dU/dT from small temperature steps, and in one set of measurements it estimated heat reasonably during continuous discharge but overestimated it during pulsed discharge, by up to 26 % for a LiFePO4 cell and 49 % for an NMC cell.12

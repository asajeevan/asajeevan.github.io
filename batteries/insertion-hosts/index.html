Chapter 7

Insertion hosts: intercalation, alloying and conversion reactions

When a cell is charged, where is the lithium stored?

Graphite: sheets, and lithium by stages

So far we have described each host in a sentence. Let us now look at them atom by atom, beginning with graphite. Graphite is a stack of graphene sheets: carbon atoms joined in hexagonal rings, the sheets held to one another by weak van der Waals forces and π–π interactions, 0.335 nm apart; in natural graphite they are mostly stacked in the order A, B, A, B.1 The lithium goes into the gaps between the sheets, at most one lithium for every six carbons, LiC6.2 The edges of the sheets are the favourable sites for lithium to go in, and it diffuses faster there; the flat faces, the basal planes, are less reactive.1

The lithium does not fill all the gaps at once. It fills some gaps and leaves the others empty, forming stages: the stage number is the number of graphene sheets between two lithium layers. As the cell charges, the graphite passes from stage 4 through stage 3 to stage 2, LiC12, and finally stage 1, LiC6, with lithium in every gap. Stages 2 and 3 are stable phases that affect the equilibrium potential and the diffusion of lithium. Each change of stage is favoured thermodynamically but held back kinetically, the last one, from LiC12 to LiC6, most of all. In the Rüdorff-Hofmann picture each stage is made of complete lithium layers, as the figure draws them; in the Daumas-Hérold picture the guest forms islands within the layers instead. Full lithiation swells the graphite by about 10 %, mostly by the sheets moving apart, not by the sheets stretching.1

LiCoO2: lithium between oxide sheets

Now we cross to the other side. LiCoO2 is a layered compound too: cobalt surrounded by oxygen in CoO2 sheets, with the lithium in the layer between two of them. The idea came from sodium, which Hagenmuller and co-workers had put between such sheets in the 1970s; in 1979 and 1980 Goodenough’s group made the lithium compound, at twice the voltage of TiS2.3 As we saw, only about half of its lithium is taken out on charge.2,4

In both hosts, then, the sheets are what makes the cell work. Lithium comes and goes; the framework of carbon or of cobalt oxide stays, so the electrodes keep their structure and shape and the reaction can run back and forth many times.5

Two layered solids and one guest: charging moves lithium from between the oxide sheets to between the carbon sheets, and discharging moves it back.

Our ion is now inside a host, protected by the SEI where it needs one. How does it move once it is in? The host decides. In the layered oxides lithium travels within two-dimensional planes; the spinel LiMn2O4 offers a three-dimensional network; in olivine LiFePO4 the paths are one-dimensional channels.2

Inside the solid the ion moves by diffusion, down its concentration gradient, as Fick’s first law describes; in a time t it typically gets about (2Dt)1/2 from where it started, where D is its diffusion coefficient.6 Turned around, emptying a particle of size L takes a time of order L2/2D: halve the particle and it empties four times faster. We return to this in chapter 11, when the particles shrink to nanometres.

The families on the map differ in what happens to the material when lithium enters it, and there are three ways for that to go. In intercalation, lithium slips between the layers of a host that keeps its structure, as in graphite, Li+ + e− + xC ⇌ LiCx, or in titanium oxides; that is why these materials last for many cycles. In alloying, lithium forms an alloy with an element such as silicon, germanium or tin, xLi+ + xe− + M ⇌ LixM. In conversion, a compound is taken apart into metal and a lithium compound and rebuilt on the way back. Alloys and conversion materials tend to break into smaller particles as they cycle.7

All three pay a price on the very first charge: they lose a large part of it, mostly to the SEI, which takes lithium from the positive electrode and lowers the first-cycle Coulombic efficiency. Alloys and conversion materials also show a large gap between the voltage at which lithium goes in and the voltage at which it comes out, which lowers the energy a full cell can deliver.7

You can now explain where the lithium sits in graphite and in LiCoO2, why the cell is called a rocking chair, why a new cell loses lithium on its first charge and what it gets in return, how the host shapes the ion’s path, and how the cell came together between 1972 and 1996.

On every trip between the two hosts, our ion had to cross something to get there.

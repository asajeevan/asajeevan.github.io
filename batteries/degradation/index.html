Chapter 16

Degradation: loss of lithium inventory, loss of active material and resistance rise

Why does my battery hold less after a few years?

Where we are. In chapter 15 we pushed both electrodes beyond today’s materials, towards lithium metal, nickel-rich oxides and organic molecules, and met materials that fail fast and loudly. A well-made lithium-ion cell fails slowly and quietly: a little capacity gone each week, whether it is used or not. In this module we ask where that capacity goes: we name the thieves and see how each one works.

Let us start from the symptom: the cell holds less than it did. Capacity is lost in two ways. Some is only hidden: at a high current the cell reaches its cut-off early, and the capacity comes back at a gentler rate. The rest is gone for good, to changes in the volume of the electrodes, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes; the cycle life ends when the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value.1 In lithium-ion cells, poor lifetimes are rooted mainly in side reactions at the electrode-electrolyte interface.2

Sorted by what they take, these causes are the three thieves of figure 10.1. Side reactions lock up lithium that could have carried charge. Cracking, contact loss and dissolution remove active material, the room the lithium needs. And thicker interfaces raise the resistance, so that at a given current the cell runs out of voltage before it runs out of charge.

A battery rarely dies of one cause; diagnosing it means separating the causes.

Ageing leads to a practical question: how long does a cell last? Two numbers summarize a cell’s life. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in on charge, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value.1

CE=100×QdisQch %

In 2004 a commercial rechargeable cell was expected to survive at least 300 full cycles with less than 20 % loss; most needed three to eight hours to charge while users wanted a quarter of an hour, and fast charging shortens cycle life.3 LiFePO4, with a much longer cycle life and calendar life than most NMC cells, is now used for grid storage and shorter-range electric vehicles.4 The fast-charging goal is 80 % of the charge in under 15 minutes.5

Not every loss is permanent. Capacity hidden by a high rate comes back at a low one; what does not come back is lost to changes in the electrodes’ volume, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes.1 The first charge of a lithium-ion cell is a special case, a loss paid once (chapter 10).1

Worked example. Let us see what the 300-cycle benchmark asks of each cycle. How good must the Coulombic efficiency be for a cell to keep 80 % of its capacity after 300 cycles? Take the simplest case, in which every bit of charge that does not come back is lost for good. after 1 cycle: CE; after 2 cycles: CE×CE=CE2; after N cycles: CEN the goal: CE300=0.80 take the 300th root: CE=0.801/300=0.99926, that is 99.93 % the other way round, at 99.9 %: 0.999N=0.80⇒N=ln0.80ln0.999=−0.223−0.001=223 cycles So losing just one coulomb in a thousand on every cycle uses up the 20 % allowance after 223 cycles, well short of 300.

Lifetime is set by tiny losses repeated hundreds of times.

Let us follow the first thief, the one that takes lithium. The SEI of chapter 10 does not stop at the first charge. It keeps growing, more slowly, adding to the interfacial impedance as it evolves.1 Its thickness follows a power law in time, L ∝ tb, and so does the capacity lost to it: b = 1/2 when the supply through the layer is limited by diffusion, the usual assumption; b = 1 when the reaction itself, or migration during charging, is the bottleneck; b = 0 for migration during discharge. It grows fastest when the cell sits at a high state of charge and is charged fast.6 Too thick a layer means lost capacity and higher impedance at once.7

How much does it matter? In storage, most of the capacity a lithium-ion cell loses goes into the SEI, and in a well-engineered cell more than half of all capacity loss can be traced to its growth.8,9 The layer grows while the graphite is being filled, not while it is being emptied,8 and in a charged cell it keeps growing at rest, more slowly; in the standard models only a fully discharged cell stops feeding it.10 On silicon the picture changes: the large volume change keeps cracking the SEI, and the growth becomes linear in time.8

Worked example. What does the square-root law mean over the years of a cell’s life? Take an illustrative start: suppose the SEI costs 2 % of the capacity in the first month. after 4 months: 2 % × √4 = 4 % after 1 year: 2 % × √12 = 6.9 %; after 4 years: 2 % × √48 = 13.9 % The loss slows down, but it never stops. With b = 1 the same start would have cost 24 % in a year.6

So far we have watched one thief at a time. They can also work together. In a cell with a spinel LiMn2O4 positive electrode, manganese dissolves as Mn(II), crosses the electrolyte and poisons the SEI on the negative electrode.1 The positive electrode loses active material; the negative electrode’s protection weakens, and with it more lithium is lost. Particles that crack and lose contact, as alloys do (figure 7.3), are the other route to losing active material.2

Cells also age when nothing is connected, on the shelf. Self-discharge, the slow loss of charge with nothing connected, differs enormously between chemistries: a nickel-metal hydride cell can lose up to 30 % a month, while a lithium-MnO2 primary cell keeps 90 % after eight years. It rises with temperature.3 Overcharging or overdischarging a cell is another way to age it fast: both drive irreversible reactions that form new compounds and cost capacity.3

You can now explain the difference between capacity that is hidden and capacity that is lost, the three thieves and how they cooperate, why the SEI keeps costing lithium and how fast, and why a cell ages on the shelf as well as in use.

To catch a thief we need a witness. Next we see how these losses are measured, and how the measurements tell one thief from another.

Go deeper: how sure is the square-root law?

The square-root law is the usual reading of storage data, and it is what a growth limited by something diffusing through the layer predicts.6,8 Which something is still debated: solvent through the pores, electrons through the compact inner layer, or neutral lithium atoms; storage tests at different states of charge favour the last.8 A recent comparison adds two cautions: few of the √t fits in the literature are statistically justified, and none of the standard models reproduces how much larger the loss in the first cycle is than in the later ones.10 The shape of a fade curve alone cannot prove a mechanism.8

Go deeper: paying back the first-charge loss

The lithium a new cell locks into its SEI on the first charge is gone before the cell is ever used, and in conversion electrodes the first-cycle loss reaches about 30 %. Can we pay it back? One remedy, proposed by Armand, is sacrificial salts: lithium salts with an oxidizable anion mixed into the positive electrode. On the first charge the anion is oxidized and gives up its lithium, which pays for the SEI; what is left of the salt leaves pores behind that help the electrolyte wet the electrode. The salt is chosen by its oxidation potential to suit the cell, and the idea extends to sodium and potassium cells.7

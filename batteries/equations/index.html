Chapter 0 · Electrostatics

Everything below is the equation behind a figure above, in the form Bard, Faulkner and White give it. Quantities: q, q′ charges (C); r distance (m); ε0 = 8.854 × 10−12 C2 N−1 m−2; e = 1.602 × 10−19 C; F = 96 485.3 C/mol.

F = qq′ / (4πε0r2)Coulomb’s law; positive means repulsion (figure 0.1)

E = F / q′ = q / (4πε0r2)the field of a point charge: force per unit charge, pointing away from a positive q

φ(r) = −∫∞r E·dl = q / (4πε0r)the potential: work per unit charge from infinity, path-independent (figure 0.2)

φ(B) − φ(A) = −∫AB E·dla potential difference is the field integrated along any path; in one dimension, E = −dφ/dx: the field is the slope of the potential

qenclosed = ε0 ∮ E·dSGauss’s law; with E = 0 inside a conductor at rest, the enclosed charge is zero and the excess charge sits on the surface

E = V / lthe uniform field between two parallel plates a distance l apart (figure 0.3)

ΔU = q Δφenergy change of a charge moved across Δφ; for q = e and Δφ = 1 V, ΔU = 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J

F = NA e = 96 485.3 C/molthe Faraday constant: one electron-volt per electron is F × 1 V = 96.5 kJ per mole

i = dQ/dtcurrent, in amperes = coulombs per second (figure 0.4); at 1 A, 1/e = 6.24 × 1018 electrons pass each second

V = IR, R = ρl/A, G = 1/R = κA/lOhm’s law; resistance grows with length and falls with cross-section; ρ resistivity, κ conductivity

P = IVpower delivered, in watts

|z|eE = 6πηrv ⇒ u = |z|e / (6πηr), v = uEan ion of charge number z in a liquid of viscosity η: the electric force balances the Stokes drag at the terminal velocity v; u is the mobility (figure 0.5)

κ = F Σj |zj| uj Cj, tj = |zj|ujCj / Σk |zk|ukCkthe conductivity of an electrolyte adds up every ion’s charge, mobility and concentration; tj, the transference number, is the share of the current that ion j carries

Jj = −Dj ∇Cj − (zjF/RT) Dj Cj ∇φthe Nernst–Planck equation: an ion’s flux has a diffusion part (down the concentration gradient) and a migration part (along the field); with Dj = ujRT/(|zj|F) the two pictures agree. The modules on ion transport, still to come, are built on it.

Chapter 1 · Galvanic cells

Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−  Cu2+ + 2e− → Cuthe two half-reactions; neither can run alone

Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cuthe cell reaction, their sum; n = 2 electrons per zinc atom

Ecell = E°(Cu2+/Cu) − E°(Zn2+/Zn) = 0.340 − (−0.763) = 1.103 Vopen-circuit voltage at unit activities, from the standard potentials against the hydrogen electrode (figure 1.1)

E = φCu′ − φCu = Σ Δφinterfacesthe voltage between two like leads is the sum of the potential steps met along the path through the cell: the two metal|liquid jumps plus the metal|metal contact potentials; only the sum is measurable (the strip under figure 1.1)

ΔG = −nFE = −2 × 96 485 × 1.103 = −213 kJ/molthe electrical work a mole of the reaction can do (chapter 3 derives it)

Vseries = Σ Vi, Qseries = Qcells in a chain: potential differences along a path add; the same current threads every cell, so each coulomb passes all of them (figure 1.3)

Vparallel = V, I = Σ Ii, Qparallel = Σ Qicells side by side: one potential difference between the joined ends; the current, and therefore the charge, is shared

Chapter 2 · Cell anatomy

Rion = l / (κA)the ionic resistance of the electrolyte layer between the electrodes: thin (small l) and wide (large A) is the whole design brief of figure 2.2. Winter and Brodd give κ of order 1 S/cm for aqueous and 10−2 to 10−3 S/cm for the organic electrolytes of lithium cells.

κeff = κ εα, α ≈ 1.5inside a porous electrode only the pore fraction ε (about 0.3, figure 2.4) carries ions, along winding paths; the Bruggeman relation gives the effective conductivity of the electrolyte in the pores. Boz and co-workers quote α = 1.5, and 1.53 measured for LiFePO4 electrodes.

Deff = D ε/τ, τ = ε1−αthe same correction for diffusion, with τ the tortuosity of the pore network

With ε = 0.3 and α = 1.5, κeff = 0.31.5 κ = 0.16 κ: the electrolyte inside a coating conducts about a sixth as well as the free liquid. The module on composite electrodes, still to come, builds on this.

Chapter 3 · Cell voltage

μ̄j = μj + zjFφthe electrochemical potential of species j: its chemical potential plus its charge times the local potential (per mole)

μj = μ°j + RT ln ajthe chemical potential in terms of the activity aj (roughly, concentration relative to a standard state)

μ̄jα = μ̄jβequilibrium of species j between phases α and β; the condition that stops the charge transfer described in figure 3.3

μ̄e = μ°e − Fφ, EF = μ̄e / NAelectrons in a metal (no activity term: their concentration never changes appreciably); the Fermi energy is the electrochemical potential of the electrons, per electron (figure 3.2). Making φ more negative by 1 V raises EF by 1 eV.

φZn − φCu = (μ°eZn − μ°eCu) / Ftwo metals in contact exchange electrons until μ̄e is equal: the contact potential

−nFE = n(μ̄eCu′ − μ̄eCu)write the equilibrium of the whole cell reaction in electrochemical potentials; the φ terms cancel everywhere except at the two copper leads, which is why only the whole-cell voltage is measurable

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln (aproducts / areactants)expanding the chemical potentials gives the Nernst equation; at 25 °C, RT/F = 25.7 mV, so a tenfold change in an activity ratio shifts a one-electron E by 59 mV

VOC = (μA − μC) / ethe same statement per electron, in Goodenough and Park’s notation: the open-circuit voltage is the difference in electron energy of the two electrodes (figures 3.4 and 3.5)

E = E° − 0.059 (q/n) pHa half-reaction with q protons per n electrons shifts 59 mV per pH unit; for water, E = 0 − 0.059 pH (hydrogen) and E = 1.229 − 0.059 pH (oxygen), so the window is 1.229 V wide at every pH and sits at 3.045 to 4.274 V versus lithium at pH 0 (figure 3.4)

ΔG = −nFE, ΔS = nF(∂E/∂T)P, ΔH = ΔG + TΔSthe thermodynamics of the cell reaction (figure 3.7); the temperature coefficient of the voltage gives the entropy change, which a later module turns into reversible heat

q = C·E, CH = εε0/dthe double layer as a capacitor (figure 3.3); in the Helmholtz picture two sheets of charge a distance d apart in a medium of dielectric constant ε; measured values 10 to 40 µF/cm2

Chapter 4 · Capacity and energy

Q = nFN, m = ItM / (nF)Faraday’s law: the charge for N moles of reactant that give n electrons each; turned round, the mass transformed by a current I for a time t

Qspec = nF / (3.6 M) mAh/gthe theoretical specific capacity of a material of molar mass M (g/mol); 3.6 converts coulombs per gram to milliampere-hours per gram (figure 4.1). Graphite as C6: 372; LiFePO4: 170; lithium metal: 3861.

energy = ∫0Δt IV dt = ∫0Q V(q) dq = 〈V〉 Qthe energy delivered is the area under the discharge curve, or the average voltage times the capacity (figure 4.2)

P = IV, specific energy = energy / mass, specific power = P / massthe two axes of the Ragone plot (figure 4.5), in Wh/kg and W/kg

ηCoulombic = 100 × Qdis / Qchthe share of the charge put in on charging that comes back on discharge; a later module follows it cycle by cycle

ηenergy = 100 × ∫Vdis dq / ∫Vch dqthe share of the energy that comes back; lower than the Coulombic efficiency because the charge branch sits above the discharge branch

Chapter 7 · Insertion hosts

LixC6, 0 ≤ x ≤ 1; Li1−yCoO2, y ≤ 0.5 in practicehow much lithium each host holds: up to one per six carbons, and about half the lithium of LiCoO2 used in practice

2 EC + 2 Li+ + 2 e− → Li2EDC + C2H4↑the main SEI-forming reduction of ethylene carbonate, from about 0.8 V vs Li/Li+; the ethylene is among the gases let out during formation

LiCoO2 + C6 ⇌ Li1−xCoO2 + LixC6the cell reaction, charge to the right: the carbon is made empty because LixC6 is very reactive, and LiCoO2 is the only source of lithium

N = Q/e = nNA, n = Q/Fthe number of ions, and of moles, that carry a charge Q (worked example)

J = −D ∂C/∂x, Δ = (2Dt)1/2, t ≈ L2/2DFick’s first law; the diffusion length in a time t; and, turned round, the order of magnitude of the time to cross a particle of size L

eVOC = μA − μC, stable if μA < LUMO and μC > HOMOthe open-circuit voltage from the two electrodes’ electrochemical potentials, and the condition for the electrolyte to survive without a passivating layer

Chapter 8 · Liquid electrolytes

Chapter 18 · Recycling

E = Q ⟨V⟩the energy a short circuit can turn into heat: all the charge stored times the average voltage (chapter 4); a 3 Ah cell at 3.7 V holds 11.1 Wh, that is 40 kJ

Q̇ = I(U − V) − I T dU/dTthe heat a cell makes in normal use (chapter 5, figure 5.5); in runaway the side reactions add their own heat on top

LSEI ∝ tbthe ageing law of chapter 16 behind the advice to avoid long periods at full charge

Chapter 8

Liquid electrolytes: formulation, salt dissociation and Li+ solvation

What is the liquid inside, and why these molecules?

Where we are. Module 6 put our ion in its two hosts and sent it from one to the other. Between them it crosses the electrolyte, the part of the cell that must carry ions and stop electrons. What is that medium, and how does an ion get through it? We start from nothing: what the liquid in a lithium-ion cell is made of, how its molecules sit around each ion, how an ion moves through it, what the electric field inside it looks like, and how the arrangement changes, layer by layer, as we approach an electrode. Then we compare the liquid with polymers and ceramics, and see why the anion matters as much as the lithium.

Let us begin with the cast. Everything in this module is made of four kinds of object, so we meet them first. A molecule is a group of atoms held together by chemical bonds; the figure draws each atom as a ball and each bond as a stick. The two solvents of a lithium-ion cell are both carbonates, built round a carbon atom bonded to three oxygen atoms. In ethylene carbonate, EC, the carbonate closes into a ring; in dimethyl carbonate, DMC, it stays an open chain: EC is a cyclic carbonate and DMC a linear one.1

In both, one oxygen is double-bonded to the carbon and sticks out of the molecule: the carbonyl oxygen. A lithium ion is held by a solvent through the spare pairs of electrons on such a donor atom.2 The salt brings the other two objects: the lithium ion, Li+, a lithium atom that has lost one electron, and the hexafluorophosphate anion, PF6−, six fluorine atoms around a phosphorus atom, carrying one negative charge.2

Electron clouds and charge

A molecule is not a set of hard balls. Its electrons form a cloud, densest at the atoms and thinning out over a fraction of a nanometre, and the figure shows that cloud as calculated for this page. The calculation also shows where each molecule is slightly negative and where slightly positive. EC has a negative end, at the carbonyl oxygen, and a positive end, at its CH2 groups: a strongly polar molecule, with a computed dipole moment of about 5 debye. In DMC the two halves almost cancel, about 0.3 debye. The same contrast shows up in the two liquids’ dielectric constants, 90 for EC against 3.1 for DMC, in the next figure, and simulations likewise treat EC as the polar solvent and DMC as the less polar one.1,3

Four objects to recognize: EC, a ring; DMC, a chain; PF6−, a ball of fluorine; and Li+, the smallest of all.

The recipe

Now we put the cast together. An electrolyte has one job that defines it: to provide purely ionic conduction between the two electrodes.4 It is a solution: a salt dissolved in a liquid, the solvent. Most commercial lithium-ion cells use roughly 1 mol of the salt lithium hexafluorophosphate, LiPF6, per litre of a mixture of carbonate solvents: a cyclic one such as ethylene carbonate (EC) or propylene carbonate (PC), and a linear one such as dimethyl carbonate (DMC), ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) or diethyl carbonate (DEC).1

Water, the obvious solvent, is out: it would be split at the voltage of a lithium cell (module 3).5 Water-based electrolytes conduct best, about 1 S/cm; the organic liquids reach 10−2 to 10−3 S/cm, because they dissolve salts less well and let the ions pair up.4

Why two solvents

Why two solvents? Because each does one job well and the other badly. A good solvent needs a high dielectric constant (relative permittivity), to pull the salt apart into separate ions, and a high fluidity, so that the ions can move.1 EC has a relative permittivity of 90 but is thick, 1.9 mPa s, and melts at about 36 °C, so on its own it is a solid at room temperature. DMC flows easily, 0.59 mPa s, but its relative permittivity is only 3.1.1

Mixed, they stay liquid far lower: a mixture of carbonates has a eutectic, the composition with the lowest melting point, at −8.6 °C for EC with DMC and −64.7 °C for EC with DMC and EMC (the solvents alone, without salt), and blends near the eutectic are preferred to work in the cold. EC is usually kept below 50 % by volume, preferably 30 %, because more of it comes out as a solid at low temperature.1

A pinch of additives

Real electrolytes also carry minute amounts of additives, each with a job: some decompose on purpose to build a thin, controlled SEI on the graphite (module 6), others protect the positive electrode or stop overcharge (module 12); no single additive can do everything, and finding the right combination is now central to electrolyte work.6

Why LiPF6

Why LiPF6? It is used almost exclusively in commercial lithium-ion cells because it has shown the best balance of the properties a salt needs: its solutions are among the most conductive known, it forms a stable surface on the aluminium current collector at high potential, and with carbonate solvents it forms a stable SEI on graphite (module 6). Its weakness is its P–F bond, which is easily broken: the salt reacts readily with water and is not very stable to heat, and hydrofluoric acid, HF, in LiPF6 electrolytes is one of the principal concerns about using it.2 It became the standard only when carbon replaced lithium metal and the solvents were optimized for the lithium-ion cells Sony introduced in 1991.2

Worked example. Let us count the crowd: 1 mol of LiPF6 dissolved in 1 litre of EC : DMC 3 : 7 by volume (the salt’s own volume neglected). EC: 300 cm3 × 1.32 g/cm3 ÷ 88.1 g/mol = 4.5 mol DMC: 700 cm3 × 1.06 g/cm3 ÷ 90.1 g/mol = 8.2 mol so for each Li+ and each PF6−: about 13 solvent molecules each Li+ has a cube of liquid about (1 L ÷ 6.02 × 1023)^1/3 = 1.2 nm on a side to itself, on average A lithium ion is never more than a few molecules from the next one, and most of what surrounds it is solvent.1,5

The liquid in a lithium-ion cell is mostly solvent: a dozen or so carbonate molecules for every lithium ion, two kinds of solvent because neither does both jobs.

Choosing a salt

We have taken one salt and two solvents as given. Why these, and not others? Let us line up the alternatives, starting with the salt. A salt for a lithium-ion electrolyte has a list of jobs. It must give a conductive solution, stay stable within the cell’s voltage window and at high temperature, help form a good SEI, protect the aluminium current collector of the positive electrode above about 3.6 V vs Li, and resist water. It should also be cheap and not toxic. Failing any one of these rules a salt out of practical use, and every one of them depends on the rest of the recipe.2

Put the candidates side by side and no salt wins everywhere. LiClO4 conducts well and is stable, but its anion is a strong oxidant, which makes the salt a potential explosive. LiAsF6 behaves much like LiPF6, but reduction could turn its arsenic into highly toxic forms. LiBF4 resists water and heat better than LiPF6 but conducts significantly less.2

LiTFSI and lithium triflate shrug off water and heat, but in ordinary dilute solutions they corrode the aluminium foil at high potential. In PC:DME the aluminium repassivation potential, above which corrosion pits keep growing, is 3.7 V vs Li with LiTFSI, against above 5 V with LiPF6.2 So LiPF6, with its known weaknesses, keeps its place in the recipe of figure 7.2.2

Choosing the solvents

The solvents tell the same story. The five carbonates of commercial electrolytes split into two families.1 The cyclic ones, EC and PC, have relative permittivities of 65 to 90 and flash points above 130 °C, but they are viscous, and EC is a solid below 36 °C. The linear ones, DMC, EMC and DEC, flow two and a half to four times more easily and, apart from DMC, stay liquid far below 0 °C. But their permittivities are only about 3, and they flash at 17 to 25 °C, near room temperature.1

All five resist oxidation on glassy carbon to above 6 V vs Li. A window measured on inert carbon, however, is not a clear guide to stability against real electrode materials.1,2

Every salt and every solvent fails at something; a commercial electrolyte is the mixture whose weaknesses matter least.

We have the recipe; now let us watch the salt go into the liquid. What does it mean for a salt to dissolve? Solid LiPF6 is made of Li+ cations and PF6− anions, which attract each other because their charges are opposite (module 0). In a carbonate solvent the Li+ ions are pulled out of it by the solvent molecules: each solvent molecule has an oxygen atom with spare pairs of electrons (in a carbonate, the carbonyl oxygen), and it binds to the lithium through them. The anions are, in general, left bare, because these solvents have no acidic hydrogen to hold them: in water an anion is held by hydrogen bonds, in a carbonate it is not. The anions can bind to Li+ in the same way, so solvent and anion compete for the places around each lithium ion, and that competition decides what is actually present in the liquid.2

And the anion?

So does a PF6− anion get a shell of its own? Not in the way Li+ does. Solvent molecules do come close to it, and simulations treat a PF6− next to an EC molecule as a complex, but nothing binds there the way the oxygens bind to lithium, and the neighbours keep changing. In the figure they are drawn faint, with their slightly positive CH ends (blue in figure 7.1) towards the anion.2,3

Dissolving is a tug of war for the lithium ion, and in a good solvent the solvent wins.

The shell

Let us follow one lithium ion out of the crystal and look at what it has gathered. How many solvent molecules fit round it? Few. Simulations find about four solvent molecules bound strongly around a lithium ion, arranged as a tetrahedron, and about 4.2 to 4.4 partners, solvent and anions together, in EC : DMC and EC : PC with LiPF6; neutron diffraction on LiPF6 in PC finds 4.5 PC molecules on average, each oxygen 0.204 nm from the lithium.3

This group is the ion’s first solvation shell. In the liquid, simulations find EC and DMC in it about equally often, even though an isolated lithium ion in the gas phase prefers EC.3 The shell is not a fixed object: a given EC molecule stays in it for about half a nanosecond, an anion partner for 2 to 3 ns.3

Pairs and clusters

When an anion wins a place, the two ions form a contact ion pair; when solvent stays between them, they are a solvent-separated ion pair; larger clusters are aggregates.2,3 The anion matters: the tendency to pair up rises in the order LiAsF6 and LiPF6, then LiTFSI, LiClO4, LiBF4 and lithium triflate.2

The solvent matters too: in simulations of EC : DMC the fraction of free ions rises nearly linearly with the share of EC; and measured with LiTFSI, the conductivity reaches 62 % of what fully independent ions would give in PC, but only 11 % in DMC. Not every cluster is lost to conduction: charged clusters carry current too.3

A lithium ion never travels alone: it carries a shell of about four solvent molecules, and sometimes an anion.

You can now explain what the liquid in a lithium-ion cell is made of and why it mixes two solvents, what a solvation shell is and how ions pair up, how an ion wanders and drifts and how weak the field in the bulk is, how the liquid changes layer by layer towards an electrode, how a liquid, a polymer and a ceramic each carry a lithium ion, why dry-polymer cells run warm, what the transference number is and why a low one leaves a salt gradient, and why a solid electrolyte needs more than a high conductivity.

Our ion has now crossed the electrolyte. On the far side, it has to find a particle.

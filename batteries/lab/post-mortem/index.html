Lab handbook · Lab 5

Post-mortem and electrode characterization

When the outside of a cell is not enough, what then?

When the outside is not enough

Everything so far was measured from outside, across both electrodes at once, and that is its limit. Telling the two electrodes apart needs a reference electrode inside the cell, placed where it does not disturb the current, which is still hard to build and not a production solution.1,2 The alternative is to open the cell. Before that come non-destructive checks: inspection, X-ray tomography, capacity, cyclic voltammetry and differential capacity. Then the cell is discharged slowly and opened in an argon glove box; its electrodes can be rebuilt into half cells or symmetric cells of two identical electrodes, whose spectra show which electrode carries which arc, and examined by microscopy and spectroscopy. A fresh cell treated the same way is the baseline.2

The full picture often needs this tear-down, with Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy, NMR, electron microscopy and X-ray absorption, while accelerating-rate calorimetry finds the conditions for thermal runaway.3 Plastic lithium-ion cells opened the way to watching the electrodes while they work,4 and an external pressure sensor can already follow the SEI growing through the swelling it causes.1

The cell itself has to be chosen too. The standard way to test a new positive electrode is a half-cell against lithium metal,5 but half-cells can fail to predict how the same electrode behaves in a full cell, and small assembly errors change the result;6 in GITT, the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage can pass for slow diffusion.7

You can now explain why a pulse resistance needs its timescale; how GITT turns two voltage changes into a diffusion coefficient, and the four checks that make it trustworthy (pulse length, IR drop, rest, particle size); what impedance is, and how to read Nyquist and Bode plots of a real cell from left to right; why the measurement must be small, steady, four-terminal and Kramers–Kronig valid; why a good circuit fit is not proof; and what DRT, nonlinear impedance and machine learning add.

We can now find out from outside what has gone wrong in a cell, and open it when the outside is not enough. The last module takes up what is left: how to keep the energy a cell stores from coming out all at once, and what becomes of the cell when it is truly finished.

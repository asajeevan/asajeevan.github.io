Post-mortem and electrode characterization

When the outside of a cell is not enough, what then?

When the outside is not enough

Everything so far was measured from outside, across both electrodes at once, and that is its limit. Telling the two electrodes apart needs a reference electrode inside the cell, placed where it does not disturb the current, which is still hard to build and not a production solution.1,2 The alternative is to open the cell. Before that come non-destructive checks: inspection, X-ray tomography, capacity, cyclic voltammetry and differential capacity. Then the cell is discharged slowly and opened in an argon glove box; its electrodes can be rebuilt into half cells or symmetric cells of two identical electrodes, whose spectra show which electrode carries which arc, and examined by microscopy and spectroscopy. A fresh cell treated the same way is the baseline.2

The full picture often needs this tear-down, with Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy, NMR, electron microscopy and X-ray absorption, while accelerating-rate calorimetry finds the conditions for thermal runaway.3 Plastic lithium-ion cells opened the way to watching the electrodes while they work,4 and an external pressure sensor can already follow the SEI growing through the swelling it causes.1

The cell itself has to be chosen too. The standard way to test a new positive electrode is a half-cell against lithium metal,5 but half-cells can fail to predict how the same electrode behaves in a full cell, and small assembly errors change the result;6 in GITT, the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage can pass for slow diffusion.7

The order of a post-mortem

A post-mortem is a sequence, and the sequence protects the evidence. First everything that can be measured from outside: the capacity and its history, the differential curves of L1.8, which already separate lost lithium from lost material and flag plating, and the impedance; a fresh cell measured the same way is the baseline, and once a cell is opened none of this can be repeated.2 Then the cell is discharged to its lower cut-off, slowly, with a constant-voltage hold, so that the positive electrode is as full of lithium as it can be: in one study, C/2 to 2.5 V and a hold to C/50.8 It is opened in an argon glovebox with oxygen and water at or below 1 ppm, a pouch along its long side with ceramic scissors and a scalpel, without touching the stack.8

Then the sampling, which is where most post-mortems go wrong. A large cell does not age as one piece: in a 16 Ah pouch cell of 38 negative and 37 positive sheets, samples were cut from the beginning, middle and end of the stack and from the edge and centre of each sheet, and every result was labelled with its position;8 a 64 Ah cell was sampled layer by layer, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 from the outside, and at the middle and the tab side of each.9 The sampling is part of the result: a number without its position is a number from somewhere.

Washing is a choice. A dip in dimethyl carbonate and drying under vacuum removes the electrolyte residue that would otherwise dominate a surface spectrum, but surface spectroscopy is also done on unwashed samples, and electrodes carrying bulky deposits are left unwashed, because the deposits would go with the solvent.8,10 Samples for microscopy travel in an inert transfer chamber or a sealed pouch; the one that was prepared in air showed oxygen where the plated lithium had reacted with it.8,9

Outside first, discharged, in argon, mapped, and washed only on purpose: the order is the method.

Harvested electrodes, cycled again

The most direct test of a harvested electrode is to make it work again. Discs are punched from the sampled positions, one side of the coating is removed where the sheet was coated on both, and each disc is built into a coin cell against lithium, in the same glovebox and with a fresh electrolyte, then cycled slowly.8 The capacity it delivers, compared with a disc from a fresh cell, is the residual capacity of that piece of electrode; the rate test that follows says whether the material is intact.8

L6.3 shows what came out of two 16 Ah LiFePO4 | graphite pouch cells cycled at 5 °C, one charged at 0.3C and one at 2C, until they had lost a fifth or more of their capacity. In cell A, the centre of the middle sheet still delivered 89 % of the pristine capacity, the edge of the first sheet 71 %, and its centre 63 %: a spread of a factor of three in one cell.8 After the first discharge, all the discs performed alike in the rate test, so the loss of active material was negligible against the loss of lithium inventory; the lithium had been consumed, unevenly, and the authors trace the pattern mainly to the pressure, which is highest in the middle of the stack and at the centre of each sheet.8 In cell B, charged faster, the spread was smaller, which they attribute to the higher current warming the cell above 5 °C.8

One cell, one electrode, three different states of health, depending on where the disc was punched.

Mechanics first: the peel test

Before chemistry, mechanics. A strip of the coating is pulled off its current collector at a right angle, slowly, and the force per width is the adhesion; it costs nothing and it decides which samples can be handled. The positive coating of a commercial LiFePO4 electrode holds with several hundred newtons per metre, pristine or aged; the graphite coating with a few, and formation alone, the first cycles in which the SEI forms and the graphite swells, cut it from 5.4 to 2.2 N/m.8 In the aged cells the positive coating stayed above 330 N/m everywhere, while the negative was so loose in places that half cells could not be built from it.8

A coating that comes away is more than a handling problem. The material that no longer touches the collector no longer counts, and its lithium no longer cycles (chapter 11); in one cell the separator carried dark patches where the graphite coating had detached, visible before any instrument was switched on.8 Thickness is measured at the same time, because a swollen electrode is one whose pores have filled with reaction products or whose particles have cracked.8

Inspection, microscopy, and the limits of the harvested disc

The eye comes first. Three 18650 LiFePO4 | graphite cells driven at 50 °C with a vehicle profile until they held 30 to 40 % of their capacity were opened in argon and photographed before anything else: the positive sheet showed no deposits, cracks or exfoliation, the negative showed all three, concentrated at the two ends of the wound roll, with deposits over 11 % of its area.10 The electron microscope then sorted the surface into regions: the dark grey coating that looked like the fresh one; light grey areas of small spheres whose elemental map read about 60 % fluorine, taken as LiF; and bulky gold deposits that were mainly oxygen and hid the graphite beneath.10 The LiFePO4 looked and analysed as it had when new.10

Then the discs. Harvested LiFePO4 discs in coin cells delivered their full capacity; harvested graphite discs had lost 9 % on average.10 But the whole cell’s negative electrode, estimated from outside in a companion study, had lost about 30 %, and the authors say why: the material under the bulky deposits peeled off the copper and could not be punched, so the discs that reached a coin cell were the good ones.10 The harvested-disc test measures what survives harvesting, and a post-mortem number carries its sampling with it; here the gap between 9 and 30 % is itself the finding, that a third of the negative electrode had become inoperable.10

The coin cell tests what could be punched; what fell off is part of the answer.

Diffraction: the lithium that did not come back

A two-phase positive electrode keeps its own account. LiFePO4 and FePO4 are distinct phases with distinct diffraction peaks, so a pattern taken on the discharged electrode itself, refined for the two phases, says what fraction is still FePO4: lattice that no lithium came back to fill, because that lithium was lost on the negative side.8 The fresh cell leaves 9 %; cell A leaves up to 30 % at the centre of its first sheet, 20 % at its edge, 15 % at the edge of the middle sheet; cell B’s last sheet only 3 %.8 The pattern follows the residual capacities of L6.3 and the surface chemistry of the same samples, where the salt-decomposition products were thickest at the edges and the outer sheets: the same lost lithium, counted three ways.8

On the negative side, diffraction reads the graphite 00l peak, whose shift with lithium content tells how much lithium the graphite still holds in the discharged state.8 For the surface itself, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy on unwashed samples, kept from air in an antechamber, found LiF, Li2CO3 and the fluorophosphates of the salt on the aged graphite, more of the salt products at the edges and in the outer sheets, and on the positive electrode a thinner interphase at the edges than in the middle of the stack.8

Each technique counts the same lost lithium in its own currency; agreement between them is the proof.

The finding that matters most: plated lithium

Metallic lithium on the graphite is the post-mortem finding that bears on safety, and the one that is easiest to miss, because by the time the cell is opened the metal may have reacted with the electrolyte into SEI and LiF, or with the air of a careless preparation.9 A study of 64.6 Ah automotive cells cycled at 1.5C and 10 °C, conditions chosen to plate, compared three ways of finding it.9

Nuclear magnetic resonance of 7Li, on active material scraped from the copper and sealed in a rotor inside the glovebox, is direct: metallic lithium has a Knight shift of 245 to 270 ppm, far from the ions near 0, and the peak appeared in both artificially aged cells and in neither the fresh cell nor a cell aged in a car to 93 %. It was still there after 11 weeks of storage. It is qualitative.9 ICP-OES of the lithium that dissolves when a weighed piece of electrode is stirred in water is quantitative, with errors of 1 to 3 %, because plated metal reacts with the water and is counted; but it is indirect, and lithium that has already become insoluble LiF is missed.9 The electron microscope showed a mossy layer on the aged graphite, absent from the fresh and the car-aged samples, with oxygen and fluorine hot spots where the metal had met the air during preparation; it cannot map lithium, and it sees a square centimetre at most.9 The authors’ own conclusion is the handbook’s: NMR direct but not quantitative, ICP quantitative but indirect, microscopy local; and the methods that need the least preparation reproduce best.9

That is the pattern of the whole page. No technique proves a mechanism alone, and L6.1 is read as a set of questions, each with the cheapest technique that answers it: the differential curves before opening for lost lithium and lost material, the peel test for delamination, the microscope for cracks, deposits and exfoliation, diffraction for the lithium left in the lattices, surface spectroscopy for what the electrolyte became, the rebuilt coin cell for what the material can still do, and NMR for the metal. Agreement among them, on samples whose positions are known, is what a post-mortem can prove.

Plated lithium is found by its nuclear signature, by what dissolves, and by what the microscope sees; one of them alone is a hint.

You can now explain why a pulse resistance needs its timescale; how GITT turns two voltage changes into a diffusion coefficient, and the four checks that make it trustworthy (pulse length, IR drop, rest, particle size); what impedance is, and how to read Nyquist and Bode plots of a real cell from left to right; why the measurement must be small, steady, four-terminal and Kramers–Kronig valid; why a good circuit fit is not proof; and what DRT, nonlinear impedance and machine learning add.

We can now find out from outside what has gone wrong in a cell, and open it when the outside is not enough. The last module takes up what is left: how to keep the energy a cell stores from coming out all at once, and what becomes of the cell when it is truly finished.

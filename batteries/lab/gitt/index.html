Pulse methods and the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT)

How do we measure resistance and diffusion?

The simplest reading: a current pulse

We begin with the simplest question: how much does the cell resist? Let us switch on a constant current and watch the voltage. It jumps at once; the jump divided by the current, ΔV/ΔI, is the ohmic resistance of electrolyte, active material, current collectors and contacts. Industry calls this the DC internal resistance. After the jump the voltage keeps moving, first as the interfaces charge (charge transfer), then, more slowly, as diffusion sets in.1

So the value we get depends on when we read it, and also on how fast the instrument samples, on the size of the current, on heating and on the change of state of charge during the pulse. That is why test standards specify pulse lengths and currents for “pulse power” tests.1 A pulse of length t probes roughly what an alternating current of frequency 1/(2πt) probes: values from the two methods approximately agree when the timescales match.1

Worked example. The cell in the figure, pulsed with 2 A. read at 0.1 ms: R≈20.5 mΩ, so ΔV≈41 mV read at 10 ms: 28 mΩ; at 1 s: 37 mΩ; at 10 s: 44 mΩ, so 88 mV Same cell, same current: more than a factor of two depending on the reading time.

A resistance quoted without its timescale is half a number.

GITT: a diffusion coefficient from a voltage

A pulse tells us how much the cell resists, but the slow, diffusive part of its answer is mixed in with everything else. If we keep the pulse short, let the cell rest afterwards and repeat, the voltage curve becomes a diffusion measurement: this is GITT. It starts from equilibrium, with the same lithium content everywhere. A constant current pulse of length τ removes (or adds) a known amount of lithium through the surface; a rest follows until the electrode is in equilibrium again; and the pair is repeated across the whole charge.2

During the pulse, lithium is taken from the surface faster than diffusion can refill it from inside, so the surface composition runs ahead and the voltage climbs: the transient change ΔEt=E3−E2, measured after the immediate IR jump. After the rest the electrode has settled at a slightly different composition: the steady-state change ΔEs=E4−E1, a step along the equilibrium curve. The slower the diffusion, the larger ΔEt compared with ΔEs. Weppner and Huggins turned the ratio into the chemical diffusion coefficient:2,3

D=4πτ(mBVMMBS)2(ΔEsΔEt)2

Here mB, MB and VM are the mass, molar mass and molar volume of the electrode material and S its area in contact with the electrolyte; the bracket mBVM/(MBS) is volume over area, the thickness of a film. For spherical particles of radius r it becomes r/3.2,4 The IR jump is left out of ΔEt because it is not diffusion: it holds the ohmic and the charge-transfer drops.4,5

Worked example. Particles of radius 5 µm, a 30 min pulse, ΔEs = 10 mV and ΔEt = 30 mV. D=4π×1800 s(5 μm3)2(1030)2=2.2×10−16 m2/s = 2.2 × 10−12 cm2/s check: Dτ/r2=2.2×10−16×1800/(5×10−6)2=0.016 The check, explained with figure L2.3, says whether the pulse was short enough for the formula; for a sphere, 0.016 just passes.6

Before we trust such a number, we should ask what the formula assumes. It rests on two straight lines. First, the steady-state step must be small, so that the equilibrium curve is straight over it. Second, during the pulse the voltage must grow as t, which is true only while the disturbed layer near the surface is thin compared with the sample: the short-time solution of the diffusion equation.2

What decides “short” is the pulse length in units of the time diffusion needs to cross the sample, τ^=Dτ/L2. The recommended limit is τ^&lt;0.25 for flat samples and about ten times less for spheres and cylinders, and one survey found that most published GITT studies used longer pulses.6 Since D is what we are measuring, we make the check afterwards, with the value just obtained. For spheres a second criterion applies: the t law is within 5 % of the exact solution only for Dt/r2&lt;0.0032.5

Particle size is the other trap. The formula needs the diffusion length, and a porous electrode has secondary particles several micrometres across made of primary grains a few hundred nanometres across. Because D scales with r2, choosing 5 µm instead of 0.25 µm gives a value 400 times larger.5 A published diffusion coefficient means little without the radius it was referred to.

Short pulse, small step, known size: without all three, the GITT formula returns a number, not a diffusion coefficient.

Even with a short enough pulse, the answer depends on how we read the voltage. In one study the same GITT data on an NCM523 positive electrode were analysed by six procedures: two formulas that need the IR drop read off the curve (P1, P2), a straight-line fit of E against t (P3), a two-line fit that leaves out the start (P4), and fits of the whole pulse to the spherical solution, with and without the start (P5, P6). At 40 °C the results differed by a factor of 3; on cooling, by more than an order of magnitude.5

The reason is the start of the pulse. The ohmic drop is instant, but the charge-transfer drop arrives only as the double layer charges, with time constant τdl=RctCdl: below 1 s above 0 °C at medium lithium content, far longer in the cold.5 A recipe that reads the IR drop at a fixed 5 s, or fits a line through the start, mistakes double-layer charging for diffusion. Procedures that skip the transition (P4, P6) are expected to be more reliable; but if Rct itself changes during the pulse, the full fit P6 is thrown off most.5

In practice this means: measure τdl by impedance before each pulse; use a three-electrode cell, so that the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage, large at low temperature, is not mistaken for slow diffusion; keep the current small (0.1C agreed with 0.05C); and check that two currents give the same answer.5

The pulse itself is the weak point, so a more robust route reads diffusion from the rest after the pulse. During the pulse the voltage carries the IR drop on top of the diffusion signal, and if the total resistance drifts, as charge-transfer resistance does when the composition changes, the t slope is wrong. In one worked example, a 17 % fall in resistance during the pulse halves the slope and makes D four times too large. During the rest no current flows, so no IR drop can drift.6

The rest can be solved exactly: plotted against t+τ−t, where t is the time since the current stopped, the voltage is a straight line whose slope gives D, without needing the final equilibrium voltage. Fast processes that are not bulk diffusion, such as a surface layer, appear at the start of the rest and can be left out of the fit; the finite size of the sample bends the curve near the end, so the straight part in between is used, which for a short pulse lasts up to about ten pulse lengths of rest. The recommended procedure: large, dense, single-phase samples; at least three pulses of different size; and the τ^ check afterwards.6

The rest must also be long enough. In one study an NMC622 electrode needed about 7 to 9 h to relax below 3.8 V, and above 3.8 V the voltage was still drifting by 0.1 to 0.6 mV per hour after 9 to 12 h; published studies use fixed rests from 40 min to a day.4 The same study showed that ignoring diffusion in the electrolyte inside the pores, as the simple formula does, made its GITT value about four times smaller than a transmission-line analysis of impedance (figure L3.4).4

GITT is easy to run and easy to misread: check the pulse length, the reading of the IR drop, the rest and the size.

So far we have looked at one step at a time. A whole GITT run gives more than a diffusion coefficient. The voltage at the end of each rest is a quasi-open-circuit voltage (QOCV); joined up, these trace the equilibrium curve of the electrode. The voltage at the end of each pulse is the closed-circuit voltage (CCV). Their difference is the overpotential at that state of charge, and dividing by the current gives an internal resistance. With D from each step, the run yields three curves against composition.2

The price is time: sixteen steps of 10 min with 4 h rests take nearly three days, and where relaxation is slow even 4 h may not be enough.4

running and analysing a GITT experiment
  1. Build a cell with the electrode against lithium, preferably with a third, reference electrode, so that the counter electrode’s own overvoltage, large at low temperature, is not read as slow diffusion.5 Before each pulse, measure the double-layer time constant by impedance.5
  2. Start from equilibrium, with the same lithium content everywhere in the material.2
  3. Apply a small constant current (0.1C gave the same result as 0.05C in one study) for a pulse of length τ, then switch it off.5
  4. Rest at open circuit until the voltage stops moving. Fixed rests of 40 min to a day are published; one NMC622 electrode needed 7 to 9 h below 3.8 V, and above 3.8 V was still drifting after 9 to 12 h.4
  5. Repeat pulse and rest across the whole composition range, recording the full voltage transient; the analysis uses the voltage before the pulse, at its start, at its end and after the rest, and the shape of the rest.2,6
  6. Analyse each step with the Weppner–Huggins formula, using r/3 for spherical particles and leaving the IR jump out of ΔEt; or fit the rest with √(t + τ) − √t, which avoids the drifting IR drop.2,6
  7. Check afterwards that Dτ/L2 was below 0.25 for a film, about ten times less for spheres, and repeat with at least two, better three, pulse sizes.5,6 State the particle radius you used: taking 5 µm instead of 0.25 µm changes D by a factor of 400.5
Planning the time: 16 steps of a 10 min pulse and a 4 h rest take 16 × 4.17 h = 67 h, nearly three days.4 The quasi-open-circuit voltages at the ends of the rests trace the equilibrium curve as a by-product.2

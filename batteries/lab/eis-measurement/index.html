Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) I: principles, measurement and validation

How do we record an impedance spectrum we can trust?

Impedance spectroscopy, from the beginning

Pulses and GITT work in time: we push the cell and watch every process answer on its own clock, all mixed in one voltage curve. Impedance spectroscopy works in frequency instead. It disturbs the cell with a small sine, a voltage or a current, and records the sine that answers, at many frequencies one after the other. If the cell behaves linearly and does not change during the measurement, the answer has the same frequency and differs only in size and timing.1 The ratio of the two amplitudes is |Z|; the timing difference is the phase φ. Both fit in one complex number:1

Z=V0I0ejφ=Z′+jZ″,Z′=|Z|cosφ,Z″=|Z|sinφ

Each circuit element has its own signature. A resistor: Z=R, in phase, at every frequency. A capacitor: Z=1/(jωC), with the current leading by 90° and the size falling as frequency rises (ω=2πf). An inductor: Z=jωL, current lagging, size rising.1,2 Because the three depend so differently on frequency, sweeping the frequency separates them.1

The instrument is a potentiostat with a frequency response analyser, which correlates the response with a sine and a cosine at the excitation frequency over whole periods to get the two parts of Z.1 Commercial instruments span about 10 µHz to 1 MHz, but slow points are expensive: a single measurement at 10 µHz takes 27.8 h, so in practice the lowest frequency is about 1 mHz, often 10 to 100 mHz. Sixty frequencies from 100 kHz down to 0.1 Hz take 2 to 3 minutes.1

Worked example. Peak, peak-to-peak or rms? A published amplitude of “10 mV” could mean any of them.1 Vp=10 mV: peak-to-peak Vpp=2Vp=20 mV root mean square Vrms=Vp/2=7 mV A factor of nearly three between the largest and smallest reading of the same signal.

Once we have Z at many frequencies, we need to draw it, and there are two standard ways. The Nyquist plot puts −Z″ upward against Z′, one point per frequency, high frequencies on the left; a resistor in parallel with a capacitor draws a semicircle of diameter R. Its weaknesses: the frequency of each point is not shown, and the large low-frequency values set the scale and squash the high-frequency end. The Bode plot shows |Z| and the phase against the logarithm of frequency, so every point’s frequency can be read and each decade gets equal room.1

Each R‖C pair has a time constant τ=RC, and its arc peaks where ωτ=1, that is at f=1/(2πτ).1 Two pairs give two separate arcs only if their time constants differ by about a factor of 100 or more; closer, the arcs merge, and at equal τ a single semicircle remains.1 One rule is often broken: the two axes of a Nyquist plot must have the same scale; stretching the vertical one makes depressed arcs look round.1,3

Worked example. Charge transfer in a commercial cell: Rct = 10 mΩ in parallel with a double layer of Cdl = 2 F. τ=RctCdl=0.010 Ω×2 F=0.020 s f=1/(2πτ)=1/(2π×0.020 s) = 8 Hz Milliohms times farads still make milliseconds: the arcs of a large cell sit at hertz, as in figure 11.10.

With these two pictures we can read the spectrum of a real cell, from left to right, high frequency to low. At the top of the range the cables and the wound electrodes act as an inductance, so the spectrum starts below the axis. Where it crosses the axis is the ohmic resistance, the sum of electrolyte, active material, current collectors and contacts. In the middle range come arcs from the interfaces, each a resistance in parallel with a capacitance; at low frequency a tail from lithium diffusing inside the particles.3

A commercial cell has large electrodes and so an impedance in the milliohm range, mostly below 0.1 Ω and at frequencies below a few tens of kilohertz. That makes it hard to measure: a small voltage drives a large current, of the order of an ampere.3 And the interpretation is ambiguous: each electrode has its own SEI and its own charge transfer, at least four processes, but with only two terminals arcs of similar time constant merge into one or two.3 Changing the state of charge or the temperature, which moves the processes by different amounts, helps tell them apart.3

Manufacturers use a single frequency, typically 1 kHz, to check and match cells on the production line; researchers use full spectra to follow degradation, which is the most common reason impedance is measured on commercial cells.3

Left to right is fast to slow: wires, ohmic parts, interfaces, diffusion.

The spectrum of figure L3.3 is idealized; real spectra are never made of perfect semicircles. Arcs are depressed: their centres sit below the axis. The usual fix replaces the capacitor with a constant phase element, Z=1/(Y0(jω)n): n=1 is a capacitor, n=0 a resistor and n=0.5 a Warburg diffusion line. Surface roughness, fractal geometry and adsorption have all been proposed as its cause, and the question is not settled.1 A CPE with a free exponent can fit almost any valid data, so its parameters cannot be read as real capacitances.3

Diffusion into a layer of finite thickness starts as the 45° Warburg line, then bends. If the far side lets lithium through, the line bends back to the axis in an arc; if it blocks lithium, as the inside of a particle does, the line turns vertical like a capacitor.1 Particles are closer to spheres than to films: with the same diffusion resistance Rd and capacitance Cd, a sphere turns upward earlier and its tail stands at Rd/15 instead of Rd/3, and the diffusion coefficient follows as D=r2/(RdCd).4

A porous electrode is a ladder: the electrolyte resistance runs down each pore, and every grain wall has its own interface. The result is a 45° line at high frequency followed by an arc.1,3 In the same study, fitted to an NMC622 electrode, such a transmission-line model gave a diffusion coefficient about four times larger than GITT or impedance models that put diffusion in simple series.4

Measuring well

So far we have trusted the measurement itself. Let us now check it, starting with the size of the sine. Impedance theory assumes a linear system, and an electrode reaction is not one: its current grows exponentially with overpotential. Close to equilibrium the Butler–Volmer curve is nearly straight with slope 1/Rct, and that straight piece is what impedance measures. A nonlinear response to a pure sine is distorted; it contains signals at 2f, 3f and higher, because the harmonics reflect the curvature of the curve.2

The amplitude is therefore a compromise: large enough for a clean signal, small enough to stay linear. Under voltage control 5 to 10 mV is usual, and the Lissajous plot shows when it is too large.1 Under current control, 0.1 to 1 A on a commercial cell gives a response of a few millivolts and may be more accurate.3 The total harmonic distortion, the harmonics’ combined size over the fundamental, checks linearity at every frequency.3

Nonlinear methods turn this round. NLEIS drives the cell moderately harder and reads the harmonics as “higher-order impedance”; NFRA uses large currents to excite them on purpose. In one study the ratio of third to second harmonic rose in cells aged at −10 °C, where lithium had plated, while ordinary spectra looked the same. There is no standard method yet, and large currents can damage the cell.3,5

Worked example. How large is Rct for an exchange current i0 = 1 mA at 25 °C? Rct=RTFi0=8.314×298.1596485×0.001 Ω = 25.7 Ω A 1000 times larger exchange current, as on a large electrode, gives 25.7 mΩ.2

Next come the wires. With two terminals the analyser passes the current and measures the voltage through the same wires, so it measures the cell plus the wires. With four, one pair carries the current and a second pair senses the voltage at the cell’s own terminals; since the sense pair carries almost no current, its wires drop no voltage, and only the cell is measured. For batteries, where the wires are not negligible, four terminals are recommended.1,3

Cables also bring inductance, proportional to their length and most visible on low-impedance cells: the high-frequency end of the spectrum drops further below the axis.1 Short cables, current and sense wires twisted in pairs, a metal box around the cell, the same cables for calibration and measurement, and a fixed holder all help; simply re-inserting a cell in its holder changed the ohmic resistance by up to 10 % in one study.3

The cell must also be steady, and that takes several steps: set the state of charge, then rest until the voltage stops moving (4 h was found adequate in one study; others report 15 min to 100 h, and half of the surveyed studies did not say); let the temperature settle, remembering that the inside of the cell lags the chamber; and set the state of charge before changing the temperature.3

With a small sine, a good connection and a steady cell, one test remains before we model anything. A spectrum is worth modelling only if it could have come from a system that was linear, causal (the response comes only from the excitation), stable (unchanged during the sweep) and finite. For such a system the real and imaginary parts are tied together by the Kramers–Kronig relations: one can be computed from the other.1,3

The relations need every frequency from zero to infinity, which no measurement has, so in practice a test circuit is fitted instead: many R‖C elements with fixed, evenly spread time constants, only their resistances free. Any valid spectrum can be fitted this way; if the fit fails, something is wrong with the data.1,3 The residuals should be small and scattered randomly around zero, well below 1 %.1,3 The test is used in only a minority of published work on commercial cells, though more often now that DRT, which needs excellent data, has spread.3

recording an impedance spectrum you can trust
  1. Set the state of charge, then rest until the voltage stops moving (4 h was adequate in one study) and the temperature has settled; set the state of charge before changing the temperature.3
  2. Connect four terminals, a current pair and a separate sense pair, with short cables twisted in pairs, the cell in a fixed holder inside a metal box, and the same cables for calibration and measurement.1,3
  3. Choose the excitation: under voltage control 5 to 10 mV is usual, and the Lissajous plot shows when it is too large; on a low-impedance commercial cell, current control (0.1 to 1 A) gives a response of a few millivolts. Check the total harmonic distortion.1,3
  4. Choose the frequencies, typically from about 100 kHz down to 10 to 100 mHz; in practice about 1 mHz is the lowest, because slow points are expensive.1
  5. Test the spectrum with Kramers–Kronig before modelling it: the residuals should be small and scattered randomly around zero, well below 1 %.1,3
  6. Fit a circuit whose elements are tied to processes in the cell, with as few as possible; remember that the fit can depend on the starting guesses and the weighting; and plot the Nyquist diagram with equal axes.1,3
Why the low end costs time: one period at 10 mHz lasts 1/0.01 = 100 s, at 1 mHz 1000 s; a single point at 10 µHz takes 27.8 h, while sixty frequencies from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz take 2 to 3 minutes.1

Check the measurement before believing the model: rest, temperature, connection, amplitude, then the Kramers–Kronig test.

Lab handbook · Lab 1

Cell assembly and galvanostatic testing: coin cells, cycling protocols and differential analysis

How is a test cell made, and what does a cycling test tell us?

Where we are. In chapter 16 we named the thieves that take a cell’s capacity. From the outside they all look alike: the cell holds less. How can we see these losses from outside, and tell one from another? In this module we build up the measurements that do it, from a single current pulse to a full impedance spectrum, and we learn to read the results honestly: what each one answers, when its formula holds, and how to analyse the data without fooling ourselves.

Let us put your cell back on the test bench of chapter 5. Every measurement answers one question, so we choose the question first. Cycling at constant current gives the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the state of health; switching the current off and timing the recovery separates the three polarizations (figure 5.4).1 A short current pulse gives a resistance. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique, GITT, measures how fast lithium diffuses inside the electrode material.2,3 Impedance spectroscopy separates the processes in a cell by their speed.4

We take them in that order, from the simplest reading to the analysis behind published diffusion coefficients and impedance spectra. On the way we lean on three tools from chapter 5: the test bench (figure 5.1), the three clocks of polarization (figure 5.4) and differential capacity (figure L1.8).

Every technique answers one question; choosing the question comes first.

Building a cell that can be trusted

Before any measurement there is a cell to make, and most of the scatter in published cycling data is made at the bench, not at the cycler. A measurement institute that audited academic laboratories found the repeatability of coin-cell results “moderate to low” and the reproducibility between laboratories poor, and traced it to bespoke assembly methods; its guide treats assembly as a process, with a written procedure, training and a record for every cell.5 We follow that guide here for the commonest format, the CR2032 half cell: 20 mm across and 3.2 mm tall, with the electrode under study facing a disc of lithium metal.5

The cell is built in an argon glovebox with oxygen and water below 0.1 ppm, and with a nitrogen filter, because lithium reacts with nitrogen as well; a dry room controls only moisture and is not enough.5 The parts go in as L1.2 shows: the can, then the gasket with its narrower opening down against the can, the positive electrode, the separator, the electrolyte dropped onto the separator once it is in place, the lithium, two spacers (the top one may carry the wave washer) and the cap; the electrode is aligned by eye through the translucent separator.5 Some laboratories build the same stack cap first, spring and spacers down, negative electrode, separator, positive electrode, can; what matters is that every cell of a batch is built the same way.6 The lithium is punched at 15 mm and 200 µm, on a PEEK or POM base and never on PTFE, with a punch kept for lithium alone; only shiny, smooth, grey foil is used, and anything black, white or reddish is rejected.5 The separator is a polypropylene-polyethylene trilayer, never dried above 50 °C, and filter paper is to be avoided.5 The electrolyte, 100 µL from a calibrated pipette, is dispensed in the same amount in every cell, because any excess is squeezed out at the crimp and the amount then differs from cell to cell.5,7 The negative electrode is cut a little larger than the positive, so that a small misalignment does not leave positive material facing nothing; that is also how commercial cells are built.7

Three habits make the difference. Never mix parts from different suppliers, and record part numbers rather than descriptions: cans, caps, spacers and gaskets differ in diameter, thickness and coating, and a mismatch of about half a millimetre between the parts and the crimper has been found in several laboratories, one of which had its crimper retooled.5 Take a break after every four or five cells, because fatigue is a source of error, and spread a large batch over several days.5 And keep a spreadsheet with, for every cell, a unique number, the date and the assembler, the collector and electrode masses and the formulation, the diameters of the electrodes and separator, the electrolyte, the open-circuit voltage as assembled, and notes.5

A cell is a process: the same parts, the same order, the same amounts, and a record of each one.

How much is in the cell

Every capacity in mAh/g divides a measured charge by a mass, so the mass must be known at least as well as the charge. The electrode disc is weighed on a balance reading to 0.1 mg or better; at least ten bare collector discs are punched with the same punch and weighed, and their mean is subtracted; the active fraction of the formulation then gives the active mass.5 The areal loading, in mg/cm2 or mAh/cm2, is that mass over the area of the disc, and it should be reported with every result: a thick electrode and a thin one of the same material behave differently at the same C-rate (chapter 11).

The theoretical specific capacity of a material is nF/M, with n the electrons per formula unit and M its molar mass, which is 26 801 n/M in mAh/g: 372 mAh/g for graphite as LiC6 and about 274 mAh/g for LiCoO2.6 The C-rate is the current divided by the capacity: 1C discharges the capacity in one hour, 2C in half an hour, C/2 in two hours.6 Which capacity? A careful study defines it from the practical capacity measured in a slow cycle, C/20 or C/50, in a half cell, not from the theoretical value, because the practical value is what the electrode actually delivers;8 whichever is chosen, it must be stated, because every current, every rate and every efficiency that follows depends on it.

Before the first cycle

The crimp makes the cell: a target of about 500 psi (3.4 MPa) on a typical manual crimper, undershooting rather than overshooting, because overpressure shorts cells and jams them in the die; one laboratory that pressed to over 700 psi lost a cell inside the glovebox.5 Right after crimping the cell is wiped with a tissue of DMC, EMC or acetone, inside the glovebox and without immersing it, because the salt residue reacts with moisture to form HF, which corrodes the can and, later, the holders.5

Then the first measurement, with a hand-held meter: the open-circuit voltage as assembled. For an NMC 811 half cell with a carbonate electrolyte, 3.18 to 3.45 V is in range; a cell at 2.00 V or below is discarded; in between, the cell may cycle, but such cells are often irreproducible for no visible reason, and they are run at the user’s own risk.5 The value is written in the spreadsheet and the number of the cell on the can, in permanent marker, never in graphite.5

Connecting is a step of its own. Holders suffer metal fatigue and lose contact pressure, and an exposed contact can short a cell, so every holder and lead is checked before every run; gold-plated holders with springs are preferred.5 And a cell on a bench is a cell at whatever temperature the room has that day: coin cells exchange heat quickly with their surroundings, so the cycling happens in a temperature chamber, after a rest there for the cell to settle, because “room temperature” drifts and the drift shows up in the long-term data.7

A cell that reads wrong before the first cycle will not read right after it.

The cycling protocol

A galvanostatic cycle is written as a short programme. Constant current (CC) until an upper cut-off voltage; a constant-voltage (CV) hold at that cut-off until the current has decayed to a set fraction of the C-rate; a rest; a constant-current discharge to a lower cut-off; a rest. The cut-offs are chosen for the chemistry: 4.2 and 2.5 V for a layered-oxide full cell, 4.2 and 2.5 V for NMC against lithium, 1.5 and 0.005 V for a graphite-based negative electrode against lithium, the last chosen because lower voltages plated lithium as dendrites.6,8 Rests of two hours between steps let the cell relax before the next one.8

Why the CV hold? The constant-current step stops when the measured voltage, which is the open-circuit voltage plus the i R drop (chapter 5), reaches the cut-off, not when the electrode is full. Holding the voltage lets the current fall and the hidden charge flow in; the faster the rate or the larger the resistance, the larger that share, as L1.5 shows. The capacity of each half cycle is the current integrated over time, which the cycler reports; the Coulombic efficiency is the discharge capacity divided by the charge capacity, and the difference between the two is the irreversible capacity.6

The first cycles are different. During them the SEI forms on the negative electrode by reduction of the electrolyte (chapter 10), consuming lithium that does not come back, so the first-cycle efficiency is low and is reported separately; a formation of two slow cycles, C/10 with CC-CV charging, precedes any test, and two further slow cycles recondition a cell before a new series.6,8 A slow cycle at C/20 also gives the pseudo-open-circuit curve of the cell: “pseudo” because the cell is never at open circuit and a small polarization remains in it.8 Its differential capacity, L1.8, shifts with polarization, the peaks moving to higher voltage on charge and lower on discharge.8

CC to the cut-off, CV until the current dies, rest, discharge: the cut-offs, the end current and the rests are part of the result.

What Coulombic efficiency can and cannot say

Coulombic efficiency is the measure of reversibility: the share of the charge put in that comes back out, with the rest gone into side reactions.6 The arithmetic in L1.6 is the whole lesson. If a cell returns 99.9 % of its charge each cycle and loses the rest for good, it keeps 90 % after a hundred cycles and reaches 80 % after about 220; at 99.95 % it takes twice as long; at 99.99 %, ten times. A tenth of a percent per cycle is invisible in a single cycle and decisive over a thousand.

That sets the demands on the measurement. To tell a 99.9 % material from a 99.95 % one, the charge of a cycle must be measured to better than 0.05 %, which asks for a stable current, a stable temperature, a fixed rate and a fixed window; the same cell at a faster rate, a warmer chamber or a wider window gives another number. Ten cycles on an ordinary cycler cannot rank two materials by cycle life, and a plot that shows a Coulombic efficiency of “about 99 %” for a hundred cycles is showing a cell that has already lost most of its capacity. The first cycle is reported on its own, because its loss is the SEI forming rather than a steady leak.6

In a half cell the lithium counter electrode supplies lithium without limit, so the cell’s capacity can hold while the electrode under study is quietly consuming lithium; the efficiency, not the capacity, shows it. A full cell has only the lithium it started with, and the same loss appears as fade (chapter 16). This is why the two formats are tested for different questions: the half cell for the electrode, the full cell for the pairing.

Efficiency, measured precisely, predicts; capacity retention over ten cycles does not.

Rate and temperature

A rate test cycles the cell a few times at each of a series of C-rates, slow to fast, and then slow again. The discharge capacity falls with the rate because the ohmic, activation and concentration polarizations grow with the current and bring the voltage to the cut-off before the electrode is empty; the ohmic part grows in proportion to the current.6 The return to the slow rate is the control: if the capacity comes back, the fast cycles hid capacity without destroying it; if it does not, they did harm.

Temperature enters through the same polarizations. Warmer, the electrolyte is less viscous and more conductive and the ohmic and activation losses fall; colder, the kinetics slow, and the concentration polarization can push a graphite electrode to the potential at which lithium plates.6 Warm cycling has its own cost, in the long run, in particle cracking and side reactions.6 So a result quoted without its temperature is not comparable with any other, and a chamber with stable control is part of the test bench, not a luxury.7

How many cells, and what to report

One cell proves little. For laboratory coin cells, three to ten cells per batch are encouraged, enough to see the spread; a study of thirty identical cells showed that even smaller sets reveal a reasonable spread, and that spread belongs on the plot.7 With every result, then: the format and parts, the electrode loading and formulation, the electrolyte and its volume, the formation, the window, the rates, the end-of-charge current, the rests, the temperature, the number of cells, and the capacity the C-rate was defined from. Cells that are built the same and tested the same can be compared; everything else is a comparison of procedures.

Report the conditions with the numbers, and the number of cells with the conditions.

We now know enough to read a real curve. A voltage curve hides a lot in its gentle bends. Plot its slope instead and the bends become peaks. Two plots are used: the differential voltage dV/dQ against charge, and the differential capacity dQ/dV against voltage.9

Peaks in differential capacity correspond to plateaus, where two phases are in equilibrium. Materials that take lithium as one smooth solid solution show no sharp peaks.9 Put compactly: “A peak in the [dQ/dV] plot features the equilibrium between different electrochemically-active phases in the electrode whereas a peak in the [dV/dQ] plot locates the transition from one equilibrium to another.”9

Two electrodes in one curve

Where do the peaks come from? A cell’s voltage is the positive electrode’s potential minus the negative’s, each set by how much lithium that electrode holds:9

V=Upos(xpos)−Uneg(xneg),dVdQ=dUposdQ−dUnegdQ

The two electrodes’ slopes simply add, so every dV/dQ peak of the cell can be traced to one electrode.9 A dQ/dV peak needs the voltage to stand still while charge flows, so both electrodes must be on a plateau at once.9

The square plot and ageing

To see both electrodes at once, we draw the state of a cell on a square plot: the negative electrode’s lithium content across, the positive electrode’s (inverted) up. A cell charging from empty to full is a straight line on it.9 The slope of the line is the ratio of the electrodes’ capacities, and where it meets the left edge measures the lithium the cell has lost for good.9

1−xpos=axneg+b

Now let the cell age. Ageing comes in two classic kinds. Loss of lithium inventory (LLI) is cyclable lithium used up by side reactions. Loss of active material (LAM) is material that has changed irreversibly, dissolved or come loose from the current collector.9 LLI moves the line up; LAM tilts it, losing positive material steepens it and losing negative material flattens it. The window between the cut-offs then picks out a shorter piece of line, so the capacity falls.9

In one LiCoO2 and graphite example, lost lithium leaves the graphite peaks where they were on the charge axis but moves the LiCoO2 peaks to lower charge.9 The toy cell does the same. Differentiating real data is sensitive to noise: in that analysis, slow C/20 data were thinned 144-fold before differentiating, to reduce the noise.9

Slopes turn a smooth curve into peaks that can be traced to each electrode, and their shifts tell lost lithium from lost material.

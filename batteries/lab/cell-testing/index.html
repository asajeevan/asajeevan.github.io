Lab handbook · Lab 1

Cell assembly and galvanostatic testing: coin cells, cycling protocols and differential analysis

How is a test cell made, and what does a cycling test tell us?

Where we are. In chapter 16 we named the thieves that take a cell’s capacity. From the outside they all look alike: the cell holds less. How can we see these losses from outside, and tell one from another? In this module we build up the measurements that do it, from a single current pulse to a full impedance spectrum, and we learn to read the results honestly: what each one answers, when its formula holds, and how to analyse the data without fooling ourselves.

Let us put your cell back on the test bench of chapter 5. Every measurement answers one question, so we choose the question first. Cycling at constant current gives the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the state of health; switching the current off and timing the recovery separates the three polarizations (figure 5.4).1 A short current pulse gives a resistance. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique, GITT, measures how fast lithium diffuses inside the electrode material.2,3 Impedance spectroscopy separates the processes in a cell by their speed.4

We take them in that order, from the simplest reading to the analysis behind published diffusion coefficients and impedance spectra. On the way we lean on three tools from chapter 5: the test bench (figure 5.1), the three clocks of polarization (figure 5.4) and differential capacity (figure L1.2).

Every technique answers one question; choosing the question comes first.

We now know enough to read a real curve. A voltage curve hides a lot in its gentle bends. Plot its slope instead and the bends become peaks. Two plots are used: the differential voltage dV/dQ against charge, and the differential capacity dQ/dV against voltage.5

Peaks in differential capacity correspond to plateaus, where two phases are in equilibrium. Materials that take lithium as one smooth solid solution show no sharp peaks.5 Put compactly: “A peak in the [dQ/dV] plot features the equilibrium between different electrochemically-active phases in the electrode whereas a peak in the [dV/dQ] plot locates the transition from one equilibrium to another.”5

Two electrodes in one curve

Where do the peaks come from? A cell’s voltage is the positive electrode’s potential minus the negative’s, each set by how much lithium that electrode holds:5

V=Upos(xpos)−Uneg(xneg),dVdQ=dUposdQ−dUnegdQ

The two electrodes’ slopes simply add, so every dV/dQ peak of the cell can be traced to one electrode.5 A dQ/dV peak needs the voltage to stand still while charge flows, so both electrodes must be on a plateau at once.5

The square plot and ageing

To see both electrodes at once, we draw the state of a cell on a square plot: the negative electrode’s lithium content across, the positive electrode’s (inverted) up. A cell charging from empty to full is a straight line on it.5 The slope of the line is the ratio of the electrodes’ capacities, and where it meets the left edge measures the lithium the cell has lost for good.5

1−xpos=axneg+b

Now let the cell age. Ageing comes in two classic kinds. Loss of lithium inventory (LLI) is cyclable lithium used up by side reactions. Loss of active material (LAM) is material that has changed irreversibly, dissolved or come loose from the current collector.5 LLI moves the line up; LAM tilts it, losing positive material steepens it and losing negative material flattens it. The window between the cut-offs then picks out a shorter piece of line, so the capacity falls.5

In one LiCoO2 and graphite example, lost lithium leaves the graphite peaks where they were on the charge axis but moves the LiCoO2 peaks to lower charge.5 The toy cell does the same. Differentiating real data is sensitive to noise: in that analysis, slow C/20 data were thinned 144-fold before differentiating, to reduce the noise.5

Slopes turn a smooth curve into peaks that can be traced to each electrode, and their shifts tell lost lithium from lost material.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) II: equivalent circuits and the distribution of relaxation times

What does a spectrum mean, and what does a good fit not prove?

From spectrum to meaning

With a valid spectrum in hand, we can ask what it means. The usual analysis fits an equivalent circuit to the spectrum by complex nonlinear least squares and judges the fit by its χ2.1 The trap is that circuits are not unique: different circuits can give exactly the same impedance at every frequency, and adding elements always improves the fit.1 The same model can also return different values depending on the starting guesses and the weighting.2

So the circuit has to come from the physics of the cell, each element tied to a process, with as few elements as possible.1,2 Physics-based models go further: the transmission line of figure 11.11 with spherical diffusion in each grain, or full electrochemical models solved for their impedance.2,3

If choosing the circuit is the problem, we can try not to choose one in advance. That is what the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) does. Every R‖C pair relaxes with one time constant, so a spectrum can be written as a sum, in the limit a distribution, of such pairs:2,4

Z(ω)=R0+Rpol∫0∞g(τ)1+jωτdτ

Recovering g(τ) turns the spectrum into peaks along the time axis, one per process, even where the arcs overlap in a Nyquist plot. That is why DRT is used on commercial cells: to count the processes and then build a circuit with the right number of elements.2,4

The catch is that recovering g from noisy data is unstable and needs a regularization, a penalty of strength λ; only a narrow range of λ gives the right answer, and the number of peaks depends on it.2,4 Bad data points distort it strongly, so DRT needs a Kramers–Kronig check first, and because the inductive and diffusion parts distort it too, it is usually applied to the SEI and charge-transfer arcs.2

Following ageing

We now have the tools to do what module 10 asked of us: follow a cell as it ages. Impedance generally rises as a cell ages, and because the measurement does not harm the cell, it can be repeated through its whole life. Ageing studies run check-ups: the same measurement at fixed conditions every so many cycles, often enough to follow the ageing, rarely enough not to add to it.2 Ageing has two visible effects: capacity fade, from losing active material, and power fade, from growing resistance through lost contacts, thickening surface films and delamination.2

A spectrum is reduced to a few features: fitted resistances, DRT peaks or impedance at chosen frequencies. Individual contributions may follow the state of health, and give early warning of sudden fade, better than the total impedance.2 The difficulty is that temperature, state of charge and ageing all move the same spectrum; for some chemistries, LiFePO4 in particular, estimating the state of charge from impedance is much harder than for NMC.2

Machine learning is the newest route: train a model on features from many cells, then predict the state of health or charge of a new one. Gaussian process regression is common, and models based on relaxation times have reached errors around 1 %.4 Their knowledge ends at their training data, and the studies so far cover few systems and conditions.4 Local impedance, measured on one spot of an electrode, is a further step, since a whole-cell spectrum averages over a structure that is not uniform.4

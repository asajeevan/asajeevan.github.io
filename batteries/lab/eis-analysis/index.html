Lab handbook · Lab 5

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) II: equivalent circuits and the distribution of relaxation times

What does a spectrum mean, and what does a good fit not prove?

From spectrum to meaning

With a valid spectrum in hand, we can ask what it means. The usual analysis fits an equivalent circuit to the spectrum by complex nonlinear least squares and judges the fit by its χ2.1 The trap is that circuits are not unique: different circuits can give exactly the same impedance at every frequency, and adding elements always improves the fit.1 The same model can also return different values depending on the starting guesses and the weighting.2

So the circuit has to come from the physics of the cell, each element tied to a process, with as few elements as possible.1,2 Physics-based models go further: the transmission line of figure L4.4 with spherical diffusion in each grain, or full electrochemical models solved for their impedance.2,3

Reading before fitting

Before any fit, a spectrum can be read by hand, and the reading is what the fit must later agree with. A resistor in parallel with a capacitor draws a semicircle whose diameter is the resistance: the curve leaves the real axis at R0 and returns to it at R0 + R1. At its top, where the imaginary part is largest, ωRC = 1, so the frequency at the top gives the time constant τ = RC, and the capacitance follows from R and τ.1 Two pairs in series draw two arcs only when their time constants differ by about a hundredfold; closer than that they overlap, and at equal time constants a single arc appears.1 A Bode plot of −Z″ against frequency shows each arc as a peak and keeps the frequencies, which a Nyquist plot discards; the two plots are read together.

These readings are approximate for real arcs, which are depressed below the axis and are drawn by constant phase elements rather than capacitors (L4.4); but a fit that returns a resistance far from the diameter read by eye, or a time constant far from the apex, is a fit to be distrusted.

Fitting, and knowing when to stop

The fit itself is complex nonlinear least squares: the parameters of the circuit are adjusted until the weighted sum of the squared differences between the model and the data, real and imaginary parts together, is as small as it can be, and that sum, the χ2, measures the fit.1 The weighting matters, because the impedance spans decades: dividing each residual by the modulus of its data point gives every frequency the same say, where an unweighted fit would see only the largest arc.2 And the answer can depend on where the fit starts, so a fit is run from several starting guesses and the one with the smallest χ2 kept, with suspicion if the others land far away.2

L5.3 shows the trap and the way out. One arc cannot follow two: the residuals, the differences between fit and data plotted against frequency, swing systematically. Two arcs leave residuals that are noise, and return the values the data were made with. Three arcs lower the χ2 a little further, because adding an element always does,1 but the third arc stands for nothing: depending on where the fit starts, it collapses to a fraction of a milliohm or takes a share of the second arc, and the data cannot decide between the two. The rule is the one the tutorial gives: as many elements as there are processes, each one tied to a process by the physics of the cell, and no more.1 The residual plot, not the look of the Nyquist curve, is the judge.

Stop at the smallest circuit whose residuals are noise and whose every element names a process.

Which arc is which: let the temperature decide

A fit returns resistances, not names. The name comes from how each resistance responds to what we change, and temperature is the most telling change. Charge transfer is an activated process: its resistance falls with temperature along an Arrhenius line, and the slope of that line is an activation energy. On an NMC 811 electrode the charge-transfer resistance fell from 1.80 Ω at 25 °C to 0.94 Ω at 60 °C, an activation energy of 17.8 kJ/mol; on the graphite-SiOx electrode of the same cell the activation energy was 35.0 kJ/mol, twice as large.4 The spectra were taken at 50 % state of charge with a 10 mV excitation from 10 mHz to 500 kHz, in a temperature chamber, and fitted with a series resistance, two R-CPE arcs and a CPE for the low-frequency tail.4

Once an arc has been named charge transfer, its resistance becomes kinetics: in the linear limit of the Butler-Volmer equation (chapter 5), Rct = RT/(F i0), so the exchange current follows, and its temperature dependence gives the activation energy of the reaction itself.4 The same logic names the other arcs: change the state of charge, the cycle count or the cell itself, in symmetric cells of two harvested electrodes, and see which resistance follows (L5.6). A spectrum measured once, at one temperature and one state of charge, cannot be named at all.

A resistance is named by what moves it: temperature for charge transfer, cycling for films, state of charge for the electrode.

If choosing the circuit is the problem, we can try not to choose one in advance. That is what the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) does. Every R‖C pair relaxes with one time constant, so a spectrum can be written as a sum, in the limit a distribution, of such pairs:2,5

Z(ω)=R0+Rpol∫0∞g(τ)1+jωτdτ

Recovering g(τ) turns the spectrum into peaks along the time axis, one per process, even where the arcs overlap in a Nyquist plot. That is why DRT is used on commercial cells: to count the processes and then build a circuit with the right number of elements.2,5

The catch is that recovering g from noisy data is unstable and needs a regularization, a penalty of strength λ; only a narrow range of λ gives the right answer, and the number of peaks depends on it.2,5 Bad data points distort it strongly, so DRT needs a Kramers–Kronig check first, and because the inductive and diffusion parts distort it too, it is usually applied to the SEI and charge-transfer arcs.2

Following ageing

We now have the tools to do what chapter 16 asked of us: follow a cell as it ages. Impedance generally rises as a cell ages, and because the measurement does not harm the cell, it can be repeated through its whole life. Ageing studies run check-ups: the same measurement at fixed conditions every so many cycles, often enough to follow the ageing, rarely enough not to add to it.2 Ageing has two visible effects: capacity fade, from losing active material, and power fade, from growing resistance through lost contacts, thickening surface films and delamination.2

A spectrum is reduced to a few features: fitted resistances, DRT peaks or impedance at chosen frequencies. Individual contributions may follow the state of health, and give early warning of sudden fade, better than the total impedance.2 The difficulty is that temperature, state of charge and ageing all move the same spectrum; for some chemistries, LiFePO4 in particular, estimating the state of charge from impedance is much harder than for NMC.2

Machine learning is the newest route: train a model on features from many cells, then predict the state of health or charge of a new one. Gaussian process regression is common, and models based on relaxation times have reached errors around 1 %.5 Their knowledge ends at their training data, and the studies so far cover few systems and conditions.5 Local impedance, measured on one spot of an electrode, is a further step, since a whole-cell spectrum averages over a structure that is not uniform.5

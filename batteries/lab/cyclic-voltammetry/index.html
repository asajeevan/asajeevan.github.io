Lab handbook · Lab 2

Cyclic voltammetry: principles, diffusion, kinetics and battery electrodes

What does a voltammogram tell us, and how do we read it?

What a cyclic voltammogram is

In Lab 1 we held the current and watched the potential. Cyclic voltammetry does the opposite: it drives the potential of an electrode and records the current that flows. The potential is swept linearly in time from an initial value to a switching potential and back; the rate of the sweep, in mV/s or V/s, is the scan rate, and the plot of current against potential is the cyclic voltammogram.1,2 A sweep in one direction only is linear sweep voltammetry.2

Why bother? A voltammogram shows at a glance where a material is oxidized and reduced and whether the reaction comes back; by changing the scan rate it separates fast from slow processes, diffusion from surface reactions, and it can estimate how fast ions diffuse.2 That breadth is also the trap: the same review warns that interpretations of CV in battery research are often deficient or disputed, and that the technique should not be used without the basic electrochemistry behind it.2 This page builds that basis, starting from a dissolved molecule and ending with battery electrodes and electrolytes.

The measurement needs three electrodes. The working electrode is where the reaction of interest happens: an inert disc of glassy carbon, platinum or gold, polished before each run, or, in battery work, the composite electrode itself. The reference electrode fixes the potential scale; the counter electrode, an inert platinum wire larger than the working electrode, carries the current so that the reference does not have to.1 The solution holds the analyte in a large excess of supporting electrolyte, which carries the current and keeps the analyte from migrating in the field, so that it moves by diffusion alone; and dissolved oxygen, which gives peaks of its own, is removed by bubbling an inert gas through it first.1

The duck

Let us follow one sweep of a solution that holds only the reduced form, Red, of a couple Red = Ox + e−. The curve has the shape known as the duck, and two things make it. The first is the Nernst equation: at every moment the potential sets the ratio of the two forms at the electrode surface,1

E=E∘′+RTnFln[Ox][Red]

so as the potential rises past the formal potential E°′, more and more of the Red at the surface must turn into Ox, and the current grows. The second is diffusion. The surface soon runs out of Red, and fresh Red has to diffuse in from the bulk. The region depleted of it, the diffusion layer, keeps growing through the sweep; the farther Red has to come, the slower it arrives, so past the peak the current falls although the potential keeps rising.1

On the way back the Ox made on the way out is reduced again, and a negative peak appears. For an electrochemically reversible one-electron couple the two peaks are 57 mV apart at 25 °C (2.22 RT/F), and their midpoint, E½, is the usual estimate of E°′.1 A caution on reading other people’s plots: two sign conventions are in use, for which way potential increases along the axis and for which current is positive, and papers seldom say which they follow.1 In this course oxidation current is positive and potential increases to the right.

Scan rate and the Randles-Sevcik equation

Sweep faster and the current grows, because the diffusion layer has less time to spread and stays thinner.1 For a reversible couple whose species diffuse freely in solution, the peak current follows the Randles-Sevcik equation,1,2

ip=0.446nFAC(nFvDRT)1/2

with A the electrode area (usually the geometric one), C the bulk concentration and D the diffusion coefficient. The peak current is proportional to the square root of the scan rate, and a straight line of ip against v1/2 is the test that the species diffuses freely rather than sitting adsorbed on the electrode; its slope gives D.1,2

Every voltammogram also carries a current that has nothing to do with the analyte: charging the electrical double layer of chapter 3. This capacitive current, CdlA v, is proportional to the scan rate itself, so it grows faster with v than the diffusion peak does.1,2 That is why a background scan of the electrolyte alone comes first: it tests the cell and measures the capacitive current that must be subtracted.1

Worked example. At 25 °C the equation reads ip = 2.69 × 105 n3/2A D1/2C v1/2, with ip in A, A in cm2, D in cm2/s, C in mol/cm3 and v in V/s.2 Take 1 mM (10−6 mol/cm3), a 3 mm disc (0.0707 cm2), D = 10−5 cm2/s, 100 mV/s. ip = 2.69 × 105 × 0.0707 × (10−5)1/2 × 10−6 × (0.1)1/2 = 19.0 µA double layer, 20 µF/cm2: CdlA v = 20 × 10−6 × 0.0707 × 0.1 = 0.14 µA At 100 V/s the peak would be 32 times larger and the capacitive current 1000 times: the background that was negligible becomes the signal.

Extracting the parameters: what one curve gives

A voltammogram is a list of (E, i) pairs, and before any model it yields a set of numbers that should be written down for every curve. The peak potentials Epa and Epc, their separation ΔEp, and their midpoint E½, the estimate of the formal potential.1 The peak currents ipa and ipc, each measured from a baseline, not from zero: the flat foot of the sweep before the reaction, fitted by a line and extrapolated under the peak, because the current there is the capacitive current CdlA v and whatever reaction is still tailing off from the previous sweep.1,2 The ratio ipc/ipa, which is 1 for a reversible couple whose product survives and falls below 1 when the product is consumed by a chemical reaction.1 The charge under a peak, the area between the curve and its baseline divided by the scan rate, is the capacity of that reaction, in coulombs or, per gram of active material, in mAh/g at that rate.2 And the double-layer capacitance, read where nothing reacts: the flat current at the foot of the forward sweep divided by the scan rate and the area, or half the gap between the forward and reverse currents in a narrow window with no reaction in it (a separate scan in practice, because inside a full voltammogram the reverse sweep still carries the tail of the previous peak), is Cdl, a current that is the same at every potential and scales with v, so that i1/v1 = i2/v2 between two scan rates is the check that it has been read correctly.3

L2.4 reads all of these off a simulated curve, and shows the two commonest errors. Peaks read from zero rather than from the baseline carry the capacitive current with them, and the error grows with scan rate, because the capacitive current grows as v and the peak as v1/2.2 And a separation above 57 mV in a cell with an uncompensated resistance is the i R drop, which spreads the peaks in proportion to the current while leaving E½ almost where it was, since the two peaks move in opposite directions.1

How fast is the electron?

The duck assumed that electron transfer at the surface is instantaneous, so that the Nernst equation holds at every moment. Such a couple is electrochemically reversible: the peak potentials do not depend on the scan rate, the peak separation stays at about 57 to 59 mV for one electron, and the two peak currents are equal.1,2 When electron transfer is slower, extra overpotential is needed to drive it, the peaks move apart and the separation grows with scan rate: a quasi-reversible process. Slower still, the process is irreversible, with the peaks far apart.2 The scan-rate dependence is the test, and lithium insertion reactions tend to be quasi-reversible.2

Keep two meanings apart. Electrochemical reversibility is about the speed of electron transfer; chemical reversibility asks whether the product survives to be converted back. A follow-up chemical reaction that destroys the product shrinks the return peak, and a faster sweep, which outruns the chemistry, can bring it back.1

Molecules that take two electrons can show two waves. If the second electron is accepted more easily than the first, the two merge into one wave with a separation of 28.5 mV; if it is harder, the wave splits into two one-electron waves, each with 57 mV.1 An organic electrode material from chapter 15 gives an example: dissolved at 5 mM and swept on platinum, Li2-PDFSA shows two successive oxidation waves at 0.23 and 0.08 V against the ferrocene couple, 3.66 and 3.51 V against lithium.4 Ferrocene, added to the solution from the start, is the recommended reference in nonaqueous solvents, whose reference electrodes drift.1

Ohmic drop

The potentiostat controls the potential between the working and reference electrodes, but part of the solution resistance lies between them, uncompensated. The electrode then feels the applied potential minus i Ru, and the voltammogram stretches: the tell-tale sign is a peak separation larger than it should be for a couple known to be reversible, such as ferrocene in the same cell.1 Ohmic drop and slow electron transfer look alike, and the larger the current, the worse it gets.

The remedies follow from i Ru. Pass less current, with a smaller electrode or a slower sweep; lower the resistance, with more supporting electrolyte; bring the reference closer to the working electrode. Many potentiostats can also measure Ru and compensate for it.1 In a battery half-cell, the resistance of the porous electrode and of the separator adds to that of the solution, which is one reason battery CVs are run slowly.

Worked example. A 5 mM solution swept at 1 V/s on a 3 mm disc gives a peak of about 300 µA (figure L2.3’s 19 µA, × 5 for the concentration, × 101/2 for the rate). i Ru = 300 µA × 200 Ω = 60 mV on each peak, in opposite directions The peak separation grows by tens of millivolts, enough to mistake a reversible couple for a quasi-reversible one.

Correct before you fit: de-polarization, de-residual, de-background

The scan-rate methods that follow assume the potential axis is the electrode’s own. In a battery electrode it rarely is: as the scan rate rises, the oxidation peaks drift positive and the reduction peaks negative, and if that drift were kinetic the plot of peak potential against peak current would curve. In LiFePO4, LiMn2O4 and Na4Fe3(PO4)2P2O7 it is a straight line, with R2 from 0.991 to 0.999, and the lines for the two peaks are parallel: the drift is an ohmic drop, the same resistance acting on both peaks, with the nonlinear kinetic and diffusional parts amounting to less than about 7 % of the total.3 The slope is the resistance, read without an impedance measurement, and the correction is simply V′ = V − R i at every point, after which the curves are symmetric about their peaks whatever the rate and the number of peaks.3 This is the first of three corrections that Pu and co-workers apply before any fit.3 One caution from our own simulation in L2.7: for a diffusion-controlled couple in solution the slope comes out above the resistance put in, because the drop also lowers the sweep rate the electrode feels near the peak, so we treat the slope as an upper estimate and the intercepts, the peak potentials at zero current, as the robust part of the result.

The second is de-residual. When the sweep reverses, the reaction of the previous sweep is still running: an oxidation current at the start of the reduction sweep and vice versa, larger at faster rates because more material has been left unreacted. Fitting these tails as exponentials from the first and last points of each segment and subtracting them makes each sweep start from zero, and raises the correlation of the scan-rate fits from poor, or even of the wrong sign, to above 0.95.3 The third is de-background: the double-layer current, read from the flat region before the reaction and checked between rates by i1/v1 = i2/v2, is subtracted, because otherwise it is counted as pseudocapacitance and inflates it.3 Only the corrected current is then fitted, in one matrix least-squares step for every potential at once, into a term in v1/2 and a term in v.3 In LiMn2O4 the result returned apparent diffusion coefficients of 9.5 × 10−11 cm2/s on oxidation and 7.0 × 10−11 on reduction, and a surface share that grows with scan rate because the diffusion length r2 = D t shrinks as the sweep gets faster.3

Subtract the i R, the tail of the last sweep and the double layer first; a fit to raw current fits the instrument as much as the electrode.

Diffusion or surface? The b-value

Battery electrodes rarely store charge in one way only. Some current comes from diffusion into the bulk of the particles, which scales with v1/2; some from processes at the surface, double-layer charging or fast surface redox, which scale with v. Writing the peak current as a power law,2,5

i=avb

b is the slope of log i against log v: 0.5 for semi-infinite diffusion, 1 for a surface-controlled process, and between them mixed control or diffusion over a finite length.2,5 A finer split works potential by potential: i(V) = k1v + k2v1/2, where a plot of i/v1/2 against v1/2 gives k1 as slope and k2 as intercept, and so the capacitive and diffusion-controlled fractions of the current.2,5

These tools are popular and easily abused. The split ignores the shift of the peaks caused by ohmic losses, which becomes significant above about 10 mV/s; a b of 1 cannot tell double-layer charging from fast surface redox without other evidence; and electrochemical signatures alone cannot establish a mechanism.5 In Nb2O5, b is close to 1 below 20 mV/s but falls to about 0.7 above it, which Kim and co-workers attribute to ohmic contributions of the active material and the SEI, and to diffusion.2 Used carefully, the method shows, for instance, that smaller TiO2 particles store more of their charge at the surface, and that a LiF–MnO composite stores about 94 % of its capacity in surface-controlled reactions.2 Pseudocapacitive materials have a recognizable voltammogram: broad peaks whose anodic and cathodic branches are mirror images, barely moving with scan rate over some range; nearly all battery-type materials fail that test (chapter 6).5

Three mechanisms, three powers of v

The k1v + k2v1/2 split of L2.8 knows two mechanisms. A tutorial from 2026 adds the third. A faradaic reaction that is not limited by diffusion, because the migrating electrolyte keeps the surface supplied, scales with neither v1/2 nor v; written against the time of the sweep, its current goes as v3/2, and the full regression becomes |i| = a v1/2 + b v + c v3/2 at every potential.6 It needs at least four scan rates, the coefficients are held non-negative, oxidation and reduction are fitted separately, and a coefficient of determination below 0.99 says the three-term model does not describe the data.6 The current of each term, integrated over the sweep, is the charge stored by that mechanism; the coefficients do not change with scan rate, but the shares do, because the three terms scale differently.6

Tested on electrodes whose mechanism is known, the method returned 73 % diffusion-limited charge for silver plating and stripping, 70 % capacitive for activated carbon, and 96 % non-diffusion-limited for MnO2 in lithium chloride, with the rest in each case a measurable deviation from the ideal.6 The same graphite stored its charge by a diffusion-limited route in sulfuric acid, by a larger non-diffusion-limited share in a carbonate lithium electrolyte, and by a predominantly non-diffusion-limited route in an ionic liquid: the mechanism belongs to the interface, not to the material alone.6 Two warnings come with the tool. It classifies by scaling, not by chemistry, so it is a phenomenological regression rather than a kinetic model; and decomposition of the electrolyte at the limits of the window is booked as a non-diffusion-limited faradaic current, so the window must stop before it.6 Both groups publish their code: a MATLAB interface and a Python notebook called deConV for the three-term fit,6 and the matrix least squares of the two-term fit.3

Fit the powers of v at every potential, integrate each term, and read the shares with their R2; the shares are properties of the rate as much as of the electrode.

Battery electrodes

A battery electrode is a solid that takes up lithium, not a dissolved couple, but the voltammogram follows from the same bookkeeping. With the potential rising at v = dV/dt and the current being I = dQ/dt, the two combine into2

dQdV=Iv

so the current at each potential is the scan rate times the charge the electrode stores per volt there. At slow enough rates, a battery CV is the differential capacity of Lab 1 turned on its side. It is not the same thing: a CV contains rate information that the dQ/dV plot of a galvanostatic test does not, it forces the potential rather than the current, and its i R drop changes along the sweep, so peak positions and shapes can shift.2

What shifts the peaks of a battery electrode? Smaller particles shorten the diffusion paths and raise the peak current; in silicon, the alloying peak that is barely visible with large particles becomes clear with small ones. Thick electrodes show peaks that split faster with scan rate than thin ones, because lithium has farther to go from the surface into the bulk. Higher temperature brings the peaks closer together and earlier.2 So report the scan rate, the loading and the temperature with every battery CV, and compare voltammograms only at matching conditions. A slow example from the review: a LiₓMn2O4+δ electrode swept at 0.04 mV/s against lithium, a full sweep up and down taking 6.9 hours.2

A diffusion coefficient from a voltammogram

The Randles-Sevcik slope is widely used to estimate an apparent diffusion coefficient of lithium in an electrode: plot the peak current against v1/2, and D follows from the slope, the area and the lithium concentration in the solid.2 The conditions are strict. The analysis assumes semi-infinite diffusion in a single phase, so for a material that changes phase as it charges, such as LiMn2O4 with its two transitions, the numbers must be treated with care; the slope of log ip against log v should be 0.5, and when the resistance of the cell rather than diffusion limits the current, the relation breaks down.2 At slow sweeps a thin electrode is filled throughout and its peak current grows with v, not v1/2.2

Figure L2.11 shows what follows in practice: CV and GITT or impedance (Lab 3, Lab 4) give values that agree within a factor of a few for some materials and span orders of magnitude for others.2 Quote an apparent D from CV with its method, its assumptions and its area, and compare trends rather than absolute numbers.

When the lattice is stressed: artefacts that look like kinetics

One more thing can move a peak, and it is neither chemistry nor the instrument. The chemical potential of lithium in a stressed solid carries a mechanical term, μ = μ0 − σHΩ, with σH the hydrostatic stress and Ω the partial molar volume of lithium; the insertion potential shifts by σHΩ/F, tensile stress raising it and compressive stress lowering it, and the stresses near 2 GPa that coherency strain produces in nanoparticles shift open-circuit voltages by tens of millivolts.7 Dislocations are the usual source: the 7 % volume mismatch between FePO4 and LiFePO4 generates them as the phase boundary sweeps through a particle, faster cycling makes more, and cycled layered oxides carry densities of order 1010 cm−2.7

In a modelled olivine particle, the stress enters the Butler-Volmer boundary condition as an effective overpotential η − σHΩ/F. With a fixed Ω the voltammogram simply translates; with Ω varying with lithium content, as it does in staged graphite and in spinels, the peak also deforms, with a higher peak current and asymmetric broadening on the high-overpotential flank.7 The Randles-Sevcik diffusivity read from five scan rates then rose from 0.83 × 10−16 cm2/s for the dislocation-free particle to 2.72 × 10−16 at the highest dislocation density and the most effective orientation, a factor of 3.3 that is a mechanical artefact, not better transport.7 The review’s own test is the scan rate: a stress shift is the same at every rate and preserves the v1/2 law, where an ohmic shift grows with scan rate; the asymmetric broadening is distinguishable from diffusional broadening; and a diffusion coefficient from CV should be checked against GITT (L2.11).7 For lithium-rich layered oxides the decisive proof is thermal: heating above 150 °C annihilates the dislocation network and recovers the lost voltage plateau, which no chemical degradation would do.7 The authors are careful to say that no experiment has yet measured a voltammogram on a particle of known dislocation content, and that the orientation hierarchy is established only for the olivine lattice; the framework is a warning and a test, not yet a correction.7

The stability window of an electrolyte

Voltammetry is also how the electrochemical stability of an electrolyte is measured, the window of chapter 3. A linear sweep runs the potential until one of the components starts to be oxidized or reduced and a current flows; the potential at which that current reaches a chosen cut-off is reported as the stability limit.8 Single carbonate solvents measured this way, in a tetraalkylammonium salt on glassy carbon at 5 mV/s with a cut-off of 1 mA/cm2, reach their oxidation limits between 6.2 V (EC) and 6.7 V (EMC, DEC) vs Li+/Li.9

Those numbers sit far above the 4.2 to 4.4 V at which carbonate electrolytes run in real cells, and the reasons are in the method. The cut-off current and the sweep rate are arbitrary choices that change the reported limit, as do the electrode, the electrolyte composition and whether a sweep or a step is used.8 An inert, non-porous electrode such as platinum or glassy carbon measures the intrinsic stability of the liquid, but excludes the catalytic activity of porous transition-metal oxides, which a real positive electrode brings; Scheers and Johansson advise taking stability values from one source rather than mixing studies, and testing as close to the real cell as possible when the cell is the question.8

Before you press start

Most bad voltammograms are made before the first sweep. Polish the working electrode, and polish it again after each experiment, because analytes adsorb. Use a large excess of dry, pure supporting electrolyte. Remove oxygen by bubbling inert gas, then lift the gas line above the liquid. Run a background scan without the analyte. In nonaqueous solvents, add ferrocene from the start and quote potentials against it. Check the uncompensated resistance on a reversible couple, and compensate it if the instrument can. And say which sign convention the plot follows.1 For a battery electrode, add the scan rate, the loading, the temperature and the cell format to that list.2

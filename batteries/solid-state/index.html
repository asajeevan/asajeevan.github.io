Chapter 14

Solid electrolytes and solid-state cells: polymer and inorganic ion conductors

Why replace the liquid with a solid, and why is it so hard?

So far our ion has travelled through a liquid. Two other kinds of electrolyte carry lithium differently: a polymer, where there is no solvent at all, and a ceramic, where the ion moves through a solid framework. Let us take the polymer first.

A dry polymer such as poly(ethylene oxide), PEO, dissolves a lithium salt with no solvent at all. Wright and co-workers first saw ionic conduction in PEO complexes of sodium and potassium salts, and in 1978 Armand proposed the first dry PEO-lithium electrolyte for lithium batteries, conducting about 10−4 S/cm at 40 to 60 °C.1

The Li+ ion is held by the oxygen atoms of the chain and hops from one coordinating site to the next, helped by the segmental motion of the chain; the motion happens in the amorphous, not the crystalline, regions of the polymer.1,2 That is the weakness of the idea: no chain motion, no conduction.

An electrolyte must carry ions and block electrons; how it carries them decides how fast, and at what temperature.

How close can a polymer come to the liquid? The polymer route improved by steps. Changing the salt in PEO from lithium triflate, LiCF3SO3, to LiTFSI, a salt Armand introduced, gains about an order of magnitude, its bulky anion acting as a plasticizer;1 adding 10 to 25 % of a plasticizer gains another, and such lightly plasticized polymers still suit a lithium-metal electrode. Gels, 60 to 95 % liquid held in a polymer, come within 2 to 5 times of the liquid’s conductivity but need a lithium-ion configuration. A dry-polymer lithium cell runs at up to 80 °C.2

LiTFSI itself is a good salt, conductive and safe, with one catch: above about 4 V (3.7 V in PC:DME, as we saw in figure 8.3) it corrodes the aluminium current collector unless a passivating salt is added.2,3 Liquid or polymer, a nonaqueous electrolyte is thermodynamically stable over a window of about 3.5 V but is used, kinetically, up to 5.5 V.2

That window depends on more than the solvent alone, because the neighbours matter: a single EC molecule is computed to resist oxidation to about 6.9 V vs Li/Li+; next to a PF6− anion, which can take a hydrogen from it as HF, the limit falls, and when the hydrogen passes to a neighbouring EC it falls to about 5 to 5.2 V, almost 2 V lower.4 In the usual mixture, the cyclic EC dissolves the salt and builds the SEI, while a linear carbonate such as DMC thins the liquid so it conducts better in the cold.4

Worked example. Let us see what the conductivity costs, for a 25 μm electrolyte layer (an illustrative thickness) of 1 cm2, using R = l/(κA) from chapter 0. liquid, κ = 10−2 S/cm: R = 0.0025 cm ÷ (10−2 S/cm × 1 cm2) = 0.25 Ω a solid at κ = 10−4 S/cm: 25 Ω, a hundred times more at 1 mA: ohmic drop 0.25 mV against 25 mV This is why 10−4 S/cm is the bar a solid electrolyte has to clear, and why it has to be thin.5–7

For a sense of scale, a 2026 review puts solid polymer electrolytes at 10−5 to 10−4 S/cm at room temperature, against 10−3 to 10−2 S/cm for liquid electrolytes.8

That leaves the ceramic, and the question of why the liquid has not simply been replaced by a solid. A solid separator that blocks dendrites would make a lithium-metal negative electrode possible (chapter 12). To do so, a solid electrolyte must conduct Li+ at above 10−4 S/cm, block dendrites without being reduced by lithium, stay chemically stable, and form a robust, flexible thin membrane. The garnet Li7−ₓLa3Zr2−ₓTaₓO12 conducts above 10−3 S/cm and is stable against lithium; a 2013 assessment suggested that meeting all four requirements at once points to ceramic-polymer composites. A polymer above 10−4 S/cm with good electrode contact had, in 2013, yet to be demonstrated.7

Even a good solid conductor has to keep touching the electrodes. Electrode particles swell and shrink as lithium goes in and out, and against an inorganic solid electrolyte the contact is lost.7 In a liquid, the electrolyte simply flows after them.

Negative electrodes for solid-state cells

So far our lithium metal has faced a liquid. The alternative is to put a solid electrolyte against it, and the main reason for moving to solid electrolytes, which we met in chapter 14, is to use lithium metal. Graphite and titanate work in solid-state cells but store too little to be worth it; silicon is attractive but its swelling is hard on a rigid electrolyte.9 A solid electrolyte should be stiffer than the electrode to hold back dendrites, and lithium is soft; yet dendrites still grow through ceramic electrolytes, probably along their grain boundaries, and this is not yet understood.9

The lithium/ceramic interface has three problems: poor wetting, an unstable SEI and dendrites. Interlayers of Al2O3, LiNbO3 or ZrO2, often made by atomic layer deposition, a polymer-gel layer, or an additive such as dimethyl sulfate that forms a stiff Li2S/Li2O interphase, all improve it.9 Two other designs avoid the problem: columnar silicon, whose columns change volume anisotropically and so build up less stress, kept 92 % of its capacity after 50 cycles with a sulfide electrolyte against 50.6 % in a liquid one, at a Coulombic efficiency of about 99.95 %; and Samsung’s silver–carbon layer, which needs no excess lithium and gave a prototype pouch cell of more than 900 Wh/L that lasted 1000 cycles.9

Worked example. An interface of 1710 Ω cm2 at a current density of 1 mA/cm2: ΔV=Ri=1710 Ωcm2×0.001 A/cm2=1.71 V with the Al2O3 interlayer, 1 Ω cm2: 1 mV Without the interlayer the interface alone would eat most of a lithium-metal cell’s voltage.

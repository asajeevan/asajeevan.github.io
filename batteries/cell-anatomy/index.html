Chapter 2

Anatomy of a lithium-ion cell: components, cell geometry and the rocking-chair principle

If we cut open the battery in a phone, what would we find?

Where we are. Module 1 showed the four jobs in a cell: give electrons, take electrons, carry ions, keep the two electrodes apart. Now the parts that do those jobs in a real cell, and why they have the shapes they have.

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide.1 The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.1

The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching.1 If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.1

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words, and they name a reaction, not a piece of metal: the anode is the electrode where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, the cathode the electrode where reduction takes them back.1 On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced, so the negative electrode is the anode and the positive electrode the cathode; that is the battery convention.1 On charge the charger drives the current the other way:1 electrons are pushed into the negative electrode, which is now reduced, and pulled out of the positive electrode, which is now oxidized, so by the reaction definition the names swap, and the graphite that was the anode while you used the phone is the cathode while it charges. The metals stay where they are and keep their signs; only the labels move. To avoid the confusion this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and the lithium host on aluminium, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell.2 Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal.2 The electrolyte is a liquid, added drop by drop during assembly: in Murray, Hall and Dahn’s research cells, about 38 mg of a 1 mol/L solution of LiPF6 in a carbonate solvent, enough to soak the separator and wet both electrodes.3

New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell” against a piece of lithium foil; such cells are easy to make but can fail to predict how the material behaves against graphite in a full cell, which is why full coin cells like this one are built.3

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal (module 0 showed why: an ion drags its way through a liquid at a terminal speed set by its mobility). So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals.2 A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.1,4

Worked example. Why area matters: the resistance of a layer is R = ρl/A (module 0).5 Keep the electrolyte layer as thin as the separator allows (small l) and make the electrodes as wide as will fit (large A), and the ionic resistance of the whole cell falls with both. A wound strip is the way to put a large A inside a small can.

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.1

The porous structure extends the surface area where the reaction can happen and lowers the current density, so the cell works more efficiently.1 A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; a later module returns to what happens where one of them does not.

Let us open your cell and look at the two electrodes. The first thing to notice is that neither of them is used up. Both are insertion hosts: solids that conduct ions and electrons and let guest ions in and out by a solid-state redox reaction. Because their structure and shape survive, insertion electrodes are highly reversible; for layered hosts the process is called intercalation.1

This is what sets the cell apart from its predecessors. Replacing their lithium-metal negative electrode with a second insertion host gave the lithium-ion or rocking-chair cell: demonstrated by Murphy and by Scrosati, put on the market by Sony in June 1991 as carbon against LiCoO2.4 In the graphite, lithium sits between the carbon sheets, up to one lithium per six carbons, LiC6.2

In LiCoO2 it sits between the oxide layers, and here the host sets a limit: LiCoO2 still gives up only about half its lithium in practice,2,4 about 135 to 140 mAh/g,6,7 because its structure degrades at high states of charge:8 once more than about 0.55 of the lithium is removed, the oxide starts to give off oxygen.2,4 Consumer cells have been pushing the upper cut-off voltage higher to win more capacity.6

Worked example. Let us count the travellers. How many ions rock in a 3 Ah phone cell on each full discharge? charge = 3 Ah × 3600 s/h = 10 800 C ions = 10 800 C ÷ 1.602 × 10−19 C = 6.7 × 1022, that is 10 800 ÷ 96 485 = 0.112 mol of lithium mass of that lithium = 0.112 mol × 6.94 g/mol = 0.78 g Less than a gram of lithium travels back and forth; the rest of the cell is the hosts, the electrolyte and the packaging that let it do so.4,5

Nothing is consumed in an ideal lithium-ion cell: the ions only change address.

We now know what is inside the cell: two hosts, a rocking guest and a protective layer. How did it come together? The pieces arrived one at a time. In 1972, at a conference at Belgirate, Steele singled out layered dichalcogenides such as TiS2 as positive electrodes and Armand set out what an intercalation electrode needs; in 1976 lithium went reversibly into TiS2 against a lithium-metal electrode.9,10 Exxon’s cell then failed on dendrites that shorted it and ignited the electrolyte.11 Armand’s answer in the late 1970s was the rocking chair, and Lazzari and Scrosati built the first one, LiₓWO2 against TiS2, at about 2 V.9,10

Around 1980 Goodenough’s group showed that over half the lithium could be taken out of LiCoO2 reversibly, at about 4 V, so that a cell could be built discharged.9,11 In 1983 Yoshino found that petroleum coke cycled in a carbonate electrolyte and proposed carbon against LiCoO2; Sony’s cell of June 1991 delivered about 80 Wh/kg and 200 Wh/L, more than any other rechargeable of the time.9

With the LiPF6 in EC/DMC electrolyte proposed in 1993 and decades of refinement, the same chemistry reached about 250 Wh/kg and 600 Wh/L by 2020.9 Today, cells with NMC-type (lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide) positive electrodes typically reach 300 Wh/kg and above 700 Wh/L, while state-of-the-art LiFePO4 cells rose from 90 Wh/kg in 2010 to 160 Wh/kg in 2022.8 Since its launch the lithium-ion cell has gained a factor of three to four in energy density while its price fell by a factor of 18.12

You can now explain the parts of a cell and their jobs, why the separator must never let the electrodes touch, why cells are built from thin, wide layers, and why an electrode is a porous coating rather than a block.

Two of those parts decide how hard the push is.

Go deeper: what a research coin cell taught its makers

Murray, Hall and Dahn built full coin cells with graphite and a nickel-rich oxide and found that three “identical” cells kept 95 %, 90 % and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. Taking them apart showed the cause: the positive and negative discs were slightly misaligned, and lithium had plated on the parts of the positive electrode not covered by the negative one. The spring and spacer had been dropped in at an angle from tweezers; placing them from directly above with a vacuum pen, and using a thicker, more compliant separator, brought the cells to the reproducibility of machine-made pouch cells.3 Why plated lithium is lost for good is a story for a later module.

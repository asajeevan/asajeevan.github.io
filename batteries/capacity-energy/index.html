Chapter 4

Capacity, energy and power: Faraday's law, the C-rate and the Ragone plot

What do mAh, Wh and Wh/kg mean, and how long would your cell run a lamp?

Where we are. Chapter 3 gave your cell a voltage: the gap between two rungs, which is the free energy of its reaction per unit of charge. Now the other factor: how much charge the materials hold. Charge times voltage is energy; energy per second is power.

Capacity is a count of electrons. Faraday’s law ties the amount of material transformed to the charge that flows, Q = nFN for N moles of reactant, and it has “no known exceptions”.1,2 Turn it around: a gram of a material of molar mass M that gives up n electrons per formula unit holds nF/M coulombs, which the battery world writes in milliampere-hours per gram, Q = nF/(3.6 M). The Faraday constant is the charge of one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C or 26.8015 Ah.2

Two conventions hide in that formula. Graphite’s 372 mAh/g refers to the mass of carbon, the host as assembled, not to the mass of LiC6: Tarascon and Armand give 372 mAh/g for the end compound LiC6 and about 350 mAh/g in practice.3 Positive-electrode materials are referred to their lithiated mass: LiFePO4 holds 170 mAh/g in theory4 and is used at about 90 % of it,3 while LiCoO2 is limited to about half of its lithium for safety, 140 mAh/g in practice.3

Worked example. Graphite, one lithium per six carbons, referred to the carbon. M(C6) = 6 × 12.011 = 72.07 g/mol, n = 1 Q = 1 × 96 485.3 C/mol ÷ (3.6 × 72.07 g/mol) = 372 mAh/g at 3.8 V: 372 mAh/g × 3.8 V = 1414 mWh/g = 1414 Wh/kg of graphite, graphite alone The same calculation on the mass of LiC6 (79.0 g/mol) gives 339 mAh/g. Both are right; they answer different questions.

Light elements and many electrons per formula unit give high capacity; high voltage turns capacity into energy.

Energy is the integral of the voltage over the charge delivered: on a discharge curve, the area under the line, which is also the average voltage times the capacity.4 Per kilogram it is called specific energy (Wh/kg), per litre energy density (Wh/L); how fast it can be delivered is specific power (W/kg) and power density (W/L).2

The capacity itself depends on how hard you pull. At high current the transfer of ions across the interfaces becomes diffusion-limited and the cell reaches less of its charge before the cut-off; that part of the loss is reversible and comes back at a gentler rate.4 The next module to come takes the curve apart.

Worked example. A cell of capacity 2 Ah discharged at an average of 3.6 V. energy = 3.6 V × 2 Ah = 7.2 Wh a 6 W lamp (P = IV, chapter 0) runs for 7.2 Wh ÷ 6 W = 1.2 hours, drawing 6 W / 3.6 V = 1.7 A At that current the curve would sag and the capacity would fall short of 2 Ah; the figure shows by how much, schematically.

The words on a datasheet

A datasheet uses a small vocabulary, and it is worth having it straight. Capacity is the charge a battery stores, in ampere-hours: a 10 Ah cell can supply 10 A for one hour, and since an ampere is a coulomb per second, 1 Ah is 3600 C.5 The C-rate scales the current to the capacity, relative to a one-hour discharge: at 1C a cell empties in an hour, at 2C in 30 minutes, at C/5 in five hours and at C/20 in twenty. The higher the C-rate, the shorter the discharge; some high-performance cells are charged and discharged above 1C with moderate stress.5

The state of charge (SOC) says how much of the capacity is left; the depth of discharge (DOD) says how much has been used, so the two add up to 100 %. A discharge to 80 % DOD is called a deep discharge, and for long cycle life and safety designers keep the depth of discharge small.5 The cut-off voltages bound the useful range: charging past the upper one is overcharge, which decomposes the electrolyte and releases gas, and discharging below the lower one is over-discharge.5

Two words describe time. The cycle life is the number of cycles a battery lasts at a given C-rate; by industry convention it is nearly over when the capacity has fallen to 80 % of its initial value (16.2). Because it depends so strongly on the depth of discharge, a cycle life means little unless the DOD is quoted with it; temperature, rate, cut-off voltages and storage conditions matter too.5 The shelf life is how long a battery can be stored without a load, and self-discharge, the slow loss of charge at open circuit through internal reactions, is what shortens it (chapter 16).5

Worked example. Electric vehicles may draw a continuous 20 to 400 A, from a single cell or from smaller cells in parallel.5 Take a 100 Ah cell. at 1C: 100 A for one hour rated at 5C for 10 s: 5 × 100 A = 500 A charge in that burst: 500 A × 10 s = 5000 C = 5000 ÷ 3600 Ah = 1.4 Ah A burst of 500 A takes only 1.4 % of the cell’s charge; what limits it is power, the subject of figure 4.4.

Power: how fast

Energy says how much work a battery can do; power says how fast it can do it.5 Power density is the greatest power a battery can deliver per kilogram (W/kg) or per litre (W/L), and it is measured by cycling the battery at different rates.5 The limit is the cell’s internal resistance. Of the power that the reaction releases, current times voltage, a part I2R is turned into heat inside the cell, so the power delivered is5

P=IV−I2Rint

which is simply the current times the terminal voltage, V − IR, of chapter 0. To get the most power, the internal resistance must be kept low. It depends on how well the electrolyte conducts lithium ions, how well the electrode materials conduct electrons, and how fast the insertion and extraction reactions run.5 So power is not only a matter of new materials: optimizing known ones, their conductivity, surface morphology and electrochemical, chemical and structural stability, can raise it too.5 Chapter 5 takes R apart into its pieces.

Worked example. The figure’s cell: V = 3.6 V, R = 30 mΩ. at 20 A: IR = 20 A × 0.030 Ω = 0.6 V, so the terminals give 3.0 V delivered: 20 A × 3.0 V = 60 W; heat: I2R = 400 A2 × 0.030 Ω = 12 W the peak: dP/dI = V − 2IR = 0, so I = V/2R = 60 A and P = V2/4R = 108 W At its peak the cell turns as much power into heat as it delivers: an efficiency of 50 %.

To compare the two abilities, energy and power, engineers use the Ragone plot. On it, supercapacitors are high-power systems, fuel cells high-energy systems, and batteries sit between them, overlapping both: a battery with thin-film electrodes reaches the power of a supercapacitor.2 No single electrochemical system matches the combustion engine on both axes at once, which is why the review suggests combining them: batteries or supercapacitors for the power, fuel cells for the energy.2

One caution before you trust any Wh/kg figure: the theoretical energy of a chemistry, reckoned on the mass of everything that reacts, is never what a cell delivers. Practical values are significantly lower, and Winter and Brodd list among the causes the inert parts a cell needs (conductive diluents, current collectors, containers), the internal resistances of electrodes, electrolyte and contacts, and incomplete use of the active masses.2

Chapter 2 showed the inert parts; chapter 0 showed why resistance costs voltage, and therefore energy, whenever current flows. How large the gap is depends on the chemistry and on how the cell is built, so this page quotes delivered values only where a source gives them.

To give the numbers a feel, Winter and Brodd note that a specific energy of 30 Wh/kg means one kilogram of lead-acid battery can power a 60 W lamp for only half an hour.2 The first commercial lithium-ion cell of 1991 gave over 3.6 V and 120 to 150 Wh/kg, two to three times the usual nickel-cadmium cell of the day.3

Worked example. Theoretical against delivered, for that 1991 cell: graphite against LiCoO2, counting all the lithium, so one electron per LiCoO2 (97.87 g/mol) and one LiC6 host (72.07 g of carbon) per electron. active mass per mole of electrons = 97.87 + 72.07 = 169.9 g; Q = 26 801 mAh ÷ 169.9 g = 158 mAh/g theoretical energy at 3.8 V = 158 mAh/g × 3.8 V ≈ 600 Wh/kg of active material delivered by the 1991 cell: 120 to 150 Wh/kg, a fifth to a quarter of that The rest of the mass was copper, aluminium, separator, electrolyte, can and carbon, and part of the lithium in LiCoO2 was never used.2–4

Three efficiencies

A cell never gives back all it took. Three ratios measure how much it keeps.5 The coulombic efficiency is the ratio of the charge taken out on discharge to the charge put in on charge, over one cycle; for a good cell it is close to 100 %, and its reciprocal is called the charge factor. The voltage efficiency is the ratio of the average discharge voltage to the average charge voltage. The energy efficiency is the ratio of the energy delivered on discharge to the energy taken on charge, and it depends strongly on the charge and discharge rates.5 Because energy is charge times voltage, E = ∫V dQ, the energy efficiency is the product of the other two when each average voltage is taken over the charge.

The two factors have different causes. The coulombic efficiency counts charge lost to side reactions such as the growth of the SEI (chapter 16); the voltage efficiency counts the overpotentials and the hysteresis of chapter 5. One textbook puts the round-trip efficiency of lithium-ion batteries at about 95 %, higher than that of other batteries.5 Data sheets add two reference values: the nominal voltage, the average voltage over a whole discharge at 0.2 C, and the nominal capacity, the capacity of that discharge.5

Worked example. The default setting of the figure. voltage efficiency = 3.65 V ÷ 3.80 V = 96.1 % energy efficiency = 0.995 × 0.961 = 95.6 %; charge factor = 1 ÷ 0.995 = 1.005 Almost all of the loss here is voltage, not charge: a cell with a perfect coulombic efficiency would still waste about 4 % of its energy as heat.

Why lithium-ion won

Why did lithium-ion displace the older rechargeable cells? Mostly because of mass. In one textbook’s round numbers, a typical lithium-ion battery stores 150 Wh/kg, nickel-metal hydride perhaps 100 and lead-acid only 25, so a lead-acid battery must weigh 6 kg to store the energy of 1 kg of lithium-ion; lithium-ion also has roughly twice the energy density of a typical nickel-cadmium battery.5 Winter and Brodd give lead-acid 30 Wh/kg (figure 4.5); the point is the ratio, not the last digit.2

The rest of the case is a list. A working voltage of about 3.6 V, two to three times that of nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride or lead-acid cells, held steady under load; low self-discharge; no memory effect, so no need to discharge fully before recharging; no regular maintenance, unlike lead-acid; a cycle life ten times that of lead-acid; and a round-trip efficiency of about 95 %.5 Behind the list is lithium itself: much lighter than the other metals used in batteries, and quick to give up its outer electron.5

Every advantage has a price. Lithium-ion cells need a protective circuit and a cover against short circuits; high-power uses need an advanced battery management system, which adds cost; they are sensitive to temperature and can explode when overheated; and they age markedly, losing performance even when unused.5 Chapter 17 and chapter 16 take these up.

You can now explain where a capacity in mAh/g comes from and which mass it refers to, why energy is an area, what the Ragone plot shows, and why a cell delivers less than its chemistry promises.

Now let us watch your cell do it.

Go deeper: the mass basis, and 372 against 339

Run Faraday’s law on the lithiated compound LiC6 (79.0 g/mol) and you get 339 mAh/g; run it on the carbon host C6 (72.07 g/mol) and you get 372 mAh/g. Both are correct; they answer different questions. The convention in the field is to refer negative-electrode hosts to their delithiated mass and positive-electrode materials to their lithiated mass, which is how each is weighed when the cell is built.3,4 Whenever you compare capacities, check the basis first.

Chapter 4

Capacity, energy and power: Faraday's law, the C-rate and the Ragone plot

What do mAh, Wh and Wh/kg mean, and how long would your cell run a lamp?

Where we are. Chapter 3 gave your cell a voltage: the gap between two rungs, which is the free energy of its reaction per unit of charge. Now the other factor: how much charge the materials hold. Charge times voltage is energy; energy per second is power.

Capacity is a count of electrons. Faraday’s law ties the amount of material transformed to the charge that flows, Q = nFN for N moles of reactant, and it has “no known exceptions”.1,2 Turn it around: a gram of a material of molar mass M that gives up n electrons per formula unit holds nF/M coulombs, which the battery world writes in milliampere-hours per gram, Q = nF/(3.6 M). The Faraday constant is the charge of one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C or 26.8015 Ah.2

Two conventions hide in that formula. Graphite’s 372 mAh/g refers to the mass of carbon, the host as assembled, not to the mass of LiC6: Tarascon and Armand give 372 mAh/g for the end compound LiC6 and about 350 mAh/g in practice.3 Positive-electrode materials are referred to their lithiated mass: LiFePO4 holds 170 mAh/g in theory4 and is used at about 90 % of it,3 while LiCoO2 is limited to about half of its lithium for safety, 140 mAh/g in practice.3

Worked example. Graphite, one lithium per six carbons, referred to the carbon. M(C6) = 6 × 12.011 = 72.07 g/mol, n = 1 Q = 1 × 96 485.3 C/mol ÷ (3.6 × 72.07 g/mol) = 372 mAh/g at 3.8 V: 372 mAh/g × 3.8 V = 1414 mWh/g = 1414 Wh/kg of graphite, graphite alone The same calculation on the mass of LiC6 (79.0 g/mol) gives 339 mAh/g. Both are right; they answer different questions.

Light elements and many electrons per formula unit give high capacity; high voltage turns capacity into energy.

Energy is the integral of the voltage over the charge delivered: on a discharge curve, the area under the line, which is also the average voltage times the capacity.4 Per kilogram it is called specific energy (Wh/kg), per litre energy density (Wh/L); how fast it can be delivered is specific power (W/kg) and power density (W/L).2

The capacity itself depends on how hard you pull. At high current the transfer of ions across the interfaces becomes diffusion-limited and the cell reaches less of its charge before the cut-off; that part of the loss is reversible and comes back at a gentler rate.4 The next module to come takes the curve apart.

Worked example. A cell of capacity 2 Ah discharged at an average of 3.6 V. energy = 3.6 V × 2 Ah = 7.2 Wh a 6 W lamp (P = IV, chapter 0) runs for 7.2 Wh ÷ 6 W = 1.2 hours, drawing 6 W / 3.6 V = 1.7 A At that current the curve would sag and the capacity would fall short of 2 Ah; the figure shows by how much, schematically.

To compare the two abilities, energy and power, engineers use the Ragone plot. On it, supercapacitors are high-power systems, fuel cells high-energy systems, and batteries sit between them, overlapping both: a battery with thin-film electrodes reaches the power of a supercapacitor.2 No single electrochemical system matches the combustion engine on both axes at once, which is why the review suggests combining them: batteries or supercapacitors for the power, fuel cells for the energy.2

One caution before you trust any Wh/kg figure: the theoretical energy of a chemistry, reckoned on the mass of everything that reacts, is never what a cell delivers. Practical values are significantly lower, and Winter and Brodd list among the causes the inert parts a cell needs (conductive diluents, current collectors, containers), the internal resistances of electrodes, electrolyte and contacts, and incomplete use of the active masses.2

Chapter 2 showed the inert parts; chapter 0 showed why resistance costs voltage, and therefore energy, whenever current flows. How large the gap is depends on the chemistry and on how the cell is built, so this page quotes delivered values only where a source gives them.

To give the numbers a feel, Winter and Brodd note that a specific energy of 30 Wh/kg means one kilogram of lead-acid battery can power a 60 W lamp for only half an hour.2 The first commercial lithium-ion cell of 1991 gave over 3.6 V and 120 to 150 Wh/kg, two to three times the usual nickel-cadmium cell of the day.3

Worked example. Theoretical against delivered, for that 1991 cell: graphite against LiCoO2, counting all the lithium, so one electron per LiCoO2 (97.87 g/mol) and one LiC6 host (72.07 g of carbon) per electron. active mass per mole of electrons = 97.87 + 72.07 = 169.9 g; Q = 26 801 mAh ÷ 169.9 g = 158 mAh/g theoretical energy at 3.8 V = 158 mAh/g × 3.8 V ≈ 600 Wh/kg of active material delivered by the 1991 cell: 120 to 150 Wh/kg, a fifth to a quarter of that The rest of the mass was copper, aluminium, separator, electrolyte, can and carbon, and part of the lithium in LiCoO2 was never used.2–4

You can now explain where a capacity in mAh/g comes from and which mass it refers to, why energy is an area, what the Ragone plot shows, and why a cell delivers less than its chemistry promises.

Now let us watch your cell do it.

Go deeper: the mass basis, and 372 against 339

Run Faraday’s law on the lithiated compound LiC6 (79.0 g/mol) and you get 339 mAh/g; run it on the carbon host C6 (72.07 g/mol) and you get 372 mAh/g. Both are correct; they answer different questions. The convention in the field is to refer negative-electrode hosts to their delithiated mass and positive-electrode materials to their lithiated mass, which is how each is weighed when the cell is built.3,4 Whenever you compare capacities, check the basis first.

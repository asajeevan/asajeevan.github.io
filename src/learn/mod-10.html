Module 10

Why batteries age

Where did the capacity go?

Where we are. Module 9 showed materials that fail fast and loudly. A well-made lithium-ion cell fails slowly and quietly: a little capacity gone each week, whether it is used or not. This module names the thieves and shows how each one works.

Capacity is lost in two ways. Some is only hidden: at a high current the cell reaches its cut-off early, and the capacity comes back at a gentler rate. The rest is gone for good, to changes in the volume of the electrodes, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes; the cycle life ends when the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value.{{cite:R6}} For lithium-ion cells Tarascon and Armand put it plainly: poor lifetimes are rooted mainly in side reactions at the electrode-electrolyte interface.{{cite:R2}}

Sorted by what they take, these causes are the three thieves of figure 10.1. Side reactions lock up lithium that could have carried charge. Cracking, contact loss and dissolution remove active material, the room the lithium needs. And thicker interfaces raise the resistance, so that at a given current the cell runs out of voltage before it runs out of charge.

A battery rarely dies of one cause; diagnosing it means separating the causes.

The SEI of module 6 does not stop at the first charge. It keeps growing, more slowly, adding to the interfacial impedance as it evolves.{{cite:R6}} Von Kolzenberg and co-workers show that its thickness follows a power law in time, L ∝ tb, and so does the capacity lost to it: b = 1/2 when the supply through the layer is limited by diffusion, the usual assumption; b = 1 when the reaction itself, or migration during charging, is the bottleneck; b = 0 for migration during discharge. It grows fastest when the cell sits at a high state of charge and is charged fast.{{cite:R37}} Too thick a layer means lost capacity and higher impedance at once.{{cite:R44}}

How much does it matter? In storage, most of the capacity a lithium-ion cell loses goes into the SEI, and in a well-engineered cell more than half of all capacity loss can be traced to its growth.{{cite:R49,R52}} The layer grows while the graphite is being filled, not while it is being emptied,{{cite:R49}} and in a charged cell it keeps growing at rest, more slowly; in the standard models only a fully discharged cell stops feeding it.{{cite:R50}} On silicon the picture changes: the large volume change keeps cracking the SEI, and the growth becomes linear in time.{{cite:R49}}

Worked example. The square-root law, with an illustrative start: suppose the SEI costs 2 % of the capacity in the first month. after 4 months: 2 % × √4 = 4 % after 1 year: 2 % × √12 = 6.9 %; after 4 years: 2 % × √48 = 13.9 % The loss slows down, but it never stops. With b = 1 the same start would have cost 24 % in a year.{{cite:R37}}

The thieves can work together. In a cell with a spinel LiMn₂O₄ positive electrode, manganese dissolves as Mn(II), crosses the electrolyte and poisons the SEI on the negative electrode.{{cite:R6}} The positive electrode loses active material; the negative electrode’s protection weakens, and with it more lithium is lost. Particles that crack and lose contact, as alloys do (figure 9.2), are the other route to losing active material.{{cite:R2}}

Cells age on the shelf too. Self-discharge, the slow loss of charge with nothing connected, differs enormously between chemistries: a nickel-metal hydride cell can lose up to 30 % a month, while a lithium-MnO₂ primary cell keeps 90 % after eight years. It rises with temperature.{{cite:R1}} Overcharging or overdischarging a cell is another way to age it fast: both drive irreversible reactions that form new compounds and cost capacity.{{cite:R1}}

You can now explain the difference between capacity that is hidden and capacity that is lost, the three thieves and how they cooperate, why the SEI keeps costing lithium and how fast, and why a cell ages on the shelf as well as in use.

To catch a thief you need a witness.

Go deeper: how sure is the square-root law?

The square-root law is the usual reading of storage data, and it is what a growth limited by something diffusing through the layer predicts.{{cite:R49,R37}} Which something is still debated: solvent through the pores, electrons through the compact inner layer, or neutral lithium atoms; storage tests at different states of charge favour the last.{{cite:R49}} A recent comparison adds two cautions: few of the √t fits in the literature are statistically justified, and none of the standard models reproduces how much larger the loss in the first cycle is than in the later ones.{{cite:R50}} The shape of a fade curve alone cannot prove a mechanism.{{cite:R49}}

Go deeper: paying back the first-charge loss

The lithium a new cell locks into its SEI on the first charge is gone before the cell is ever used, and in conversion electrodes the first-cycle loss reaches about 30 %. Shanmukaraj and co-workers describe a remedy proposed by Armand: sacrificial salts, lithium salts with an oxidizable anion mixed into the positive electrode. On the first charge the anion is oxidized and gives up its lithium, which pays for the SEI; what is left of the salt leaves pores behind that help the electrolyte wet the electrode. The salt is chosen by its oxidation potential to suit the cell, and the idea extends to sodium and potassium cells.{{cite:R44}}

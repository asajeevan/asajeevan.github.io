Charging and discharging

Why does the voltage sag when you draw current, and where does the lost energy go?

A cell has an internal resistance, and the current has to push through it. On discharge the terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization η; on charge it is the open-circuit voltage plus η, because the charger must overcome the same resistance in the other direction.{{cite:R6}} So the charge curve always lies above the discharge curve, and the gap between them is paid twice per cycle.

The polarization has three parts. Activation polarization comes from the kinetics of the charge-transfer reactions at the two electrode-electrolyte interfaces; ohmic polarization from the resistance of the cell’s components and their contacts; concentration polarization from the limits of mass transport during operation.{{cite:R1}} All three grow with current, and the third grows fastest near the end of a discharge, which is why a hard-driven cell gives up early.{{cite:R6}}

The gap between charge and discharge is energy lost as heat; the shrinking width over cycles is ageing.

The three parts can be told apart by their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears instantaneously, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² s. Concentration polarization is slow, taking 10⁻² s or longer to appear and to fade.{{cite:R1}} Interrupt the current and watch the voltage recover: the step is ohmic, the fast curve is activation, the slow drift is concentration.{{cite:R1}} Module 11 uses the same clocks with an alternating current.

Why are some curves flat and others sloping? Winter and Brodd answer with the Gibbs phase rule. When the reaction converts one solid phase into another, the number of phases equals the number of components, and once pressure and temperature are fixed no degree of freedom is left: the voltage must stay constant, and the curve is a plateau. When lithium dissolves into a single host phase, one degree of freedom remains and the voltage can slope.{{cite:R1}} LiFePO₄ is the two-phase case with its flat 3.5 V plateau;{{cite:R6}} the spinel LiMn₂O₄ shows steps near 4 V and a drop to 3 V as the lithium changes sites.{{cite:R6}}

Which is better depends on the use: electronics prefer a flat, unchanging voltage, while a sloping curve makes it easy to read how much charge is left.{{cite:R1}}

Two numbers summarize a cycle. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial reversible value.{{cite:R6}} A commercial rechargeable cell must survive at least 300 full cycles while losing under 20 % of its capacity, and most need three to eight hours to charge; fast charging stresses the electrodes and shortens cycle life.{{cite:R1}}

Some capacity loss is only borrowed: what a high rate hides comes back at a low rate. What does not come back, the irreversible loss, comes from electrode volume changes, reactions between electrode and electrolyte, and decomposition of the electrodes.{{cite:R6}} Module 10 names the thieves.

The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage: if dE/dT is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as in nickel-cadmium; lead-acid has a negative dE/dT and does the opposite. An irreversible part, Joule heat, comes from the polarization and grows with current; in operation it usually dwarfs the entropic part. The heat is released inside the cell at the electrode surfaces, so high-rate cells must be built to shed it.{{cite:R1}}

You can now explain why the charge curve sits above the discharge curve, what the three polarizations are and how fast each relaxes, why some curves are flat, and why 99.9 % per cycle is not enough.

Go deeper: the shape of the polarization curve

Near equilibrium the current is proportional to the overpotential, and the ratio defines the charge-transfer resistance, R_ct = RT/(nF i₀), where i₀ is the exchange current density: a reaction with a large exchange current is more reversible and polarizes less for a given current. Battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10⁻² A/cm² at room temperature are favoured.{{cite:B2,R1}} At large overpotentials one branch of the Butler-Volmer equation dominates and the overpotential grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation, which holds once the back reaction contributes under 1 % of the current, beyond about 118 mV at 25 °C.{{cite:B2}}

Module 5

Charging and discharging

What actually happens when you charge a cell and then use it, why does the voltage sag when current flows, and where does the lost energy go?

Where we are. Module 4 counted the charge your cell can hold and turned it into energy, with the cell mostly at rest. Now the current flows. This module starts at the test bench, with how a charge and a discharge are actually done and recorded. Then it follows what current costs (voltage, capacity and heat), why some curves are flat and others slope, what happens inside real electrodes made of many particles, how a curve can be read for signs of ageing, and how many cycles a cell survives. From here on the page follows one lithium ion, our ion, through the cell.

A discharge is the cell doing work: current flows out through a lamp, a phone or a motor. A charge is the reverse: a charger pushes current back in, in the opposite direction, and restores the cell to its charged condition.{{cite:R1}} On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced; charging runs both reactions backwards.{{cite:R1}} In a lithium-ion cell that means lithium ions leave one electrode, cross the electrolyte and enter the other, while their electrons go round through the wire (module 6).

The test bench

Cells are studied, and their capacities measured, on a cycler: an instrument that sets the current and records the voltage at regular intervals (once a second in Olson and co-workers’ tests). Most tests hold the current constant. This is galvanostatic cycling, and its record, the voltage against the charge passed, is the raw material of everything else in this module.{{cite:R60}}

At constant current the charge passed is simply current times time, Q = I\,t. Currents are quoted as C-rates: 1C is the current that passes the cell’s full nominal charge in one hour, so C/20 takes twenty hours. Olson and co-workers, for example, recorded a 210 mAh cell at C/20, that is 10.5 mA.{{cite:R60}}

Each step stops at a cut-off voltage. Overcharging or overdischarging drives unwanted reactions that cost capacity.{{cite:R1}} Charging often ends with a constant-voltage step: the cycler holds the upper cut-off, the current falls away by itself, and the step ends when it reaches a small limit. Olson and co-workers held 4.2 V until the current fell to C/20; Safari and Delacourt until it fell to C/25.{{cite:R60,R59}}

Worked example. A 2.0 Ah cell discharged at 1C for 30 minutes. I = 1\,\mathrm{C} \times 2.0\ \mathrm{Ah} = 2.0\ \mathrm{A} Q = I\,t = 2.0\ \mathrm{A} \times 1800\ \mathrm{s} = 3600\ \mathrm{C} = 1.0 Ah, half the cell At C/20 the same half takes ten hours, at 0.10 A.

A test is a recipe of currents, limits and rests; the cell answers with its voltage.

At rest, a cell’s voltage is the sum of the potential jumps at its two electrode-electrolyte boundaries (module 3). Draw a current and two things change. The ions must be driven through the electrolyte, which has a resistance, so the potential now slopes across it, cations running down the slope and anions up it: the ohmic drop, I R.{{cite:B2}} And each boundary has to be pushed off its equilibrium before its reaction keeps pace with the current; that extra push is the electrode’s overpotential.{{cite:B2}}

Goodenough and Park put the whole cell in two lines. On discharge the terminals give the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization \eta; on charge the charger must supply the open-circuit voltage plus it:{{cite:R6}}

V_\mathrm{dis} = V_\mathrm{OC} - \eta, \qquad V_\mathrm{ch} = V_\mathrm{OC} + \eta

Winter and Brodd split the polarization into three parts: activation, from the charge-transfer reactions at the interfaces; ohmic, from the resistance of the cell’s components and contacts; and concentration, from the limits of mass transport.{{cite:R1}}

Polarization or overpotential? You will meet both. Winter and Brodd use polarization for the whole cell, \eta = E_\mathrm{OCV} - E_\mathrm{T}, the open-circuit voltage minus the voltage under load; Bard, Faulkner and White use overpotential, \eta = E - E_\mathrm{eq}, for one electrode, with its own sign. This page keeps them apart.{{cite:R1,B2}}

Current costs voltage: a working cell gives less than its open-circuit voltage, and charging it takes more.

Record the voltage while the current runs and you get the discharge curve. Winter and Brodd draw its typical shape with the three polarizations marked, and it tells you the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the health of the cell.{{cite:R1}} A LiCoO₂ electrode, for instance, is charged only to about 4.2 V, using about half its lithium, for safety.{{cite:R2}}

Push harder and the curve pays twice. The polarization grows with the current, so the two branches move apart; and the discharge reaches its cut-off sooner, before all the charge is out. That missing capacity is not lost for good: at high current the transfer of ions becomes diffusion-limited, and what the cell could not deliver comes back at a gentler rate.{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. A 2 Ah cell with V_\mathrm{OC} = 3.8 V and an internal resistance of 50 mΩ, run at 1C, that is 2 A, counting only the ohmic part. \eta = I R_\mathrm{b} = 2\ \mathrm{A} \times 0.050\ \text{Ω} = 0.10\ \mathrm{V} discharge: 3.8 - 0.1 = 3.7\ \mathrm{V}; charge: 3.8 + 0.1 = 3.9\ \mathrm{V} \dfrac{E_\mathrm{out}}{E_\mathrm{in}} = \dfrac{3.7 \times 2\ \mathrm{Ah}}{3.9 \times 2\ \mathrm{Ah}} = 0.95; the other 5 % leaves as heat Double the current and the ohmic part alone doubles: 0.2 V each way, and the share returned falls to 90 %.

The gap between the charge and discharge branches is energy paid as heat on every cycle, and it widens with current.

The three parts can be told apart by their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears almost instantly, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² s. Concentration polarization is slow: 10⁻² s or longer.{{cite:R1}} So switch the current off and time the recovery: the step is ohmic, the fast relaxation activation, the slow drift concentration. Interrupting the current in this way is how the polarizations are separated in practice.{{cite:R1}}

The middle clock is set by the charge-transfer reaction itself. A reaction that runs fast in both directions at equilibrium, one with a large exchange current, needs only a small overpotential to carry a given current, and battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10⁻² A/cm² are favoured.{{cite:R1,B2}} Module 11 meets the same clocks again, read with an alternating current.

Ohmic, activation, concentration: instant, milliseconds, tens of milliseconds and longer.

Why does a battery’s curve look the way it does? It helps to start from two extremes. Olson, López and Dickinson, following Conway, define an ideal battery material as one that supplies all its capacity through one uniform chemical process at one fixed potential. Its charge per volt, the differential capacity \mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}U, is a single spike, and the energy it stores is simply proportional to the charge.{{cite:R60}}

The opposite extreme is an ideal capacitor material: its differential capacity is constant, its specific capacitance C_\mathrm{sp}, so the potential rises in a straight line as charge goes in.{{cite:R60}}

\text{battery:}\ \ U = U_\mathrm{eq} \qquad\qquad \text{capacitor:}\ \ U = U_\mathrm{ref} + \frac{q}{C_\mathrm{sp}}

“Ideal” here means a mathematical limit, not the best material for a job. And “capacitor material” covers more than capacitors: double layers, surface reactions, and insertion electrodes that take lithium as a single phase all behave this way to some degree.{{cite:R60}} For any real material, a capacity only means something together with the voltage window it was measured in.{{cite:R60}}

A real supercapacitor

Moya charged and discharged three commercial double-layer capacitors at constant current and read their curves with a small circuit. It has a resistance R_\mathrm{H} in series with the capacitance C, plus an inner branch, a resistance R_\mathrm{i} in parallel with a capacitance C_\mathrm{i}, that he links to the diffusion of ions in the capacitor.{{cite:R61}} For a current I_0 switched on at t = 0:{{cite:R61}}

v(t) = R_\mathrm{H} I_0 + R_\mathrm{i} I_0\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_\mathrm{i}}\right) + \frac{I_0\, t}{C}, \qquad \tau_\mathrm{i} = R_\mathrm{i} C_\mathrm{i}

So the voltage jumps by R_\mathrm{H} I_0 the moment the current starts, then bends for a fraction of a second before settling to a straight line of slope I_0/C. After about five time constants it runs along that straight asymptote, which starts from (R_\mathrm{H} + R_\mathrm{i}) I_0.{{cite:R61}}

Reverse the current and the voltage drops at once by 2R_\mathrm{H} I_0, because the current has changed by twice I_0. The bend that follows is twice as large as at the start, and Moya measured that 2 : 1 ratio on all three capacitors (for the 1 F one at 10 mA, 0.34 mV on charge and 0.69 mV on discharge; his fitted circuit gives larger bends, so only the ratio is compared here). The inner capacitance came out close to two sevenths of the main one.{{cite:R61}}

Worked example. Moya’s 1 F capacitor (Table 2) charged at 50 mA for 10 s. jump: R_\mathrm{H} I_0 = 0.31\ \text{Ω} \times 0.050\ \mathrm{A} = 15.5\ \mathrm{mV} ramp: I_0/C = 0.050 / 0.94 = 53\ \mathrm{mV/s} after 10 s: (R_\mathrm{H} + R_\mathrm{i}) I_0 + I_0 t_0 / C = 0.032 + 0.532 = 0.56 V on reversal: 2 R_\mathrm{H} I_0 = 31\ \mathrm{mV} down, then a ramp of −53 mV/s An ideal battery material at the same current would not move at all: it stores its charge in a reaction at one fixed potential.

A battery stores charge at a fixed potential, a capacitor in proportion to its voltage; real electrodes sit between the two.

Why are some curves flat and others sloping? Winter and Brodd answer with the Gibbs phase rule, which counts the freedoms F left to a system of C components in P phases:{{cite:R1}}

F = C - P + 2

When the reaction turns one solid phase into another, two phases coexist. With temperature and pressure fixed nothing is left free, so the voltage stays constant: a plateau. When lithium dissolves into a single phase, the composition stays free and the voltage slopes with it.{{cite:R1}}

Olson and co-workers give a neat example. An electrode with the overall composition Li0.75M can be one uniform phase of that composition, or half Li0.5M and half LiM. In the second case, adding lithium only changes the proportions of two fixed phases; materials that split like this are often called staging materials.{{cite:R60}}

LiFePO₄, measured

LiFePO₄ is the classic two-phase case. Most of its range is a mixture of a lithium-poor and a lithium-rich phase.{{cite:R59}} Safari and Delacourt measured its open-circuit curve as the average of a charge and a discharge at C/100 and fitted it: flat at 3.43 V vs Li over the middle of the range, sloping at both ends, where the material takes lithium as a partial solid solution.{{cite:R59}}

Even this plateau is not perfectly single-valued. Dreyer and co-workers found a gap of about 20 mV between the charge and discharge voltages even at C/1000. That is a true, thermodynamic hysteresis, which they traced to the many particles of the electrode.{{cite:R59}}

The spinel LiMn₂O₄ shows steps near 4 V and a drop to 3 V.{{cite:R6}} Which shape is better depends on the use: electronics prefer a flat, steady voltage, while a sloping curve makes it easy to read how much charge is left.{{cite:R1}}

Two phases pin the voltage to a plateau; one phase lets it slope.

Real electrodes are not one particle but billions, and they do not all react together. LiFePO₄ conducts electrons very poorly, about 10⁻⁹ S/cm on its own, and is made usable by coating its particles with carbon and making them small.{{cite:R59}} Each particle then gets its electrons through its contact with the conductive carbon, and some contacts are better than others.{{cite:R59}}

Safari and Delacourt built that into a simple model, a resistive reactant: four groups of identical particles, differing only in their contact resistance. Its parameters were fitted to their cells, and it does not try to describe the two phases inside a particle at all.{{cite:R59}} It reproduced their charge and discharge curves from C/25 to 1C with one set of parameters, the first charge simulation of LiFePO₄ they knew of.{{cite:R59}}

Why the plateau tilts

Their measured curves tilt more steeply in the middle as the rate rises. Since the open-circuit curve there is flat, that means the cell’s resistance grows as the charge or discharge goes on.{{cite:R59}} The model explains why: the particles with the lowest resistance react first, the worse-connected ones later, and the faster the current, the stronger the effect.{{cite:R59}}

They tested the idea directly. Adding one or two extra steel discs inside the coin cell squeezed the electrode harder. The curves sloped less and the cells delivered more, and the fitted contact resistance of the worst group fell by up to 44 %.{{cite:R59}}

LiFePO₄ also gives more on charge than on discharge at the same rate, by 8 % at 3C and 11.5 % at 5C in their cells. The model captures this with a solid diffusion coefficient that is lower when the particle is full of lithium.{{cite:R59}}

An electrode with a memory

Bring the electrode to half charge in two ways, by charging from empty or discharging from full, rest it for two hours, then charge it at 1C. The two charges differ: 3.96 % in delivered capacity in their cells. This is path dependence, and it fades at low rates.{{cite:R59}}

It has two ingredients. The particle groups end up holding different amounts of lithium, and nothing evens them out. Lithium moves between particles only where the open-circuit curve slopes, because only there does a fuller particle sit at a different voltage from an emptier one. On the plateau it barely moves, even after two hours of rest.{{cite:R59}} Near 5 % charge, on the sloping end, the same experiment gave about a fifth of the effect.{{cite:R59}}

A flat curve hides a crowd of particles reacting in turn; how well each is wired decides the rate, and the past decides the present.

An electrode is not a solid block. It is a composite: particles of active material, often wired together by a conductive additive such as carbon,{{cite:R59}} with the pores between them filled with electrolyte. Doyle, Fuller and Newman modelled a composite of TiS₂ particles, themselves good electronic conductors, mixed with a polymer electrolyte, treating the particles and the electrolyte as two continua laid over each other. Their cell had a lithium foil, the same polymer electrolyte as separator and this TiS₂ positive electrode.{{cite:R62}}

Their computed discharges show the main lesson at once. The faster the current, the less of the material is used before the voltage collapses: at 20 A/m² the voltage drops sharply when only about 30 % of the positive material has reacted.{{cite:R62}}

Where the reaction happens

At first the reaction crowds near the separator. The particles carry electrons about 10⁵ times better than the polymer carries ions, so the short ionic path wins. As the particles at the front fill up, the reaction moves towards the middle of the electrode.{{cite:R62}}

Meanwhile the salt piles up near the lithium foil and is drawn down at the back of the electrode, where it cannot be replaced fast enough. There the conductivity falls and the material is poorly used.{{cite:R62}}

Two dimensionless numbers decide which transport matters, each a ratio of a transport time to the discharge time. For diffusion inside their particles, of 1 µm radius, it was only 0.0001, so solid diffusion hardly mattered: the particles would have had to be as large as the electrode is thick. For the electrolyte it was 0.15 at 10 A/m², and at high currents it is the electrolyte that ends the discharge early.{{cite:R62}}

Worked example. Doyle and co-workers’ solid-diffusion number (their eq. 26), with their Table II at 10 A/m²: particle radius 1.0 µm, solid diffusion coefficient 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ m²/s, porosity 0.3, maximum lithium concentration 29 000 mol/m³, electrode 100 µm thick. the number: S_\mathrm{c} = \dfrac{R_\mathrm{s}^2\, I}{D_\mathrm{s}\, F\, (1 - \varepsilon)\, c_\mathrm{T}\, \delta_\mathrm{c}} top: R_\mathrm{s}^2 I = (1.0\times10^{-6})^2 \times 10 = 1.0\times10^{-11} bottom: 5.0\times10^{-13} \times 96\,485 \times 0.7 \times 29\,000 \times 1.0\times10^{-4} \phantom{\text{bottom: }} = 9.8\times10^{-8} S_\mathrm{c} = \dfrac{1.0\times10^{-11}}{9.8\times10^{-8}} \approx 1.0 × 10⁻⁴, far below 1 The units cancel, so the number is a pure ratio: diffusion inside a particle takes about a ten-thousandth of the time the discharge takes.

The design lesson follows. Keeping the theoretical capacity fixed, a more open electrode (porosity 0.60 instead of 0.30, and so thicker) raised the material used at 10 A/m² from 84 % to 97 %; 0.60 was their optimum. In general, thinner electrodes use their material better when the electrolyte is the bottleneck, at the cost of harder processing and the extra weight of more, thinner cells.{{cite:R62}} They also suggested a stronger starting salt solution, because the 1 M presumably chosen for its conductivity is far weaker by the time it reaches the depths of the electrode.{{cite:R62}}

At high current the back of a thick electrode starves of ions, so the cell stops with material unused.

A voltage curve hides a lot in its gentle bends. Plot its slope instead and the bends become peaks. Two plots are used: the differential voltage \mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}Q against charge, and the differential capacity \mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}V against voltage.{{cite:R60}}

Peaks in differential capacity correspond to plateaus, where two phases are in equilibrium. Materials that take lithium as one smooth solid solution show no sharp peaks.{{cite:R60}} Olson and co-workers quote Safari and Delacourt’s study of an aged graphite and LiFePO₄ cell (a different paper from the one behind figures 5.6 and 5.7): “A peak in the [dQ/dV] plot features the equilibrium between different electrochemically-active phases in the electrode whereas a peak in the [dV/dQ] plot locates the transition from one equilibrium to another.”{{cite:R60}}

Two electrodes in one curve

A cell’s voltage is the positive electrode’s potential minus the negative’s, each set by how much lithium that electrode holds:{{cite:R60}}

V = U_\mathrm{pos}(x_\mathrm{pos}) - U_\mathrm{neg}(x_\mathrm{neg}), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}Q} = \frac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{pos}}{\mathrm{d}Q} - \frac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{neg}}{\mathrm{d}Q}

The two electrodes’ slopes simply add, so every dV/dQ peak of the cell can be traced to one electrode.{{cite:R60}} A dQ/dV peak needs the voltage to stand still while charge flows, so both electrodes must be on a plateau at once.{{cite:R60}}

The square plot and ageing

Olson and co-workers draw the state of a cell on a square plot: the negative electrode’s lithium content across, the positive electrode’s (inverted) up. A cell charging from empty to full is a straight line on it.{{cite:R60}} The slope of the line is the ratio of the electrodes’ capacities, and where it meets the left edge measures the lithium the cell has lost for good.{{cite:R60}}

1 - x_\mathrm{pos} = a\, x_\mathrm{neg} + b

Ageing comes in two classic kinds. Loss of lithium inventory (LLI) is cyclable lithium used up by side reactions. Loss of active material (LAM) is material that has changed irreversibly, dissolved or come loose from the current collector.{{cite:R60}} LLI moves the line up; LAM tilts it, losing positive material steepens it and losing negative material flattens it. The window between the cut-offs then picks out a shorter piece of line, so the capacity falls.{{cite:R60}}

In their LiCoO₂ and graphite example, lost lithium leaves the graphite peaks where they were on the charge axis but moves the LiCoO₂ peaks to lower charge.{{cite:R60}} The toy cell does the same. Differentiating real data is sensitive to noise: they used slow C/20 data and thinned it 144-fold before differentiating, to reduce the noise.{{cite:R60}}

Slopes turn a smooth curve into peaks that can be traced to each electrode, and their shifts tell lost lithium from lost material.

Two numbers summarize a cell’s life. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in on charge, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value.{{cite:R6}}

\mathrm{CE} = 100 \times \frac{Q_\mathrm{dis}}{Q_\mathrm{ch}}\ \%

A commercial rechargeable cell must survive at least 300 full cycles with less than 20 % loss; most need three to eight hours to charge, a quarter of an hour is what users want, and fast charging shortens cycle life.{{cite:R1}}

Not every loss is permanent. Capacity hidden by a high rate comes back at a low one; what does not come back is lost to changes in the electrodes’ volume, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes.{{cite:R6}} The first charge of a lithium-ion cell is a special case, a loss paid once (module 6).{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. How good must the Coulombic efficiency be for a cell to keep 80 % of its capacity after 300 cycles? Take the simplest case, in which every bit of charge that does not come back is lost for good. after 1 cycle: \mathrm{CE}; after 2 cycles: \mathrm{CE}\times\mathrm{CE} = \mathrm{CE}^2; after N cycles: \mathrm{CE}^N the goal: \mathrm{CE}^{300} = 0.80 take the 300th root: \mathrm{CE} = 0.80^{1/300} = 0.99926, that is 99.93 % the other way round, at 99.9 %: 0.999^N = 0.80 \;\Rightarrow\; N = \dfrac{\ln\,0.80}{\ln\,0.999} = \dfrac{-0.223}{-0.001} = 223 cycles So losing just one coulomb in a thousand on every cycle uses up the 20 % allowance after 223 cycles, well short of 300.

Lifetime is set by tiny losses repeated hundreds of times.

The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. An irreversible part, often loosely called Joule heat, comes from the whole polarization: it is released whichever way the current flows. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction, and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage, \mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}T. If it is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as nickel-cadmium does; lead-acid has a negative coefficient and does the opposite.{{cite:R1}} Bernardi’s energy balance writes the two side by side:{{cite:R40}}

\dot{Q} = I\,(U - V) - I\,T\,\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}T}

The heat is released inside the cell, at the electrode surfaces, so a cell meant for high currents has to be built to shed it.{{cite:R1}} Because the entropic term changes sign with the current, the heat it takes in on one half of a cycle it gives back on the other; the irreversible term only ever adds up.{{cite:R40}}

Go deeper: the exchange current, the Butler-Volmer equation and the Tafel line

For one electrode, the current and the overpotential \eta = E - E_\mathrm{eq} are linked by the Butler-Volmer equation. Bard, Faulkner and White write it with reduction current positive,{{cite:B2}}

i = i_0\left[\exp(-\alpha f \eta) - \exp\big((1 - \alpha) f \eta\big)\right], \qquad f = \frac{F}{RT}

with \alpha the transfer coefficient (between 0.3 and 0.7 in most cases) and i_0 the exchange current. It holds while the current stays below about 10 % of the limiting current, so that mass transport does not interfere.{{cite:B2}} Near equilibrium the exponentials can be expanded and the current is simply proportional to \eta: the electrode behaves like a resistor, the charge-transfer resistance R_\mathrm{ct} = RT/(F i_0) for a one-electron reaction. A large exchange current means a small R_\mathrm{ct}.{{cite:B2}}

Far from equilibrium one exponential wins and \eta grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation \eta = a + b \log i, which holds once |\eta| exceeds about 118 mV at 25 °C; Winter and Brodd quote the same empirical form.{{cite:B2,R1}} Winter and Brodd write it with oxidation current positive (their printed eq. 13 lost a bracket and a sign); the physics is the same with the roles of \alpha and 1 - \alpha swapped.{{cite:B2,R1}}

One caution about the heat equation. It takes the open-circuit voltage U from a slow measurement and \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}T from small temperature steps, and Jindal and co-workers found that it estimates heat reasonably during continuous discharge but overestimates it during pulsed discharge, by up to 26 % for a LiFePO₄ cell and 49 % for an NMC cell.{{cite:R40}}

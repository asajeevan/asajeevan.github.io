Module 5

Charging and discharging

What actually happens when you charge a cell and then use it, why does the voltage sag when current flows, and where does the lost energy go?

Where we are. In {{ch:capacity-energy}} we counted the charge your cell can hold and turned it into energy, with the cell mostly at rest. Now we let the current flow and ask what that costs. We start at the test bench, with how a charge and a discharge are actually done and recorded. Then we follow what current costs (voltage, capacity and heat), why some curves are flat and others slope, what happens inside real electrodes made of many particles, how a curve can be read for signs of ageing, and how many cycles a cell survives. From here on we follow one lithium ion, our ion, through the cell.

Let us connect your cell to something that uses it. A discharge is the cell doing work: current flows out through a lamp, a phone or a motor. A charge is the reverse: a charger pushes current back in, in the opposite direction, and restores the cell to its charged condition.{{cite:R1}} On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced; charging runs both reactions backwards.{{cite:R1}} In a lithium-ion cell that means lithium ions leave one electrode, cross the electrolyte and enter the other, while their electrons go round through the wire ({{ch:cell-anatomy}}).

The test bench

To watch this happen we put the cell on a test bench. Cells are studied, and their capacities measured, on a cycler: an instrument that sets the current and records the voltage at regular intervals (once a second in one set of tests). Most tests hold the current constant. This is galvanostatic cycling, and its record, the voltage against the charge passed, is the raw material of everything else in this module.{{cite:R60}}

At constant current the charge passed is simply current times time, Q = I\,t. Currents are quoted as C-rates: 1C is the current that passes the cell’s full nominal charge in one hour, so C/20 takes twenty hours. In one study, for example, a 210 mAh cell was recorded at C/20, that is at 10.5 mA.{{cite:R60}}

A step cannot run for ever. Each step stops at a cut-off voltage. Overcharging or overdischarging drives unwanted reactions that cost capacity.{{cite:R1}} Charging often ends with a constant-voltage step: the cycler holds the upper cut-off, the current falls away by itself, and the step ends when it reaches a small limit. In two published protocols the cycler held 4.2 V until the current had fallen to C/20 in one and to C/25 in the other.{{cite:R60,R59}}

Worked example. A 2.0 Ah cell discharged at 1C for 30 minutes. I = 1\,\mathrm{C} \times 2.0\ \mathrm{Ah} = 2.0\ \mathrm{A} Q = I\,t = 2.0\ \mathrm{A} \times 1800\ \mathrm{s} = 3600\ \mathrm{C} = 1.0 Ah, half the cell At C/20 the same half takes ten hours, at 0.10 A.
building a coin cell and running a cycling test
  1. Punch discs of both electrodes (1.27 cm in one published guide, the same size for both), dry the coated discs under vacuum overnight at 110 °C in the antechamber of an argon glove box, and assemble the cell inside it.{{cite:R21}}
  2. Stack from the bottom: the can, the positive electrode, the separator, the negative electrode (lithium foil in a half cell, graphite in a full cell), a spacer, a spring, the gasket and the cap.{{cite:R21}}
  3. Add the electrolyte drop by drop between the layers, a fixed amount for every cell: in that guide 19 drops, about 38 mg, with two thin separators, or 21 drops, about 42 mg, with one thick one.{{cite:R21}}
  4. Keep the discs exactly on top of each other. Place the spacer and spring from directly above, with a vacuum pen rather than tweezers: a slightly misaligned disc let lithium plate where the positive electrode was not covered by the negative, and three “identical” cells kept about 95, 90 and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. One thick, compliant separator gave more even pressure than two thin ones.{{cite:R21}}
  5. Crimp the cell, then cycle it in a temperature-controlled box (30.0 ± 0.1 °C there): a slow formation cycle and then repeated cycles, C/10 followed by C/5 or C/5 followed by C/3, between fixed cut-offs, 3.0 to 4.3 V for NMC622 against graphite.{{cite:R21}}
  6. Charge at constant current, hold the upper cut-off until the current falls to a small limit such as C/20, and discharge at constant current; record the capacity and Coulombic efficiency of every cycle.{{cite:R60,R76}}
  7. Read half-cell results with care: their excess of lithium masks the side reactions that consume lithium in a full cell. A lithium foil over 50 µm thick holds more than 10 mAh/cm²: against a positive electrode of about 3 mAh/cm², a negative-to-positive capacity ratio above 3.3, against about 1.1 in a full cell.{{cite:R21,R76}}
Setting the current: a positive electrode disc of 1.27 cm with a loading of 21.3 mg/cm², taken here as the whole coating, of which 96 % is active material, holds 21.3 \times \pi (0.635)^2 \times 0.96 = 25.9\ \mathrm{mg} of active material.{{cite:R21}} At an illustrative 170 mAh/g that is 4.40 mAh, so C/10 is 0.44 mA and C/3 is 1.47 mA.

A test is a recipe of currents, limits and rests; the cell answers with its voltage.

The cycler shows us only the voltage at the terminals. Let us look along the cell to see where that voltage goes. At rest, a cell’s voltage is the sum of the potential jumps at its two electrode-electrolyte boundaries ({{ch:cell-voltage}}). Draw a current and two things change. The ions must be driven through the electrolyte, which has a resistance, so the potential now slopes across it, cations running down the slope and anions up it: the ohmic drop, I R.{{cite:B2}} And each boundary has to be pushed off its equilibrium before its reaction keeps pace with the current; that extra push is the electrode’s overpotential.{{cite:B2}}

For the whole cell this comes down to two lines. On discharge the terminals give the open-circuit voltage minus a polarization \eta; on charge the charger must supply the open-circuit voltage plus it:{{cite:R6}}

V_\mathrm{dis} = V_\mathrm{OC} - \eta, \qquad V_\mathrm{ch} = V_\mathrm{OC} + \eta

The polarization itself has three parts: activation, from the charge-transfer reactions at the interfaces; ohmic, from the resistance of the cell’s components and contacts; and concentration, from the limits of mass transport.{{cite:R1}}

Polarization or overpotential? You will meet both. Here polarization belongs to the whole cell, \eta = E_\mathrm{OCV} - E_\mathrm{T}, the open-circuit voltage minus the voltage under load; overpotential, \eta = E - E_\mathrm{eq}, belongs to one electrode and has its own sign. We keep them apart.{{cite:R1,B2}}

Why do practical values fall short of the theoretical ones a material promises? One textbook groups the causes into three: charge transfer and mass transport, in the bulk and at the interfaces; the structural and phase changes of the electrode as lithium leaves and enters it; and the way electrical and chemical effects are coupled in time.{{cite:B3}} This chapter deals with the first; {{ch:insertion-hosts}} and {{ch:degradation}} with the other two.

Current costs voltage: a working cell gives less than its open-circuit voltage, and charging it takes more.

Now let us record the voltage while the current runs, all the way from full to empty: that record is the discharge curve. Its typical shape carries all three polarizations, and it tells us the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the health of the cell.{{cite:R1}} The upper end of the curve has its own story. A LiCoO₂ electrode, for instance, gives up only about half its lithium.{{cite:R2,R73}} In practice that is about 135 to 140 mAh/g,{{cite:R71,R72}} because its structure degrades at high states of charge.{{cite:R73}} Consumer cells have been pushing the upper cut-off voltage higher to win more capacity.{{cite:R71}}

Push harder and the curve pays twice. The polarization grows with the current, so the two branches move apart; and the discharge reaches its cut-off sooner, before all the charge is out. That missing capacity is not lost for good: at high current the transfer of ions becomes diffusion-limited, and what the cell could not deliver comes back at a gentler rate.{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. Let us price the ohmic part on its own. A 2 Ah cell with V_\mathrm{OC} = 3.8 V and an internal resistance of 50 mΩ, run at 1C, that is 2 A, counting only the ohmic part. \eta = I R_\mathrm{b} = 2\ \mathrm{A} \times 0.050\ \text{Ω} = 0.10\ \mathrm{V} discharge: 3.8 - 0.1 = 3.7\ \mathrm{V}; charge: 3.8 + 0.1 = 3.9\ \mathrm{V} \dfrac{E_\mathrm{out}}{E_\mathrm{in}} = \dfrac{3.7 \times 2\ \mathrm{Ah}}{3.9 \times 2\ \mathrm{Ah}} = 0.95; the other 5 % leaves as heat Double the current and the ohmic part alone doubles: 0.2 V each way, and the share returned falls to 90 %.

The gap between the charge and discharge branches is energy paid as heat on every cycle, and it widens with current.

If the three parts all add to one gap, how can we tell them apart? By their clocks. Ohmic polarization appears and disappears almost instantly, within about a microsecond. Activation polarization builds up and decays in about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² s. Concentration polarization is slow: 10⁻² s or longer.{{cite:R1}} So let us switch the current off and time the recovery: the step is ohmic, the fast relaxation activation, the slow drift concentration. Interrupting the current in this way is how the polarizations are separated in practice.{{cite:R1}}

The middle clock is set by the charge-transfer reaction itself. A reaction that runs fast in both directions at equilibrium, one with a large exchange current, needs only a small overpotential to carry a given current, and battery reactions with exchange current densities around 10⁻² A/cm² are favoured.{{cite:R1,B2}} We meet the same clocks again in {{ch:lab/eis-measurement}}, read with an alternating current.

Ohmic, activation, concentration: instant, milliseconds, tens of milliseconds and longer.

We have seen how current pulls the curve away from the open-circuit voltage. But why does a battery’s curve have the shape it has in the first place? Let us start from two extremes. An ideal battery material is one that supplies all its capacity through one uniform chemical process at one fixed potential. Its charge per volt, the differential capacity \mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}U, is a single spike, and the energy it stores is simply proportional to the charge.{{cite:R60}}

The opposite extreme is an ideal capacitor material: its differential capacity is constant, its specific capacitance C_\mathrm{sp}, so the potential rises in a straight line as charge goes in.{{cite:R60}}

\text{battery:}\ \ U = U_\mathrm{eq} \qquad\qquad \text{capacitor:}\ \ U = U_\mathrm{ref} + \frac{q}{C_\mathrm{sp}}

“Ideal” here means a mathematical limit, not the best material for a job. And “capacitor material” covers more than capacitors: double layers, surface reactions, and insertion electrodes that take lithium as a single phase all behave this way to some degree.{{cite:R60}} For any real material, a capacity only means something together with the voltage window it was measured in.{{cite:R60}}

A real supercapacitor

Real capacitors are not ideal either; let us see how one departs from the straight line. In one study, three commercial double-layer capacitors were charged and discharged at constant current and their curves read with a small circuit. It has a resistance R_\mathrm{H} in series with the capacitance C, plus an inner branch, a resistance R_\mathrm{i} in parallel with a capacitance C_\mathrm{i}, attributed to the diffusion of ions in the capacitor.{{cite:R61}} For a current I_0 switched on at t = 0:{{cite:R61}}

v(t) = R_\mathrm{H} I_0 + R_\mathrm{i} I_0\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_\mathrm{i}}\right) + \frac{I_0\, t}{C}, \qquad \tau_\mathrm{i} = R_\mathrm{i} C_\mathrm{i}

So the voltage jumps by R_\mathrm{H} I_0 the moment the current starts, then bends for a fraction of a second before settling to a straight line of slope I_0/C. After about five time constants it runs along that straight asymptote, which starts from (R_\mathrm{H} + R_\mathrm{i}) I_0.{{cite:R61}}

Reverse the current and the voltage drops at once by 2R_\mathrm{H} I_0, because the current has changed by twice I_0. The bend that follows is twice as large as at the start, and the measurements showed that 2 : 1 ratio on all three capacitors (for the 1 F one at 10 mA, 0.34 mV on charge and 0.69 mV on discharge; the fitted circuit gives larger bends, so only the ratio is compared here). The inner capacitance came out close to two sevenths of the main one.{{cite:R61}}

Worked example. The 1 F capacitor of that study, with its fitted values, charged at 50 mA for 10 s. jump: R_\mathrm{H} I_0 = 0.31\ \text{Ω} \times 0.050\ \mathrm{A} = 15.5\ \mathrm{mV} ramp: I_0/C = 0.050 / 0.94 = 53\ \mathrm{mV/s} after 10 s: (R_\mathrm{H} + R_\mathrm{i}) I_0 + I_0 t_0 / C = 0.032 + 0.532 = 0.56 V on reversal: 2 R_\mathrm{H} I_0 = 31\ \mathrm{mV} down, then a ramp of −53 mV/s An ideal battery material at the same current would not move at all: it stores its charge in a reaction at one fixed potential.

A battery stores charge at a fixed potential, a capacitor in proportion to its voltage; real electrodes sit between the two.

Which behaviour a material shows can depend on its size. LiCoO₂ behaves pseudocapacitively as a nanoscale material and as a conventional battery material in particles of micrometres and larger.{{cite:R81}}

Real battery electrodes sit between the two extremes, and their curves differ: some are flat, others slope. Why? The answer is the Gibbs phase rule, which counts the freedoms F left to a system of C components in P phases:{{cite:R1}}

F = C - P + 2

When the reaction turns one solid phase into another, two phases coexist. With temperature and pressure fixed nothing is left free, so the voltage stays constant: a plateau. When lithium dissolves into a single phase, the composition stays free and the voltage slopes with it.{{cite:R1}}

A simple example makes the difference concrete. An electrode with the overall composition Li0.75M can be one uniform phase of that composition, or half Li0.5M and half LiM. In the second case, adding lithium only changes the proportions of two fixed phases; materials that split like this are often called staging materials.{{cite:R60}}

LiFePO₄, measured

LiFePO₄ is the classic two-phase case. Most of its range is a mixture of a lithium-poor and a lithium-rich phase.{{cite:R59}} Its open-circuit curve, measured as the average of a charge and a discharge at C/100 and fitted, is flat at 3.43 V vs Li over the middle of the range and slopes at both ends, where the material takes lithium as a partial solid solution.{{cite:R59}}

Even this plateau is not perfectly single-valued. A gap of about 20 mV remains between the charge and discharge voltages even at C/1000. That is a true, thermodynamic hysteresis, traced to the many particles of the electrode.{{cite:R59}}

Other materials draw other shapes. The spinel LiMn₂O₄ shows steps near 4 V and a drop to 3 V.{{cite:R6}} Which shape is better depends on the use: electronics prefer a flat, steady voltage, while a sloping curve makes it easy to read how much charge is left.{{cite:R1}}

Two phases pin the voltage to a plateau; one phase lets it slope.

That hysteresis is our first hint that the many particles matter, so let us now take them seriously. Real electrodes are not one particle but billions, and they do not all react together. LiFePO₄ conducts electrons very poorly, about 10⁻⁹ S/cm on its own, and is made usable by coating its particles with carbon and making them small.{{cite:R59}} Each particle then gets its electrons through its contact with the conductive carbon, and some contacts are better than others.{{cite:R59}}

A simple model builds that in, a resistive reactant: four groups of identical particles, differing only in their contact resistance. Its parameters were fitted to measured cells, and it does not try to describe the two phases inside a particle at all.{{cite:R59}} With one set of parameters it reproduced those cells’ charge and discharge curves from C/25 to 1C, the first charge simulation of LiFePO₄ its authors knew of.{{cite:R59}}

Why the plateau tilts

The measured curves tilt more steeply in the middle as the rate rises. Since the open-circuit curve there is flat, that means the cell’s resistance grows as the charge or discharge goes on.{{cite:R59}} The model explains why: the particles with the lowest resistance react first, the worse-connected ones later, and the faster the current, the stronger the effect.{{cite:R59}}

The idea was tested directly. In LiFePO₄ coin cells, adding one or two extra steel discs squeezed the electrode harder. The curves sloped less and the cells delivered more, and the fitted contact resistance of the worst group fell by up to 44 %.{{cite:R59}}

LiFePO₄ also gives more on charge than on discharge at the same rate, by 8 % at 3C and 11.5 % at 5C in those cells. The model captures this with a solid diffusion coefficient that is lower when the particle is full of lithium.{{cite:R59}}

An electrode with a memory

The groups have one more surprise for us. Bring the electrode to half charge in two ways, by charging from empty or discharging from full, rest it for two hours, then charge it at 1C. The two charges differ: 3.96 % in delivered capacity in those cells. This is path dependence, and it fades at low rates.{{cite:R59}}

It has two ingredients. The particle groups end up holding different amounts of lithium, and nothing evens them out. Lithium moves between particles only where the open-circuit curve slopes, because only there does a fuller particle sit at a different voltage from an emptier one. On the plateau it barely moves, even after two hours of rest.{{cite:R59}} Near 5 % charge, on the sloping end, the same experiment gave about a fifth of the effect.{{cite:R59}}

A flat curve hides a crowd of particles reacting in turn; how well each is wired decides the rate, and the past decides the present.

So far we have followed the electrons to each particle. The ions must reach the particles too, and for that we must look at the space between them. An electrode is not a solid block. It is a composite: particles of active material, often wired together by a conductive additive such as carbon,{{cite:R59}} held by a binder, with about 30 % of its volume left as pores that the electrolyte fills.{{cite:R1}} Every ion that reaches a particle has travelled through those pores, and two numbers describe them.

Porosity: how much room

The porosity \varepsilon is the fraction of the electrode’s volume that is pore. It is well defined and easy to find.{{cite:R74}} We weigh a disc of the coating and measure its thickness. From the masses and densities of the solid ingredients we get the volume the solids fill, and whatever is left of the coating’s volume is pore.{{cite:R74}}

Freshly coated electrodes are then pressed, or calendered, to lower their porosity. That packs more energy into each litre and improves the electronic contact through the coating and to the current collector; typical lithium-ion electrodes end at roughly 30 %.{{cite:R74}}

Tortuosity: how far round

The pores do not run straight across. An ion has to wind round the particles, so its path is longer than the coating is thick, and the channels narrow and widen on the way. The tortuosity \tau collects these effects. In its simplest, geometric sense it is the length of the path divided by the straight distance. The tortuosity that matters for transport, the effective tortuosity, also includes the changing cross-section and the rough surfaces; it is found by measurement, or by simulation on a three-dimensional image of the electrode.{{cite:R74}}

Together the two numbers set how well the electrolyte conducts inside the coating, \kappa_\mathrm{eff} = \kappa\,\varepsilon/\tau. The ratio N_\mathrm{M} = \kappa/\kappa_\mathrm{eff} = \tau/\varepsilon, the MacMullin number, says how many times worse the filled pores conduct than the free liquid.{{cite:R74}} Battery models often take the tortuosity from the porosity alone, with the Bruggeman relation \tau = \varepsilon^{-0.5}, which gives \kappa_\mathrm{eff} = \kappa\,\varepsilon^{1.5}.{{cite:R74}} The modelled cell in the next figure uses exactly this correction, for diffusion as well as for conduction.{{cite:R62}}

Measuring it, and why it matters

How is \tau measured? One clean way builds a cell from two identical electrodes and fills it with a salt that cannot react with them or enter the particles, so that no charge crosses into the particles and the ions can only charge the pore walls. The impedance spectrum then follows a transmission line, a ladder of resistances along the pores and capacitances at their walls that we meet again in {{ch:lab/eis-measurement}}. Fitting it gives the ionic resistance R_\mathrm{ion} of the pores, and for the pair of electrodes \tau = R_\mathrm{ion} A \kappa \varepsilon / (2d), with A the area and d the thickness of one electrode.{{cite:R74}}

Measured this way, real electrodes are worse than the estimate. In one systematic study, every electrode tested had a MacMullin number larger than the Bruggeman relation predicts, by about 1.5 to 3 times in the authors’ summary and by more in some of their fits (figure {{fig:f5-8p}}). The measured curves fall with porosity at roughly the same power as the estimate, exponents of 1.1 to 1.6 against 1.5, but start three to five times lower.{{cite:R74}} A graphite electrode of porosity 0.43 gave \tau = 4.3 \pm 0.6, about three times the estimate of 1.5 (the fitted graphite curve in figure {{fig:f5-8p}}, drawn through electrodes of many porosities, runs a little lower than this single point). Graphite’s plate-like particles lie flat in the coating and hinder the way across: at similar porosities, electrodes of round NMC particles had MacMullin numbers of 10 to 11, against 18 to 19 for graphite. Particle size and the amount of conductive carbon matter too.{{cite:R74}}

Worked example. Let us find the porosity of a coating and what it leaves of the electrolyte’s conductivity. Take an illustrative NMC coating 50.0 µm thick with 15.6 mg of solids per cm²: 96 % NMC (density 4.7 g/cm³), 2 % carbon (2.2 g/cm³),{{cite:R74}} and 2 % binder, for which we assume 1.8 g/cm³. solids per cm²: 15.6\ \mathrm{mg} \times \left(\dfrac{0.96}{4.7} + \dfrac{0.02}{2.2} + \dfrac{0.02}{1.8}\right)\ \mathrm{cm^3/g} = 3.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^3} coating per cm²: 50.0\ \mathrm{\mu m} \times 1\ \mathrm{cm^2} = 5.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^3} porosity: \varepsilon = 1 - \dfrac{3.50}{5.00} = 0.30 estimate: \kappa_\mathrm{eff}/\kappa = \varepsilon^{1.5} = 0.30^{1.5} = 0.16 measured graphite: \kappa_\mathrm{eff}/\kappa = \varepsilon/\tau = 0.43/4.3 = 0.10, \text{against } 0.43^{1.5} = 0.28 Even by the estimate the coating keeps only a sixth of the liquid’s conductivity; the measured graphite electrode keeps a tenth, not the 28 % the estimate gives it.
measuring the porosity and tortuosity of a coating

Porosity, from a balance and a thickness gauge.

  1. Punch discs of the electrode, 11 mm across in one study.{{cite:R74}} Punch discs of the bare current-collector foil too: their mass and thickness are subtracted, because we want the coating alone.
  2. Weigh each disc on a microbalance (1 µg resolution in that study) and measure its thickness with a precision gauge. Thickness was the weaker measurement there: about ±2 µm, that is 4 to 10 % of a coating 20 to 50 µm thick, against ±0.01 mg/cm² for the mass.{{cite:R74}}
  3. From the composition and the bulk density of each ingredient, compute the volume of the solids and the porosity, \varepsilon = 1 - \frac{m}{Ad}\sum_i \frac{w_i}{\rho_i}, as in the worked example above.{{cite:R74}}

Tortuosity, from an impedance spectrum.

  1. Build a symmetric cell from two electrodes of closely matched loading and porosity, with a separator between them, in a T-cell or a pouch cell.{{cite:R74}}
  2. Fill it with a salt whose ions cannot react with the electrodes or enter them, such as tetrabutylammonium perchlorate in EC:DMC, so that no charge crosses into the particles. Measure the conductivity κ of this electrolyte separately at the same temperature.{{cite:R74}}
  3. Record the impedance around open circuit, for example from 200 kHz to 0.5 Hz with a 10 mV perturbation, at 25 °C.{{cite:R74}}
  4. Fit a transmission-line model with constant-phase elements, or extrapolate the low-frequency branch and the high-frequency intercept to the real axis: the gap between them is R_\mathrm{ion}/3.{{cite:R74}}
  5. Compute \tau = R_\mathrm{ion} A \kappa \varepsilon / (2d); the 2 is there because the spectrum holds both electrodes.{{cite:R74}}
Worked numbers: two graphite electrodes, each 63.2 µm thick with porosity 0.41 and an effective area of 2.37 cm², in an electrolyte of 1.74 mS/cm, gave R_\mathrm{ion} = 31.0\ \Omega for the pair.{{cite:R74}} So \tau = 31.0 \times 2.37 \times 0.00174 \times 0.41 / (2 \times 0.00632) \approx 4.1, with lengths in cm and κ in S/cm, close to the 4.3 ± 0.6 the study reports for graphite electrodes of porosity about 0.43.

This is why the two numbers matter. At high rates the performance of a cell is largely set by the ionic resistance of its electrodes and separator, filled with electrolyte, and porosity and tortuosity are the key to that resistance.{{cite:R74}} Pressing a coating therefore cuts both ways: more material in each litre, but less room for the ions and a longer way round. Porosity says how much room the ions have, tortuosity how far round they must go; together they divide the electrolyte’s conductivity inside the coating.

Now let us run a discharge through such a coating and look across its whole thickness. One model treats a composite of TiS₂ particles, themselves good electronic conductors, mixed with a polymer electrolyte, as two continua, the particles and the electrolyte, laid over each other. The modelled cell had a lithium foil, the same polymer electrolyte as separator and this TiS₂ positive electrode.{{cite:R62}}

The computed discharges show the main lesson at once. The faster the current, the less of the material is used before the voltage collapses: at 20 A/m² the voltage drops sharply when only about 30 % of the positive material has reacted.{{cite:R62}}

Where the reaction happens

Why does the material go unused? Let us follow where the reaction happens. At first it crowds near the separator. The particles carry electrons about 10⁵ times better than the polymer carries ions, so the short ionic path wins. As the particles at the front fill up, the reaction moves towards the middle of the electrode.{{cite:R62}}

Meanwhile the salt piles up near the lithium foil and is drawn down at the back of the electrode, where it cannot be replaced fast enough. There the conductivity falls and the material is poorly used.{{cite:R62}}

Two dimensionless numbers decide which transport matters, each a ratio of a transport time to the discharge time. For diffusion inside the modelled particles, of 1 µm radius, it was only 0.0001, so solid diffusion hardly mattered: the particles would have had to be as large as the electrode is thick. For the electrolyte it was 0.15 at 10 A/m², and at high currents it is the electrolyte that ends the discharge early.{{cite:R62}}

Worked example. Let us check the solid-diffusion number with the model’s parameters at 10 A/m²: particle radius 1.0 µm, solid diffusion coefficient 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ m²/s, porosity 0.3, maximum lithium concentration 29 000 mol/m³, electrode 100 µm thick. the number: S_\mathrm{c} = \dfrac{R_\mathrm{s}^2\, I}{D_\mathrm{s}\, F\, (1 - \varepsilon)\, c_\mathrm{T}\, \delta_\mathrm{c}} top: R_\mathrm{s}^2 I = (1.0\times10^{-6})^2 \times 10 = 1.0\times10^{-11} bottom: 5.0\times10^{-13} \times 96\,485 \times 0.7 \times 29\,000 \times 1.0\times10^{-4} \phantom{\text{bottom: }} = 9.8\times10^{-8} S_\mathrm{c} = \dfrac{1.0\times10^{-11}}{9.8\times10^{-8}} \approx 1.0 × 10⁻⁴, far below 1 The units cancel, so the number is a pure ratio: diffusion inside a particle takes about a ten-thousandth of the time the discharge takes.

The design lesson follows. Keeping the theoretical capacity fixed, a more open electrode (porosity 0.60 instead of 0.30, and so thicker; in figure {{fig:f5-8}}, 175 µm instead of 100 µm) raised the material used at 10 A/m² from 84 % to 97 %; 0.60 was the optimum found. In general, thinner electrodes use their material better when the electrolyte is the bottleneck, at the cost of harder processing and the extra weight of more, thinner cells.{{cite:R62}} A stronger starting salt solution was also suggested, because the 1 M presumably chosen for its conductivity is far weaker by the time it reaches the depths of the electrode.{{cite:R62}}

At high current the back of a thick electrode starves of ions, so the cell stops with material unused.

We now know enough to read a real curve. A voltage curve hides a lot in its gentle bends. Plot its slope instead and the bends become peaks. Two plots are used: the differential voltage \mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}Q against charge, and the differential capacity \mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}V against voltage.{{cite:R60}}

Peaks in differential capacity correspond to plateaus, where two phases are in equilibrium. Materials that take lithium as one smooth solid solution show no sharp peaks.{{cite:R60}} Put compactly: “A peak in the [dQ/dV] plot features the equilibrium between different electrochemically-active phases in the electrode whereas a peak in the [dV/dQ] plot locates the transition from one equilibrium to another.”{{cite:R60}}

Two electrodes in one curve

Where do the peaks come from? A cell’s voltage is the positive electrode’s potential minus the negative’s, each set by how much lithium that electrode holds:{{cite:R60}}

V = U_\mathrm{pos}(x_\mathrm{pos}) - U_\mathrm{neg}(x_\mathrm{neg}), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}Q} = \frac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{pos}}{\mathrm{d}Q} - \frac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{neg}}{\mathrm{d}Q}

The two electrodes’ slopes simply add, so every dV/dQ peak of the cell can be traced to one electrode.{{cite:R60}} A dQ/dV peak needs the voltage to stand still while charge flows, so both electrodes must be on a plateau at once.{{cite:R60}}

The square plot and ageing

To see both electrodes at once, we draw the state of a cell on a square plot: the negative electrode’s lithium content across, the positive electrode’s (inverted) up. A cell charging from empty to full is a straight line on it.{{cite:R60}} The slope of the line is the ratio of the electrodes’ capacities, and where it meets the left edge measures the lithium the cell has lost for good.{{cite:R60}}

1 - x_\mathrm{pos} = a\, x_\mathrm{neg} + b

Now let the cell age. Ageing comes in two classic kinds. Loss of lithium inventory (LLI) is cyclable lithium used up by side reactions. Loss of active material (LAM) is material that has changed irreversibly, dissolved or come loose from the current collector.{{cite:R60}} LLI moves the line up; LAM tilts it, losing positive material steepens it and losing negative material flattens it. The window between the cut-offs then picks out a shorter piece of line, so the capacity falls.{{cite:R60}}

In one LiCoO₂ and graphite example, lost lithium leaves the graphite peaks where they were on the charge axis but moves the LiCoO₂ peaks to lower charge.{{cite:R60}} The toy cell does the same. Differentiating real data is sensitive to noise: in that analysis, slow C/20 data were thinned 144-fold before differentiating, to reduce the noise.{{cite:R60}}

Slopes turn a smooth curve into peaks that can be traced to each electrode, and their shifts tell lost lithium from lost material.

Ageing leads to a practical question: how long does a cell last? Two numbers summarize a cell’s life. The Coulombic efficiency is the charge returned on discharge divided by the charge put in on charge, as a percentage; the cycle life is the number of cycles until the capacity has faded to 80 % of its initial value.{{cite:R6}}

\mathrm{CE} = 100 \times \frac{Q_\mathrm{dis}}{Q_\mathrm{ch}}\ \%

In 2004 a commercial rechargeable cell was expected to survive at least 300 full cycles with less than 20 % loss; most needed three to eight hours to charge while users wanted a quarter of an hour, and fast charging shortens cycle life.{{cite:R1}} LiFePO₄, with a much longer cycle life and calendar life than most NMC cells, is now used for grid storage and shorter-range electric vehicles.{{cite:R73}} The fast-charging goal is 80 % of the charge in under 15 minutes.{{cite:R70}}

Not every loss is permanent. Capacity hidden by a high rate comes back at a low one; what does not come back is lost to changes in the electrodes’ volume, to reactions between electrodes and electrolyte, and to decomposition of the electrodes.{{cite:R6}} The first charge of a lithium-ion cell is a special case, a loss paid once ({{ch:sei}}).{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. Let us see what the 300-cycle benchmark asks of each cycle. How good must the Coulombic efficiency be for a cell to keep 80 % of its capacity after 300 cycles? Take the simplest case, in which every bit of charge that does not come back is lost for good. after 1 cycle: \mathrm{CE}; after 2 cycles: \mathrm{CE}\times\mathrm{CE} = \mathrm{CE}^2; after N cycles: \mathrm{CE}^N the goal: \mathrm{CE}^{300} = 0.80 take the 300th root: \mathrm{CE} = 0.80^{1/300} = 0.99926, that is 99.93 % the other way round, at 99.9 %: 0.999^N = 0.80 \;\Rightarrow\; N = \dfrac{\ln\,0.80}{\ln\,0.999} = \dfrac{-0.223}{-0.001} = 223 cycles So losing just one coulomb in a thousand on every cycle uses up the 20 % allowance after 223 cycles, well short of 300.

Lifetime is set by tiny losses repeated hundreds of times.

One cost of current is still to be followed: the energy lost on every cycle. The lost energy becomes heat, of two kinds. An irreversible part, often loosely called Joule heat, comes from the whole polarization: it is released whichever way the current flows. A reversible part comes from the entropy change of the reaction, and its sign follows the temperature coefficient of the voltage, \mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}T. If it is positive the cell heats on charge and cools on discharge, as nickel-cadmium does; lead-acid has a negative coefficient and does the opposite.{{cite:R1}} Bernardi’s energy balance writes the two side by side:{{cite:R40}}

\dot{Q} = I\,(U - V) - I\,T\,\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}T}

The heat is released inside the cell, at the electrode surfaces, so a cell meant for high currents has to be built to shed it.{{cite:R1}} Because the entropic term changes sign with the current, the heat it takes in on one half of a cycle it gives back on the other; the irreversible term only ever adds up.{{cite:R40}}

You can now explain how a cycler charges and discharges a cell and what a C-rate is, why a working cell gives less than its open-circuit voltage and why charging takes more, what the three polarizations are and how fast each relaxes, how a battery’s curve differs from a capacitor’s, why some curves are flat and others slope, why a flat curve tilts at high rate and can remember its past, why fast discharges leave material unused, how slopes of the curve reveal ageing, why 99.9 % per cycle is not enough, and where the lost energy goes.

So far we have watched the cell from outside, through its voltage. To see why the curve has the shape it has, let us follow our ion inside the cell.

Go deeper: the exchange current, the Butler-Volmer equation and the Tafel line

For one electrode, the current and the overpotential \eta = E - E_\mathrm{eq} are linked by the Butler-Volmer equation. Written with reduction current positive, it reads{{cite:B2}}

i = i_0\left[\exp(-\alpha f \eta) - \exp\big((1 - \alpha) f \eta\big)\right], \qquad f = \frac{F}{RT}

with \alpha the transfer coefficient (between 0.3 and 0.7 in most cases) and i_0 the exchange current. It holds while the current stays below about 10 % of the limiting current, so that mass transport does not interfere.{{cite:B2}} Near equilibrium the exponentials can be expanded and the current is simply proportional to \eta: the electrode behaves like a resistor, the charge-transfer resistance R_\mathrm{ct} = RT/(F i_0) for a one-electron reaction. A large exchange current means a small R_\mathrm{ct}.{{cite:B2}}

Far from equilibrium one exponential wins and \eta grows with the logarithm of the current, the Tafel relation \eta = a + b \log i, which holds once |\eta| exceeds about 118 mV at 25 °C; the same empirical form is quoted for batteries.{{cite:B2,R1}} In the battery literature it is written with oxidation current positive (the printed equation lost a bracket and a sign); the physics is the same with the roles of \alpha and 1 - \alpha swapped.{{cite:B2,R1}}

One caution about the heat equation. It takes the open-circuit voltage U from a slow measurement and \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}T from small temperature steps, and in one set of measurements it estimated heat reasonably during continuous discharge but overestimated it during pulsed discharge, by up to 26 % for a LiFePO₄ cell and 49 % for an NMC cell.{{cite:R40}}

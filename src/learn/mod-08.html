Module 8

Electrodes are composites

Why is an electrode not just a block of the good stuff?

Where we are. Our ion has crossed the electrolyte (module 7) and has to get into a particle of the positive electrode (module 6). Module 2 showed that an electrode is a coating of particles, carbon, binder and pores. This module asks what each of those is for, and what happens when one of them fails.

Most battery electrodes are porous composites: particles of the active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon or a metal powder, and a polymer binder, coated on a current collector. About 30 % of the coating is pore space, so the surface where the reaction can happen is far larger than the electrode’s geometric area.{{cite:R1}} A large surface lowers the current density at each point of it, and with it the polarization.{{cite:R1}}

Each ingredient keeps one road open. The carbon carries electrons from the foil to every particle; the electrolyte in the pores carries ions from the separator to every particle; the binder holds the coating together and onto the foil. A particle that one of the two roads cannot reach stores nothing and only adds mass.

A picture to keep: a town where every house needs two roads, one for each of two deliveries that must arrive together, and is only lived in if both reach it. The picture breaks because here the roads also have tolls: the farther a delivery travels through the coating, the more voltage it costs, which is the next figure.

A particle that the electrons or the ions cannot reach contributes nothing.

Inside a porous electrode the reaction is not spread evenly. Where it happens depends on the conductivities of the solid matrix and of the electrolyte, the exchange current, diffusion, the thickness of the electrode, its porosity, pore size and tortuosity; and a nonuniform current distribution wastes active material.{{cite:R1}}

The reaction concentrates where the electrons and the ions together have the shortest, cheapest way to meet. In the simplest theory the profile is a hyperbolic cosine with a characteristic depth; Pathak and Bazant find this reaction-limited picture most realistic for thin electrodes, of order 10 μm, and in need of corrections beyond about 100 μm.{{cite:R41}}

The electrolyte inside the pores conducts worse than in bulk, because the ions have less room and a winding path. Boz and co-workers give the usual correction, κeff = κεα, with ε the porosity and α = 1.5 (1.53 for LiFePO₄ electrodes).{{cite:R36}} Ionic transport is far slower than electronic transport, which is also why cells use thin electrodes of large area.{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. How well does the electrolyte conduct inside a 30 % porous electrode? κeff/κ = 0.301.5 = 0.164 for a liquid of 10⁻² S/cm: κeff = 1.6 × 10⁻³ S/cm, about a sixth Every micrometre of coating the ions must cross costs six times the voltage it would in the open electrolyte; another reason the reaction in figure 8.2 crowds toward the separator.{{cite:R36,R1}}

An active material that conducts electrons poorly works only where it touches the conductive additive, and the reacted zone spreads outward from that contact; Winter and Brodd show it for MnO₂.{{cite:R1}} LiFePO₄ is the modern case: its poor electronic conductivity was overcome by coating the particles with carbon and making them small.{{cite:R6}}

The coating Armand and co-workers introduced is made by decomposing a polymer precursor on the particles, which leaves a uniform layer of carbon;{{cite:R44}} the once dead-end material is now produced in thousands of tonnes a year.{{cite:R44,R47}}

Small particles bring the ions closer to every atom: the time to cross a particle falls with the square of its size (module 6). But carbon-coated nanoparticles have a lower tap density, so the electrode holds less energy per litre, and coating them evenly gets harder as they shrink.{{cite:R6,B2}} Highly porous aerogel electrodes pay the same price, at a tap density of about 0.2 g/cm³.{{cite:R2}}

The composite of this module is made in a few industrial steps. The powders, about 90 % active material by mass with conductive carbon and a polymer binder, are mixed into a slurry, coated on the current-collector foil, dried so that the pores form, and pressed between rollers to an even, denser layer; the electrodes are then cut, stacked or wound with the separator, filled with electrolyte, and cycled for the first time to form the SEI.{{cite:R48}}

Every step leaves its mark on the roads of figure 8.1: where the carbon and binder end up, how open the pores stay, how well the electrolyte wets them.{{cite:R48}}

You can now explain what each ingredient of an electrode coating is for, why a particle without both roads is dead weight, where the reaction happens inside a thick electrode and why, why poorly conducting materials need carbon, and what small particles gain and cost.

The round trip works. Now let us see how it fails.

Go deeper: the porous electrode in one equation

Write the current in the solid i1 and in the electrolyte i2; their sum is the cell current, and the electrolyte current grows through the thickness by whatever the reaction converts, di2/dx = a j, with a the reacting area per volume. Ohm’s law in each phase, dφ1/dx = −i1/σ and dφ2/dx = −i2/κ, and the linearized kinetics of module 5, j = η/Rct, with η = φ1 − φ2 − U the local overpotential, give d²η/dx² = (ν/L)²η, with ν² = aL²(1/σ + 1/κ)/Rct, the same balance of current that underlies the porous-electrode equations reviewed by Boz and co-workers.{{cite:B2,R36}} Its solution is the cosh profile of figure 8.2, and 1/ν is the reaction depth as a fraction of the thickness. Pathak and Bazant analyse this reaction-limited case and its depth scale in full; when the depth is much larger than the electrode the overpotential is uniform.{{cite:R41}}

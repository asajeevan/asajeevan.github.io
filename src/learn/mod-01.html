What a battery does

Why does the lamp light when you close the switch, and why not when it is open?

Close the switch. Zinc atoms at the left electrode give up two electrons each and dissolve into the liquid as zinc ions; at the right electrode copper ions take two electrons each and settle as copper metal. The electrons cannot cross the liquid, so they take the wire, and the lamp lights on the way.{{cite:R1,B2}} Inside the cell the current is carried by ions: cations drift toward the copper side and anions toward the zinc side, so no charge piles up anywhere.{{cite:R1,R6}}

Open the switch and everything stops, but nothing is lost. The push is still there: it is a property of the two materials and the electrolyte between them. The voltage you measure with nothing connected is called the open-circuit voltage,{{cite:R1}} and for this pair of electrodes it is 1.10 V.{{cite:B2}} The wire only lets the push act.

That is the whole trick, in Goodenough and Park’s words: the electrolyte “conducts the ionic component of the chemical reaction between the anode and the cathode, but it forces the electronic component to traverse an external circuit where it does work”.{{cite:R6}}

A battery is a chemical reaction that is only allowed to happen through a wire.

A battery keeps its reacting materials inside, so it is a closed system: storage and conversion happen in the same box. A fuel cell is an open system, fed with fuel and oxidant from outside, so its energy is stored in a tank and converted in the cell. A supercapacitor stores charge in the electrical double layers at its electrode surfaces, without a chemical reaction.{{cite:R1}}

One thing they share sets them apart from engines: none of them turns heat into work, so none is bound by the Carnot limit that caps every heat engine.{{cite:R1}}

Strictly, one of these units is a cell, and several connected together are a battery, although the two words are used loosely.{{cite:R1}} Connecting cells in series adds their voltages; connecting them in parallel adds their current and their run time.{{cite:R6}} Everything in this story happens inside a single cell.

You can now explain why a battery has a voltage even when nothing is connected, why the wire matters, and what separates a battery from a fuel cell and a supercapacitor.

Go deeper: primary and secondary cells

A primary battery is assembled in the charged state and used until exhausted. A secondary, or rechargeable, battery can be restored to its charged condition by a current flowing in the opposite direction to the discharge current; since secondary batteries are usually assembled in the discharged state, they must be charged before their first use.{{cite:R1}}

Which reactions can be run backwards, and which cannot, is the subject of module 6.

What a battery does

Why does the lamp light when you close the switch, and why not when it is open?

Where we are. Module 0 gave you the volt (a joule per coulomb), the field (the push per unit charge), the conductor at rest (one potential throughout) and the two kinds of carrier: electrons in metals, ions in liquids. Now two different metals go into a liquid, and a potential difference appears between them.

Close the switch. Zinc atoms at the left electrode give up two electrons each and dissolve into the liquid as Zn²⁺ ions; at the right electrode Cu²⁺ ions take two electrons each and settle as copper metal.{{cite:R1,B2}} Each of those is a half-reaction, and neither can happen alone: each must be coupled, through a cell, to a second half-reaction running the other way.{{cite:B2}} The electrons cannot cross the liquid, so they take the wire, from the more negative electrode to the more positive, and the lamp lights on the way.{{cite:B2}} Inside the cell the current is carried by ions: cations drift toward the copper and sulfate toward the zinc, so no charge piles up anywhere.{{cite:R1,B2}}

Open the switch and everything stops, but nothing is lost. The voltage you measure with nothing else connected is the open-circuit voltage,{{cite:R1}} and a high-impedance voltmeter can read it without drawing any appreciable current.{{cite:B2}} For this pair of metals it is 1.10 V: the difference between the standard potentials of the two half-reactions, +0.340 V for copper and −0.763 V for zinc.{{cite:B2}} Where a potential sits inside the cell is the strip under the figure: it jumps at each metal|liquid boundary, in a zone so narrow that a very strong field exists there, and stays flat through the liquid; the voltmeter reads the sum of the jumps along the path from one electrode to the other.{{cite:B2}}

That is the whole trick, in Goodenough and Park’s words: the electrolyte “conducts the ionic component of the chemical reaction between the anode and the cathode, but it forces the electronic component to traverse an external circuit where it does work”.{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. Where does 1.10 V come from, and what does it buy? E = E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) − E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = 0.340 V − (−0.763 V) = 1.10 V energy per electron round the circuit = 1.10 eV; two electrons per zinc atom per mole of zinc: 2 × 96 485 C × 1.10 V = 213 kJ Module 3 shows that this 213 kJ is the free energy of the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, and module 4 turns the “per mole” into “per gram”.

A battery is a chemical reaction that is only allowed to happen through a wire.

Why do the electrons choose the wire? Because they have no other road. In a metal, charge is carried by electrons moving through a lattice of fixed ion cores; in an electrolyte it is carried by ions, and the “free” electrons a liquid might briefly hold are so short-lived that they contribute nothing to its conductivity.{{cite:B2}} At the boundary between the two, charge can pass only if the two kinds of carrier are linked by an electrode reaction: electrons consumed or produced in the metal, ions produced or consumed in the liquid.{{cite:B2}}

The field does the rest. One field points from the positive side to the negative side; it pushes a positive ion along itself and an electron against itself, so the cations in the liquid and the electrons in the wire go round the circuit in opposite senses and meet again at the electrodes.{{cite:B2}} In the wire, the potential difference that drives the electrons through the lamp is the one you met in figure 0.4; in the liquid, the potential slopes gently and drives the ions.{{cite:B2}}

Electrons travel in metals, ions travel in liquids, and only a reaction can hand charge from one to the other.

Strictly, one of these units is a cell, and several connected together are a battery, although the two words are used loosely.{{cite:R1}} Connecting cells in series adds their voltages; connecting them in parallel adds their current and their run time.{{cite:R6}} The strip says why: a potential difference between two points is the sum of the drops and rises along any path between them,{{cite:B2}} so three cells in a chain give three rises, while three cells side by side each give the same single rise but share the current.

A battery keeps its reacting materials inside, so it is a closed system: storage and conversion happen in the same box. A fuel cell is an open system, fed with fuel and oxidant from outside, so its energy is stored in a tank and converted in the cell. A supercapacitor stores charge in the electrical double layers at its electrode surfaces, without a chemical reaction.{{cite:R1}} None of the three turns heat into work, so none is bound by the Carnot limit that caps every heat engine.{{cite:R1}} Everything on this page happens inside a single cell.

You can now explain why a cell has a voltage even when nothing is connected, where inside the cell that voltage sits, why the electrons take the wire and the ions the liquid, and why voltages add in series.

Go deeper: primary and secondary cells

A primary battery is assembled in the charged state and used until exhausted. A secondary, or rechargeable, battery can be restored to its charged condition by a current flowing in the opposite direction to the discharge current; since secondary batteries are usually assembled in the discharged state, they must be charged before their first use.{{cite:R1}} To charge, a power supply is connected across the cell and its voltage is adjusted; the interfacial potential differences shift, especially at the electrode|electrolyte boundaries, and current can be driven in either direction.{{cite:B2}} Which reactions can be run backwards, and which cannot, is the subject of module 6.

Module 1

What a battery does

Why does the lamp light when you close the switch, and why not when it is open?

Where we are. {{Ch:electrostatics}} gave you the volt (a joule per coulomb), the field (the push per unit charge), the conductor at rest (one potential throughout) and the two kinds of carrier: electrons in metals, ions in liquids. Now two different metals go into a liquid, and a potential difference appears between them.

Close the switch. Zinc atoms at the left electrode give up two electrons each and dissolve into the liquid as Zn²⁺ ions; at the right electrode Cu²⁺ ions take two electrons each and settle as copper metal.{{cite:R1,B2}} Each of those is a half-reaction, and neither can happen alone: each must be coupled, through a cell, to a second half-reaction running the other way.{{cite:B2}}

The electrons cannot cross the liquid, so they take the wire, from the more negative electrode to the more positive, and the lamp lights on the way.{{cite:B2}} Inside the cell the current is carried by ions: cations drift toward the copper and sulfate toward the zinc, so no charge piles up anywhere.{{cite:R1,B2}}

Open the switch and everything stops, but nothing is lost. The voltage you measure with nothing else connected is the open-circuit voltage,{{cite:R1}} and a high-impedance voltmeter can read it without drawing any appreciable current.{{cite:B2}} For this pair of metals it is 1.10 V: the difference between the standard potentials of the two half-reactions, +0.340 V for copper and −0.763 V for zinc.{{cite:B2}}

Where a potential sits inside the cell is the strip under the figure: it jumps at each metal|liquid boundary, in a zone so narrow that a very strong field exists there, and stays flat through the liquid; the voltmeter reads the sum of the jumps along the path from one electrode to the other.{{cite:B2}}

{{Ch:cell-voltage}} shows how each jump is built: the chemistry moves a minute charge across the boundary, and the field of that separated charge is what the strip shows.

That is the whole trick, in Goodenough and Park’s words: the electrolyte “conducts the ionic component of the chemical reaction between the anode and the cathode, but it forces the electronic component to traverse an external circuit where it does work”.{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. Where does 1.10 V come from, and what does it buy? E = E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) − E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = 0.340 V − (−0.763 V) = 1.10 V energy per electron round the circuit = 1.10 eV; two electrons per zinc atom per mole of zinc: 2 × 96 485 C × 1.10 V = 213 kJ {{Ch:cell-voltage}} shows that this 213 kJ is the free energy of the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, and {{ch:capacity-energy}} turns the “per mole” into “per gram”.

A battery is a chemical reaction that is only allowed to happen through a wire.

Why do the electrons choose the wire? Because they have no other road. In a metal, charge is carried by electrons moving through a lattice of fixed ion cores; in an electrolyte it is carried by ions, and the “free” electrons a liquid might briefly hold are so short-lived that they contribute nothing to its conductivity.{{cite:B2}}

At the boundary between the two, charge can pass only if the two kinds of carrier are linked by an electrode reaction: electrons consumed or produced in the metal, ions produced or consumed in the liquid.{{cite:B2}}

The field does the rest. One field points from the positive side to the negative side; it pushes a positive ion along itself and an electron against itself, so the cations in the liquid and the electrons in the wire go round the circuit in opposite senses and meet again at the electrodes.{{cite:B2}}

In the wire, the potential difference that drives the electrons through the lamp is the one you met in figure {{fig:f0-4}}; in the liquid, the potential slopes gently and drives the ions.{{cite:B2}}

Electrons travel in metals, ions travel in liquids, and only a reaction can hand charge from one to the other.

What makes a battery a battery?

We have used the word battery freely. It now covers systems that store energy in very different ways, and a 2026 Comment by Villevieille, Novák and Girault asks which of them deserve it.{{cite:R81}} Let us start with the official words. IUPAC gives no formal definition of a battery: it defines an electrochemical cell as a system of at least two electron conductors, the electrodes, in contact with ionic conductors, the electrolytes, and mentions batteries only as examples of such cells. The International Electrotechnical Commission defines a battery as one or more electrochemical cells fitted with the devices needed for use, such as a case, terminals, marking and protective devices.{{cite:R81}}

Those definitions say how a battery is built, not how it works. The Comment adds five operational criteria, all of which must hold. C1: the system is closed, so the electroactive species stay inside it. C2: energy is stored and released through reversible oxidation and reduction, that is, through faradaic reactions; the current they carry is what IUPAC calls a faradaic current, the current that results from the electrooxidation or electroreduction of an electroactive substance. C3: oxidation and reduction happen at distinct electrodes, separated by an ion-conducting medium. C4: ions migrate through the electrolyte while electrons flow through an external circuit. C5: stored chemical energy is delivered as electrical energy through that circuit.{{cite:R81}} On discharge, oxidation takes place at the negative electrode and reduction at the positive one; on charge, the other way round.{{cite:R81}}

Applied to real systems, the criteria sort them cleanly. Redox-flow cells pass even though their two liquids sit in tanks outside the cell, because the reactants stay inside the system; the liquids are called the negolyte and the posolyte, not anolyte and catholyte, because anode and cathode swap between charge and discharge. Fuel cells do not pass: fuel and oxidant are fed from outside, so the system is open. Metal-air cells, whose positive electrode takes its oxygen from the air, are hybrids. Capacitors and supercapacitors store charge without faradaic reactions and are not batteries; a pseudocapacitor, which stores charge through fast faradaic reactions confined to surfaces while behaving like a capacitor, meets two of the tests only in part and is not a battery either.{{cite:R81}}

The same test settles the stranger uses of the word. The “thermal battery” of the handbooks is a battery: a primary cell whose salt electrolyte is solid at room temperature and is melted with pyrotechnic material to start it. A store of heat sold as a “thermal battery” is not, because it makes no electricity without a turbine and generator; nor are nuclear, quantum and mechanical “batteries” such as a flywheel, which deliver electricity without electrochemistry. A “structural battery”, which carries mechanical loads as well as storing energy, passes all five tests.{{cite:R81}}

A provocation about graphite. The table marks lithium-ion cells with a graphite negative electrode as only partly meeting C2. Citing a recent analysis, the Comment argues that no redox reaction tied to energy storage takes place in graphite, which works instead as a lithium-ion volumic capacitor; it still classes these cells as batteries.{{cite:R81}} This is one group’s proposal, not settled usage, and in this course we keep the usual picture of lithium inserted into graphite ({{ch:insertion-hosts}}).

From Volta’s pile to the lithium-ion cell

The cell of figure {{fig:f1-1}} has a long history. Around 1800 Alessandro Volta built the first real battery, a pile of zinc and silver sheets separated by cloth soaked in salt water. In 1836 John Frederic Daniell put a copper electrode in copper(II) sulfate and a zinc electrode in zinc sulfate, the two solutions separated by a porous partition: our figure {{fig:f1-1}}, with Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu as its cell reaction.{{cite:B3}}

The first rechargeable battery to be produced commercially was Gaston Planté’s lead-acid cell of 1859: lead and lead dioxide in dilute sulfuric acid, PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ + Pb ⇌ 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O.{{cite:B3}} It still serves in cars and uninterruptible power supplies.{{cite:R82}} In 1866 Georges Leclanché’s zinc-carbon cell paired zinc with manganese dioxide and carbon in ammonium chloride solution; for commercial use the liquid was replaced by a paste, and the first dry cell, a variant of the Leclanché cell, followed in 1886.{{cite:B3,R82}} In 1901 Waldemar Jungner and Thomas Edison introduced nickel-cadmium and nickel-iron cells. For many years nickel-cadmium was the rechargeable cell of choice, until the toxicity of cadmium sent the search elsewhere.{{cite:B3}}

Lithium was the next step: the lightest of these metals, with a very negative electrode potential. Sony brought out the first commercial lithium-ion battery in 1991, with LiCoO₂ as the positive electrode and carbon as the negative, and in 2019 John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of lithium-ion batteries.{{cite:B3,R82}} {{Ch:cell-anatomy}} follows the insertion electrodes behind that cell from 1972 on (figure {{fig:f6-5}}).

Strictly, one of these units is a cell, and several connected together are a battery, although the two words are used loosely.{{cite:R1}} Connecting cells in series adds their voltages; connecting them in parallel adds their current and their run time.{{cite:R6}} The strip says why the voltages add: a potential difference between two points is the sum of the rises and drops along any path between them,{{cite:B2}} so three cells in a chain give three rises of V, and a coulomb that travels the whole chain picks up energy at each one.

Why the capacity does not add in series is a matter of counting coulombs. The cells in a chain carry one and the same current: the charge that leaves the first cell has nowhere to go but through the second and the third, so every coulomb drawn from the chain is a coulomb drawn from each cell. When one cell has given up its Q, they all have. In parallel the picture is reversed. All three + terminals are tied together and all three − terminals are tied together, so the ends of the battery see one potential difference, V, and no more; but the current is shared, each cell supplies a third of it, and together they can deliver three times the charge before they are empty. Series adds the rises along the path; parallel adds the paths.

A battery keeps its reacting materials inside, so it is a closed system: storage and conversion happen in the same box. A fuel cell is an open system, fed with fuel and oxidant from outside, so its energy is stored in a tank and converted in the cell. A supercapacitor stores charge in the electrical double layers at its electrode surfaces, without a chemical reaction.{{cite:R1}}

None of the three turns heat into work, so none is bound by the Carnot limit that caps every heat engine.{{cite:R1}} Everything on this page happens inside a single cell.

You can now explain why a cell has a voltage even when nothing is connected, where inside the cell that voltage sits, why the electrons take the wire and the ions the liquid, and why voltages add in series.

Go deeper: primary and secondary cells

A primary battery is assembled in the charged state and used until exhausted. A secondary, or rechargeable, battery can be restored to its charged condition by a current flowing in the opposite direction to the discharge current; since secondary batteries are usually assembled in the discharged state, they must be charged before their first use.{{cite:R1}} To charge, a power supply is connected across the cell and its voltage is adjusted; the interfacial potential differences shift, especially at the electrode|electrolyte boundaries, and current can be driven in either direction.{{cite:B2}} Which reactions can be run backwards, and which cannot, is a question for the modules still to come.

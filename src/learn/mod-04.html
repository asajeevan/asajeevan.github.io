Capacity, energy and power

How long would your cell run a lamp?

Capacity is a count of electrons. Faraday’s law ties the amount of material transformed to the charge that flows, mass = I·t·M/(nF), and it has “no known exceptions”.{{cite:R1}} Turn it around: a gram of a material of molar mass M that gives up n electrons per formula unit holds nF/M coulombs, which the battery world writes in milliampere-hours per gram, Q = nF/(3.6 M). The Faraday constant is the charge of one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 C or 26.8015 Ah.{{cite:R1}}

Two conventions hide in that formula. Graphite’s 372 mAh/g refers to the mass of carbon, the host as assembled, not to the mass of LiC₆: Tarascon and Armand give 372 mAh/g for the end compound LiC₆ and about 350 mAh/g in practice.{{cite:R2}} Positive-electrode materials are referred to their lithiated mass: LiFePO₄ holds 170 mAh/g in theory{{cite:R6}} and is used at about 90 % of it,{{cite:R2}} while LiCoO₂ is limited to about half of its lithium for safety, 140 mAh/g in practice.{{cite:R2}}

Light elements and many electrons per formula unit give high capacity; high voltage turns capacity into energy.

Energy is the integral of the voltage over the charge delivered: on a discharge curve, the area under the line, which is also the average voltage times the capacity.{{cite:R6}} Per kilogram it is called specific energy (Wh/kg), per litre energy density (Wh/L); how fast it can be delivered is specific power (W/kg) and power density (W/L).{{cite:R1}}

The capacity itself depends on how hard you pull. At high current the transfer of ions across the interfaces becomes diffusion-limited and the cell reaches less of its charge before the cut-off; that part of the loss is reversible and comes back at a gentler rate.{{cite:R6}} Module 5 takes the curve apart.

To compare the two abilities, energy and power, engineers use the Ragone plot. On it, supercapacitors are high-power systems, fuel cells high-energy systems, and batteries sit between them, overlapping both; no single electrochemical system matches the combustion engine on both axes at once, which is why the review suggests combining them.{{cite:R1}}

As a rule of thumb, a rechargeable battery delivers about 25 % of its theoretical energy, and a primary battery more than 50 %. The difference has three causes: the inert parts a cell needs, such as conductive diluents, current collectors and containers; the internal resistances of electrodes, electrolyte and contacts; and incomplete use of the active masses.{{cite:R1}}

To give the number a feel, Winter and Brodd note that a specific energy of 30 Wh/kg means one kilogram of lead-acid battery can power a 60 W lamp for only half an hour.{{cite:R1}} The first commercial lithium-ion cell of 1991 gave over 3.6 V and 120 to 150 Wh/kg, two to three times the usual nickel-cadmium cell of the day.{{cite:R2}}

You can now explain where a capacity in mAh/g comes from, why energy is an area, what the Ragone plot shows, and why practice gives a quarter of theory.

Go deeper: the mass basis, and 372 against 339

Run Faraday’s law on the lithiated compound LiC₆ (79.0 g/mol) and you get 339 mAh/g; run it on the carbon host C₆ (72.07 g/mol) and you get 372 mAh/g. Both are correct; they answer different questions. The convention in the field is to refer negative-electrode hosts to their delithiated mass and positive-electrode materials to their lithiated mass, which is how each is weighed when the cell is built.{{cite:R2,R6}} Whenever you compare capacities, check the basis first.

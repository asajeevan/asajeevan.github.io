Module 9

Negative electrodes: from graphite to lithium metal

If lithium metal is the best negative electrode, why is it not in your phone?

Where we are. Act B followed our ion through a lithium-ion cell that works. Act C is about how that journey goes wrong and what is being done about it. It starts with the negative electrode, the side that holds the lithium when the cell is charged: graphite, which made the first lithium-ion cell possible, and the materials that might replace it, silicon, conversion materials and lithium metal itself, then the ions that might one day replace lithium.

The negative electrode stores the lithium while the cell is charged and gives it back on discharge. It was the negative electrode that made the first lithium-ion cell possible: graphite held lithium reversibly and stayed stable in carbonate electrolytes, where lithium metal did not.{{cite:R70}} Its 372 mAh/g now limits how much energy a cell can hold, and the search for something better has produced four families of material, named for how they store lithium: intercalation hosts, alloys, conversion materials and lithium metal.{{cite:R70}}

A good negative electrode needs a high reversible capacity, a low potential, fast kinetics, low cost, stability against the electrolyte, a stable SEI and a light environmental footprint.{{cite:R70}} On the first two counts lithium metal and silicon stand out; both still face serious problems at the level of the material, the electrode and the cell.{{cite:R70}}

Worked example. Ten times the capacity, but not ten times the energy. Take a positive electrode of 200 mAh/g at 3.8 V (illustrative). The same charge passes through both electrodes, so a gram of the pair passes Q = QposQneg/(Qpos + Qneg). graphite: Q = \frac{200 \times 372}{200 + 372} = 130\ \mathrm{mAh/g}, at 3.8 − 0.15 = 3.65 V: 475 Wh/kg lithium metal: Q = \frac{200 \times 3860}{200 + 3860} = 190\ \mathrm{mAh/g}, at 3.8 V: 723 Wh/kg A factor of 1.5, because the positive electrode now limits the cell. Rahman and co-workers put the gain from lithium metal at up to a factor of two over today’s lithium-ion cells.{{cite:R70}}

A negative electrode should sit low and store a lot, but past a point the positive electrode sets the limit.

In intercalation, lithium slips between the layers of a host that keeps its structure, as in graphite, Li⁺ + e⁻ + xC ⇌ LiCx, or in titanium oxides; that is why these materials last for many cycles. In alloying, lithium forms an alloy with an element such as silicon, germanium or tin, xLi⁺ + xe⁻ + M ⇌ LixM. In conversion, a compound is taken apart into metal and a lithium compound and rebuilt on the way back. Alloys and conversion materials tend to break into smaller particles as they cycle.{{cite:R70}}

All three lose a large part of their first charge, mostly to the SEI, which takes lithium from the positive electrode and lowers the first-cycle Coulombic efficiency. Alloys and conversion materials also show a large gap between the voltage at which lithium goes in and the voltage at which it comes out, which lowers the energy a full cell can deliver.{{cite:R70}}

Graphite, still the workhorse

Sony’s first lithium-ion cell of 1991 paired graphite with LiCoO₂, and three decades later graphite, alone or with some silicon, is still the negative electrode of high-energy cells, now facing nickel-rich layered oxides.{{cite:R70}} It is cheap, conducts electrons well, and lithium goes in at about 0.15 V with only 10 to 50 mV of hysteresis.{{cite:R70}}

Its one real weakness is that it works below the potential at which the electrolyte is stable. On the first charge the electrolyte is reduced on its surface and leaves the SEI (module 6), consuming lithium; once the layer is complete it protects the graphite and lets it cycle.{{cite:R70}} Changes at this interface are a major cause of ageing in commercial cells. Rahman and co-workers list five: exfoliation and cracking of the graphite, an SEI that keeps forming and dissolving, SEI conversion and growth, SEI dissolution, and lithium plating.{{cite:R70}} Graphite particles also crack along their grain boundaries as they expand and shrink, exposing new surface that builds more SEI.{{cite:R70}}

Temperature decides which route dominates: hot, the SEI grows thicker and consumes lithium and electrolyte; cold, lithium plates on the graphite instead of entering it.{{cite:R70}} Module 10 follows the growth of the SEI over time.

Where graphite comes from. Natural graphite costs about $8000 to $11 000 per tonne, synthetic graphite about $20 000; China produces nearly 75 % of the world’s graphite, which is why the United States and Europe are building their own supply. Demand is expected to reach about 1800 kt by 2030.{{cite:R70}}

Charging fast

Electric cars, drones, electric aircraft and grid storage all want faster charging; industry aims to reach 80 % in under 15 minutes without losing cycle life or safety.{{cite:R70}} On the negative electrode three things go wrong. Lithium can arrive faster than it can enter the graphite and plate as metal, a risk made worse by graphite’s low potential; the particles are stressed and crack; and the heat destabilizes the SEI.{{cite:R70}}

The remedies run from material to cell: smaller particles and porous structures; thin, porous electrodes of low tortuosity; electrolytes with a LiFSI salt or an FEC additive, which improve the SEI and reduce plating; and, at cell level, the right ratio of negative to positive capacity (the N/P ratio), the charging protocol and cooling.{{cite:R70}} Silicon-based negative electrodes are being developed for fast charging too: one company reports silicon nanowires charged from 0 to 80 % in under 6 minutes, another columnar silicon grown on copper charged to 80 % in 7 minutes at 1350 Wh/L.{{cite:R70}}

The other route is to move away from 0 V. Lithium titanate, Li₄Ti₅O₁₂, works at about 1.55 V, where little SEI forms, and changes volume by only about 0.2 %, a “zero-strain” material: it is safe, long-lived and fast, but stores only 175 mAh/g, conducts poorly and can give off gas (H₂, CO₂, CO) by decomposing the electrolyte on its surface, a mechanism not yet well understood.{{cite:R70}} Niobium oxides such as Nb₂O₅, TiNb₂O₇ and Nb₁₄W₃O₄₄ also work at 1 to 2 V, store more (about 200 to 350 mAh/g), change little in volume and charge very fast; they are already being made at industrial scale for high-power cells.{{cite:R70}}

Worked example. What the safe voltage costs. With the same illustrative positive electrode of 200 mAh/g at 3.8 V: graphite: 3.65 V × 130 mAh/g = 475 Wh/kg of the pair Li₄Ti₅O₁₂: Q = \frac{200 \times 175}{375} = 93\ \mathrm{mAh/g}, at 3.8 − 1.55 = 2.25 V: 210 Wh/kg Less than half the energy, in exchange for a volt and a half of margin against plating. That is why titanate serves where power and life matter more than range.

Silicon: ten times the capacity, four times the volume

Silicon stores 3579 mAh/g, close to ten times graphite, at a low potential, and it is cheap. Its problem is volume: it swells by about 280 % when fully lithiated. The particles pulverize, lose contact with each other, and the electrode can come away from its current collector; the SEI breaks on every swelling and forms again on the fresh surface, using up lithium and electrolyte, so the first-cycle efficiency is low and the capacity fades fast. Silicon also conducts electrons poorly.{{cite:R70}}

Nanoparticles, porous and hollow spheres, nanowires, yolk–shell and pomegranate-like structures all help, though small particles cost volumetric energy. Binders that hold the contact, perhaps self-healing polymers, could be the breakthrough. Today’s best cells already use graphite with 5 to 10 wt% silicon; more than 20 % is the industry’s next step, trading energy against cycle life.{{cite:R70}} The silicon anode market, about $279 million in 2023, is projected to reach $15 billion by 2035.{{cite:R70}}

Worked example. What 280 % does to a particle, and what a little silicon does for a blend. volume after = 1 + 2.8 = 3.8 times; diameter after = 3.81/3 = 1.56 times: a 100 nm particle grows to about 156 nm and back on every cycle 8 wt% silicon in graphite: 0.92 \times 372 + 0.08 \times 3579 = 629\ \mathrm{mAh/g}, 1.7 times graphite The coating, the binder and the SEI on each silicon particle all have to follow that breathing.

A conversion electrode is not a host at all. Lithium reduces the compound to metal grains embedded in a lithium compound, for an oxide MxOy + 2yLi⁺ + 2ye⁻ ⇌ yLi₂O + xM, and on the way back the compound forms again. Oxides, sulfides, phosphides and fluorides work this way, with high capacities: about 1000 mAh/g for Fe₂O₃, 1230 for MnO₂, 670 for MoS₂ and 900 for FeP.{{cite:R70}} Nano-sized transition-metal oxides were shown to store two to three times the capacity of carbon in 2000.{{cite:R2}}

The drawbacks are four: the particles swell and pulverize; they conduct poorly (the Li₂O formed is an insulator); the SEI is unstable; and the voltage hysteresis is about 1 V, because several solid phases must form and dissolve and oxygen and metal atoms must rearrange, not just lithium.{{cite:R70}} Shanmukaraj and co-workers add that the first cycle loses about 30 % and that the wasted energy is the main obstacle.{{cite:R44}} Nano-sized particles shorten the paths and spread the stress, but increase the surface that reacts with the electrolyte.{{cite:R70}}

Transition-metal fluorides work the same way as positive electrodes, with theoretical capacities of 500 to 1500 mAh/g, and sulfur, at 2.4 V, is also a conversion electrode.{{cite:R44,R2}}

Lithium metal

Lithium metal is the most attractive negative electrode of all: 3860 mAh/g, the lowest potential, and nothing in it but active material. It was studied long before graphite, and kept out of commercial cells by dendrites.{{cite:R70}} In 2004 lithium-metal cells reached about 100 to 150 cycles, against the 300 a commercial cell needed.{{cite:R1}} Rahman and co-workers list the problems: dendrites, an unstable SEI, low Coulombic efficiency, dead lithium, volume changes and pulverization, and reaction with the electrolyte.{{cite:R70}}

Several models explain the dendrites. In the space-charge model, when plating outruns ion conduction at high current, the Li⁺ concentration near the surface falls to zero and dendritic growth begins. Cracks in the SEI, tips and surface defects concentrate the electric field, and lithium grows fastest there. On stripping, pieces break off the dendrites and lose contact: dead lithium, which in one study thickened from about 2 µm to about 100 µm within a few dozen cycles, lowering the efficiency and raising the impedance. Whiskers that pierce the separator short the cell, with a risk of fire.{{cite:R70}}

Most of these problems start at the interface, not in the bulk metal, which is why coatings, artificial SEI layers and new electrolytes are where the work is.{{cite:R70}} The interface is studied with surface spectroscopy (XPS, soft X-ray absorption), optical and electron microscopy, cryo-electron microscopy, which preserves fragile interphase compounds, and synchrotron X-ray diffraction and pair-distribution analysis, which identified the much-debated LiH in the interphase on lithium metal.{{cite:R70}}

Lithium metal stores the most, but it plates back unevenly, and that, not the chemistry of storage, is what limits it.

An anode-free cell goes one step further: it is assembled with no lithium on the negative side at all, only a copper current collector. On the first charge the lithium in the positive electrode is plated onto the copper, and on discharge it goes back. That is the highest energy a lithium-metal cell can reach, and the simplest to make; but with no lithium in reserve, every loss shows directly as lost capacity.{{cite:R70}}

Worked example. Why early lithium-metal cells carried far more lithium than they used. Brandt gives the lithium cycling efficiency E from the charge stripped each cycle and the excess of lithium used up; the relative excess needed for N cycles is R = N(1 − E). LiClO₄ in propylene carbonate, E ≈ 60 %: for just 100 cycles, R = 100 × 0.40 = an excess of 40 times the lithium cycled Moli Energy, late 1980s, E > 99 %: about 300 cycles, but only by carrying three times the lithium: R = 300 × 0.01 = an excess of 3 times and for an anode-free cell to keep 80 % for 1000 cycles: E^{1000} \ge 0.8, so E \ge 0.8^{1/1000} = 99.978\ \% The efficiency, not the capacity, decides whether lithium metal can be used.{{cite:R46}}

Three kinds of fix are being tried. An artificial SEI, made beforehand on the copper or formed in the cell, guides the lithium to deposit evenly. Electrolyte engineering: additives that build a better SEI, two-salt electrolytes, localized high-concentration electrolytes that mix a dissolving solvent with a diluent that does not dissolve the salt, and fluorinated solvents that are stable against both lithium and nickel-rich positive electrodes. And modified current collectors, such as three-dimensional porous copper that spreads the current and gives the lithium room.{{cite:R70}}

Lithium metal was long thought too reactive to cycle in a liquid electrolyte. The new electrolytes have changed that: they now allow promising cycling with little excess lithium and heavily loaded positive electrodes.{{cite:R70}}

Negative electrodes for solid-state cells

The main reason for moving to solid electrolytes (module 7) is to use lithium metal. Graphite and titanate work in solid-state cells but store too little to be worth it; silicon is attractive but its swelling is hard on a rigid electrolyte.{{cite:R70}} A solid electrolyte should be stiffer than the electrode to hold back dendrites, and lithium is soft; yet dendrites still grow through ceramic electrolytes, probably along their grain boundaries, and this is not yet understood.{{cite:R70}}

The lithium/ceramic interface has three problems: poor wetting, an unstable SEI and dendrites. Interlayers of Al₂O₃, LiNbO₃ or ZrO₂, often made by atomic layer deposition, a polymer-gel layer, or an additive such as dimethyl sulfate that forms a stiff Li₂S/Li₂O interphase, all improve it.{{cite:R70}} Two other designs avoid the problem: columnar silicon, whose columns change volume anisotropically and so build up less stress, kept 92 % of its capacity after 50 cycles with a sulfide electrolyte against 50.6 % in a liquid one, at a Coulombic efficiency of about 99.95 %; and Samsung’s silver–carbon layer, which needs no excess lithium and gave a prototype pouch cell of more than 900 Wh/L that lasted 1000 cycles.{{cite:R70}}

Worked example. An interface of 1710 Ω cm² at a current density of 1 mA/cm²: \Delta V = R\,i = 1710\ \Omega\,\mathrm{cm^2} \times 0.001\ \mathrm{A/cm^2} = 1.71\ \mathrm{V} with the Al₂O₃ interlayer, 1 Ω cm²: 1 mV Without the interlayer the interface alone would eat most of a lithium-metal cell’s voltage.

Beyond lithium

Lithium itself is a critical raw material, so its supply and sustainability matter too.{{cite:R70}} A sodium-ion cell would rock sodium ions instead of lithium ions, on the principle of module 6. But Na⁺ is larger than Li⁺ and needs a larger interstitial space than many of the hosts that serve lithium offer, so sodium-ion cells need hosts of their own.{{cite:R6}} The older sodium cells used molten electrodes instead: the sodium-sulfur battery, invented at Ford in 1967 after Kummer and Weber found fast Na⁺ conduction in a ceramic above 300 °C, runs at 300 to 350 °C.{{cite:R45,R6}} Sodium-ion is the first of the “beyond lithium” chemistries to become commercially viable, so far mainly for stationary storage, where energy density matters less.{{cite:R48}}

Organic electrodes put the redox centre in a molecule or polymer. They come as n-type, reduced to a negative state balanced by a cation such as Li⁺; p-type, oxidized to a positive state balanced by an anion; or bipolar. Not limited by the size of the ion, one material can serve in lithium, sodium, potassium or magnesium cells, and they can be made from abundant, even bio-sourced, elements; their weak points are, for some, a low working voltage, dissolution in the electrolyte, low volumetric capacity and poor electronic conductivity. Lithium terephthalate works as a negative electrode at 0.8 V vs Li/Li⁺ with 300 mAh/g.{{cite:R44}}

Module 9

Lithium metal, sodium and organic electrodes

If lithium metal is the best negative electrode, why is it not in your phone?

Where we are. Act B followed our ion through a lithium-ion cell that works. Act C is about how that journey goes wrong and what is being done about it. It starts with the materials beyond today’s cell: the lithium metal that the rocking chair replaced, the materials that store more than graphite, and the ions and molecules that might one day replace lithium and cobalt.

A lithium-metal negative electrode would give the highest energy of all, but lithium does not plate back the way it left: it redeposits as a mossy, dendritic layer. In 2004 that limited such cells to about 100 to 150 cycles, against the 300 a commercial cell must reach, and raised the risk of a short circuit.{{cite:R1}}

Each fresh, high-surface deposit also builds new SEI and loses more lithium.{{cite:R48}} Tarascon and Armand show the dendrites growing in situ in the electron microscope.{{cite:R2}} The SEI that forms on lithium lets Li⁺ through, yet it does not make the lithium plate back evenly.{{cite:R6}}

The routes tried since include special electrolytes, such as dioxolane with LiAsF₆, protective coatings on the lithium, glassy thin-film electrolytes that have reached 50 000 cycles, and cells assembled with no lithium metal at all.{{cite:R2}} A solid separator that blocks the dendrites is the route behind the solid-state cells of module 7.{{cite:R6}} Lithium metal stays indispensable in the laboratory, where half-cells against it are the standard way to test a new positive electrode.{{cite:R6}}

Worked example. Why early lithium-metal cells carried far more lithium than they used. Brandt gives the usual definition of the lithium cycling efficiency E from the charge stripped each cycle and the excess of lithium used up; the relative excess needed for N cycles is R = N(1 − E). LiClO₄ in propylene carbonate, E ≈ 60 %: for just 100 cycles, R = 100 × 0.40 = an excess of 40 times the lithium cycled Moli Energy, late 1980s, E > 99 %: about 300 cycles, but only by carrying three times the lithium: R = 300 × 0.01 = an excess of 3 times so the 3861 mAh/g of lithium metal becomes 3861 ÷ (1 + 3) ≈ 965 mAh/g for a rechargeable negative electrode Still well above graphite’s 372 mAh/g (module 4).{{cite:R46}}

Lithium metal stores the most, but it plates back unevenly, and that, not the chemistry of storage, is what limits it.

The negative electrodes that store more than graphite pay in volume. Lithium alloys, at 0.2 to 0.8 V vs Li/Li⁺, can swell by up to 200 %, and silicon by about 300 %.{{cite:R6,R2}} Swelling and shrinking on every cycle, the particles crack, lose electrical contact and stop working, unless a buffer matrix around them takes up the strain and keeps the electrical pathway.{{cite:R2}}

Worked example. What a 300 % volume change means for the size of a particle. volume after = 1 + 3 = 4 times the volume before diameter after = 41/3 = 1.59 times the diameter before a 100 nm particle grows to about 160 nm, and back, on every cycle The coating around it, the binder and the SEI on its surface all have to follow that.{{cite:R6}}

A third family abandons the host altogether. In a conversion electrode a transition-metal oxide reacts with lithium as lithium goes in and turns into metal nanoparticles embedded in Li₂O; as lithium comes out, the oxide forms again. Such nano-sized oxides store two to three times the capacity of carbon.{{cite:R2}} The price is in efficiency: the oxide reforms only partly, much lithium goes into the SEI, the first cycle loses about 30 %, and charge and discharge are separated by almost a volt, which wastes energy.{{cite:R44}}

Transition-metal fluorides work the same way as positive electrodes, with theoretical capacities of 500 to 1500 mAh/g.{{cite:R44}} Sulfur, at 2.4 V, also works by conversion rather than insertion.{{cite:R2}}

Put together, the materials form a small family tree: lithium metal; the insertion hosts of today’s cells; the alloys; the conversion electrodes; and organic electrodes, in which the redox centre is part of a molecule. In Tarascon and Armand’s 2001 review the organic examples were disulfides, whose S–S bonds break into thiolates and reform, at up to 3 V, held back by their solubility and by self-discharge.{{cite:R2}} In 2013 Goodenough and Park ended their perspective by calling for organic electrodes with multiple-electron redox centres.{{cite:R6}}

Organic electrodes have since become a field of their own. Their redox centres sit in molecules or polymers, and they come in three kinds: n-type, reduced to a negative state whose charge a cation such as Li⁺ balances; p-type, oxidized to a positive state balanced by an anion of the electrolyte; and bipolar, which can go either way.{{cite:R44}}

Unlike inorganic insertion hosts, they are not limited by the size of the ion, so one material can serve in lithium, sodium, potassium or magnesium cells.{{cite:R44}} They are softer and swell less than crystalline hosts, can be made from abundant, even bio-sourced, elements, and their chemistry can be tuned; the weak points of some of them are a low working voltage, possible dissolution in the electrolyte, low volumetric capacity and poor electronic conductivity.{{cite:R44}} One benchmark, lithium terephthalate, works as a negative electrode at 0.8 V vs Li/Li⁺ with 300 mAh/g.{{cite:R44}}

A sodium-ion cell would rock sodium ions instead of lithium ions, on the principle of module 6. But Na⁺ is larger than Li⁺ and needs a larger interstitial space than many of the hosts that serve lithium offer, so sodium-ion cells need hosts of their own.{{cite:R6}}

The older sodium cells used molten electrodes instead: the sodium-sulfur battery, invented at Ford in 1967 after Kummer and Weber found fast Na⁺ conduction in a ceramic above 300 °C, runs at 300 to 350 °C, and the ZEBRA cell is a variant with NaCl and iron.{{cite:R45,R6}} Sodium-ion is the first of the “beyond lithium” chemistries to become commercially viable, so far mainly for stationary storage, where energy density matters less.{{cite:R48}}

You can now explain why lithium metal is hard to cycle and what is being tried, why higher-capacity negative electrodes swell, what a conversion electrode does, where each family of materials sits on the lithium scale, and why sodium needs hosts of its own.

Where the voltage comes from

Why does a lithium-ion cell give over 3.6 V and a torch battery 1.5 V?

The open-circuit voltage of a cell is the difference between the electrochemical potentials of its two electrodes.{{cite:R6}} Picture each material as a rung on a ladder: the voltage is the gap between the two rungs you choose. Battery scientists measure the rungs against lithium metal, which is why every value here reads “versus Li/Li⁺”.

Lithium sits at the bottom. In Tarascon and Armand’s words it is “the most electropositive” metal, meaning the one with the most negative standard potential, −3.04 V against the standard hydrogen electrode, and it is also the lightest metal, at 6.94 g/mol and 0.53 g/cm³.{{cite:R2}} Any positive electrode paired with lithium, or with a host that holds lithium near its own potential, gets the largest possible gap.

Think of two reservoirs at different heights: the pressure at the pipe between them depends on the height difference, not on how wide the reservoirs are. The picture breaks as soon as water flows: the height stays, but the flow costs pressure. Module 5 is about that cost.

Voltage is a property of the pair of materials, not of the size of the cell.

The gap has a ceiling. An electrolyte is stable only between two energy levels: the energy of its lowest unoccupied orbital and of its highest occupied one. A negative electrode whose electrons sit above the first will reduce the electrolyte, and a positive electrode whose empty states sit below the second will oxidize it, unless a passivating layer forms at the surface and blocks the reaction.{{cite:R6}}

Water is thermodynamically stable over only 1.23 V, and thanks to slow kinetics about 2 V in practice,{{cite:R1}} which is why cells with an aqueous electrolyte and a long shelf life stop near 1.5 V.{{cite:R6}} The organic carbonate electrolytes of lithium-ion cells reach about 4.3 V versus lithium before their filled orbitals are attacked,{{cite:R6}} and about 4.6 V in practice.{{cite:R1}} That is the difference between the torch battery and the phone battery: not a stronger reaction, but an electrolyte that tolerates a wider gap.

Graphite sits about 0.2 V above lithium, below the carbonate electrolyte’s limit, so a layer must form on it during the first charge; lithium titanate at 1.5 V sits inside the window and needs none.{{cite:R6}} Module 6 follows that layer as it forms.

Why do oxide positive electrodes reach 4 V when the sulfide of the first rechargeable lithium cell reached only 2.2 V? The redox couple of the host cannot be pushed below the top of the host’s anion band. In the layered sulfides that top sits about 2.5 eV below the lithium level; in the layered oxides it sits about 4.0 eV below, which is why oxide hosts are used in today’s lithium-ion cells.{{cite:R6}} Polyanions such as phosphate pull the band lower still through what chemists call the inductive effect, which is how LiFePO₄ reaches 3.5 V.{{cite:R6,R2}}

In the language of thermodynamics the same gap reads ΔG = −nFE: the electrical energy available from a reaction is the number of electrons it moves per formula unit, times the Faraday constant, times the cell voltage. So the voltage is the reaction’s free energy per unit of charge, and nF, the total charge available, is its capacity factor.{{cite:R1}} The Faraday constant is the charge carried by one mole of electrons, 96 485.3 coulombs.{{cite:R1}} Module 4 turns that constant into the capacity of your cell.

You can now explain why lithium cells have the highest voltages, why oxide hosts beat sulfides, and what sets the ceiling.

Go deeper: reading “versus Li/Li⁺”

A potential is always a difference, so every value needs a reference. Textbook tables use the standard hydrogen electrode, against which lithium sits at −3.04 V;{{cite:R2}} battery papers use lithium metal itself as the zero, which is why the same LiCoO₂ electrode reads about 4.0 V “versus Li/Li⁺” and about 1 V versus hydrogen.{{cite:R6,R2}} In a lithium-ion cell the negative electrode is not lithium metal but a host that holds lithium near lithium’s potential, so the cell voltage is a little less than the positive electrode’s ladder value: graphite costs about 0.2 V.{{cite:R6}}

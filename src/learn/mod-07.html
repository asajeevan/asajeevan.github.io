Module 7

The electrolyte

How does an ion travel through a liquid, a polymer or a solid?

Where we are. Module 6 put our ion in its two hosts. Between them it crosses the electrolyte, the part of the cell that must carry ions and stop electrons. This module starts from nothing: what the liquid in a lithium-ion cell is made of, how its molecules sit around each ion, how an ion moves through it, what the electric field inside it looks like, and how the arrangement changes, layer by layer, as you approach an electrode. Then it compares the liquid with polymers and ceramics, and shows why the anion matters as much as the lithium.

Everything in this module is made of four kinds of object, so meet them first. A molecule is a group of atoms held together by chemical bonds; the figure draws each atom as a ball and each bond as a stick. The two solvents of a lithium-ion cell are both carbonates, built round a carbon atom bonded to three oxygen atoms. In ethylene carbonate, EC, the carbonate closes into a ring; in dimethyl carbonate, DMC, it stays an open chain: EC is a cyclic carbonate and DMC a linear one.{{cite:R54}}

In both, one oxygen is double-bonded to the carbon and sticks out of the molecule: the carbonyl oxygen. A lithium ion is held by a solvent through the spare pairs of electrons on such a donor atom.{{cite:R53}} The salt brings the other two objects: the lithium ion, Li⁺, a lithium atom that has lost one electron, and the hexafluorophosphate anion, PF₆⁻, six fluorine atoms around a phosphorus atom, carrying one negative charge.{{cite:R53}}

Electron clouds and charge

A molecule is not a set of hard balls. Its electrons form a cloud, densest at the atoms and thinning out over a fraction of a nanometre, and the figure shows that cloud as calculated for this page. The calculation also shows where each molecule is slightly negative and where slightly positive. EC has a negative end, at the carbonyl oxygen, and a positive end, at its CH₂ groups: a strongly polar molecule, with a computed dipole moment of about 5 debye. In DMC the two halves almost cancel, about 0.3 debye. The same contrast shows up in the two liquids’ dielectric constants, 90 for EC against 3.1 for DMC, in the next figure; the simulations quoted below likewise call EC the polar solvent and DMC the less polar one.{{cite:R54,R57}}

Four objects to recognize: EC, a ring; DMC, a chain; PF₆⁻, a ball of fluorine; and Li⁺, the smallest of all.

The recipe

An electrolyte has one job that defines it: to provide purely ionic conduction between the two electrodes.{{cite:R1}} It is a solution: a salt dissolved in a liquid, the solvent. Most commercial lithium-ion cells use roughly 1 mol of the salt lithium hexafluorophosphate, LiPF₆, per litre of a mixture of carbonate solvents: a cyclic one such as ethylene carbonate (EC) or propylene carbonate (PC), and a linear one such as dimethyl carbonate (DMC), ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) or diethyl carbonate (DEC).{{cite:R54}}

Water, the obvious solvent, is out: it would be split at the voltage of a lithium cell (module 3).{{cite:B2}} Water-based electrolytes conduct best, about 1 S/cm; the organic liquids reach 10⁻² to 10⁻³ S/cm, because they dissolve salts less well and let the ions pair up.{{cite:R1}}

Why two solvents

Why two solvents? Because each does one job well and the other badly. A good solvent needs a high dielectric constant (relative permittivity), to pull the salt apart into separate ions, and a high fluidity, so that the ions can move.{{cite:R54}} EC has a relative permittivity of 90 but is thick, 1.9 mPa s, and melts at about 36 °C, so on its own it is a solid at room temperature. DMC flows easily, 0.59 mPa s, but its relative permittivity is only 3.1.{{cite:R54}}

Mixed, they stay liquid far lower: a mixture of carbonates has a eutectic, the composition with the lowest melting point, at −8.6 °C for EC with DMC and −64.7 °C for EC with DMC and EMC (the solvents alone, without salt), and blends near the eutectic are preferred to work in the cold. EC is usually kept below 50 % by volume, preferably 30 %, because more of it comes out as a solid at low temperature.{{cite:R54}}

A pinch of additives

Real electrolytes also carry minute amounts of additives, each with a job: some decompose on purpose to build a thin, controlled SEI on the graphite (module 6), others protect the positive electrode or stop overcharge (module 12); no single additive can do everything, and finding the right combination is now central to electrolyte work.{{cite:R55}}

Why LiPF₆

Why LiPF₆? It is used almost exclusively in commercial lithium-ion cells because it has shown the best balance of the properties a salt needs: its solutions are among the most conductive known, it forms a stable surface on the aluminium current collector at high potential, and with carbonate solvents it forms a stable SEI on graphite (module 6). Its weakness is its P–F bond, which is easily broken: the salt reacts readily with water and is not very stable to heat, and hydrofluoric acid, HF, in LiPF₆ electrolytes is one of the principal concerns about using it.{{cite:R53}} It became the standard only when carbon replaced lithium metal and the solvents were optimized for the lithium-ion cells Sony introduced in 1991.{{cite:R53}}

Worked example. Counting the crowd: 1 mol of LiPF₆ dissolved in 1 litre of EC : DMC 3 : 7 by volume (the salt’s own volume neglected). EC: 300 cm³ × 1.32 g/cm³ ÷ 88.1 g/mol = 4.5 mol DMC: 700 cm³ × 1.06 g/cm³ ÷ 90.1 g/mol = 8.2 mol so for each Li⁺ and each PF₆⁻: about 13 solvent molecules each Li⁺ has a cube of liquid about (1 L ÷ 6.02 × 10²³)^1/3 = 1.2 nm on a side to itself, on average A lithium ion is never more than a few molecules from the next one, and most of what surrounds it is solvent.{{cite:R54,B2}}

The liquid in a lithium-ion cell is mostly solvent: a dozen or so carbonate molecules for every lithium ion, two kinds of solvent because neither does both jobs.

Choosing a salt

A salt for a lithium-ion electrolyte has a list of jobs. It must give a conductive solution, stay stable within the cell’s voltage window and at high temperature, help form a good SEI, protect the aluminium current collector of the positive electrode above about 3.6 V vs Li, and resist water. It should also be cheap and not toxic. Failing any one of these rules a salt out of practical use, and every one of them depends on the rest of the recipe.{{cite:R53}}

Line the candidates up and no salt wins everywhere. LiClO₄ conducts well and is stable, but its anion is a strong oxidant, which makes the salt a potential explosive. LiAsF₆ behaves much like LiPF₆, but reduction could turn its arsenic into highly toxic forms. LiBF₄ resists water and heat better than LiPF₆ but conducts significantly less.{{cite:R53}}

LiTFSI and lithium triflate shrug off water and heat, but in ordinary dilute solutions they corrode the aluminium foil at high potential. In PC:DME the aluminium repassivation potential, above which corrosion pits keep growing, is 3.7 V vs Li with LiTFSI, against above 5 V with LiPF₆.{{cite:R53}} So LiPF₆, with its known weaknesses, keeps its place in the recipe of figure 7.2.{{cite:R53}}

Choosing the solvents

The five carbonates of commercial electrolytes split into two families.{{cite:R54}} The cyclic ones, EC and PC, have relative permittivities of 65 to 90 and flash points above 130 °C, but they are viscous, and EC is a solid below 36 °C. The linear ones, DMC, EMC and DEC, flow two and a half to four times more easily and, apart from DMC, stay liquid far below 0 °C. But their permittivities are only about 3, and they flash at 17 to 25 °C, near room temperature.{{cite:R54}}

All five resist oxidation on glassy carbon to above 6 V vs Li. Henderson warns that a window measured on inert carbon is not a clear guide to stability against real electrode materials.{{cite:R54,R53}}

Every salt and every solvent fails at something; a commercial electrolyte is the mixture whose weaknesses matter least.

What does it mean for a salt to dissolve? Solid LiPF₆ is made of Li⁺ cations and PF₆⁻ anions, which attract each other because their charges are opposite (module 0). In a carbonate solvent the Li⁺ ions are pulled out of it by the solvent molecules: each solvent molecule has an oxygen atom with spare pairs of electrons (in a carbonate, the carbonyl oxygen), and it binds to the lithium through them. The anions are, in general, left bare, because these solvents have no acidic hydrogen to hold them: in water an anion is held by hydrogen bonds, in a carbonate it is not. The anions can bind to Li⁺ in the same way, so solvent and anion compete for the places around each lithium ion, and that competition decides what is actually present in the liquid.{{cite:R53}}

And the anion?

So does a PF₆⁻ anion get a shell of its own? Not in the way Li⁺ does. Solvent molecules do come close to it, and simulations treat a PF₆⁻ next to an EC molecule as a complex, but nothing binds there the way the oxygens bind to lithium, and the neighbours keep changing. In the figure they are drawn faint, with their slightly positive CH ends (blue in figure 7.1) towards the anion.{{cite:R53,R57}}

Dissolving is a tug of war for the lithium ion, and in a good solvent the solvent wins.

The shell

How many solvent molecules fit round one lithium ion? Few. Simulations find about four solvent molecules bound strongly around a lithium ion, arranged as a tetrahedron, and about 4.2 to 4.4 partners, solvent and anions together, in EC : DMC and EC : PC with LiPF₆; neutron diffraction on LiPF₆ in PC finds 4.5 PC molecules on average, each oxygen 0.204 nm from the lithium.{{cite:R57}}

This group is the ion’s first solvation shell. In the liquid, simulations find EC and DMC in it about equally often, even though an isolated lithium ion in the gas phase prefers EC.{{cite:R57}} The shell is not a fixed object: a given EC molecule stays in it for about half a nanosecond, an anion partner for 2 to 3 ns.{{cite:R57}}

Pairs and clusters

When an anion wins a place, the two ions form a contact ion pair; when solvent stays between them, they are a solvent-separated ion pair; larger clusters are aggregates.{{cite:R57,R53}} The anion matters: the tendency to pair up rises in the order LiAsF₆ and LiPF₆, then LiTFSI, LiClO₄, LiBF₄ and lithium triflate.{{cite:R53}}

The solvent matters too: in simulations of EC : DMC the fraction of free ions rises nearly linearly with the share of EC; and measured with LiTFSI, the conductivity reaches 62 % of what fully independent ions would give in PC, but only 11 % in DMC. Not every cluster is lost to conduction: charged clusters carry current too.{{cite:R57}}

A lithium ion never travels alone: it carries a shell of about four solvent molecules, and sometimes an anion.

Wandering

Even with no current, nothing in the liquid is still. Every ion is bumped continually by the molecules around it and wanders at random, in every direction alike. On average it goes nowhere; it only spreads out, a distance that grows as the square root of time, (2Dt)1/2, where D is its diffusion coefficient. This is diffusion, met inside the electrode particles in module 6.{{cite:B2}}

Drifting in a field

Draw current and a small electric field appears across the liquid. It does not replace the random wandering; it biases it: each step is pushed slightly one way, cations down the potential and anions up it. That steady bias is migration, and the Nernst–Planck equation adds the two together (module 0).{{cite:B2}}

What moves is the ion together with whatever stays bound to it, which is why a bigger moving object, or a thicker liquid, conducts less: u = |z|e/(6πηr).{{cite:B2}} The lithium ion moves in two ways: it carries its first shell along with it, or it is handed on, leaving some solvent molecules behind and gaining others. In EC : DMC with LiPF₆ the two contribute roughly equally; in short ethylene-oxide chains (oligoethers) the ion travels a distance of three to four solvent molecules before its shell is renewed, so there carrying dominates.{{cite:R57}}

A picture to keep: ionic conduction is a crowd crossing a corridor. More people, or people who move more freely, carry more across per second. The picture breaks in two places: the ions also push on each other through the field, and in a polymer the corridor itself has to keep moving for anyone to get through.

Worked example. How strong is the field, and how fast do the ions go? A current density of 1 mA/cm² through a liquid of conductivity κ = 10⁻² S/cm, with 1 mol/L of Li⁺. field in the liquid, from Ohm’s law: E = i/κ = 10⁻³ A/cm² ÷ 10⁻² S/cm = 0.1 V/cm in the steady state, when all the current is carried by lithium: mean velocity v = i/(Fc) = 10⁻³ ÷ (96 485 × 10⁻³) cm/s ≈ 0.1 µm per second to cross a 25 µm layer of liquid: about 4 minutes The field in the liquid is gentle and each ion drifts slowly; the current is large because there are so many ions, about 6 × 10²⁰ in every cubic centimetre.{{cite:B2,R1}}

An ion in a liquid wanders at random; a current only nudges the wandering, gently and all the same way.

The bulk and the diffuse layer

The electrolyte is not the same all the way across. Far from either electrode, in the bulk, every small volume holds as much positive as negative charge: a separation of charge there would set up a field large enough to erase it at once.{{cite:B2}} The molecules point every way, and with no current the potential is flat. With current, the only field is the gentle ohmic one of the worked example above, about 0.1 V/cm.

A few nanometres from a charged electrode this changes. Ions of the opposite sign crowd towards the surface and solvent molecules turn: together they cancel the electrode’s charge, so its field does not reach the bulk.{{cite:B2}} The excess of ions thins out over the diffuse layer, less than about 10 nm thick above 0.01 M; closest to the surface is the compact layer, one solvent shell thick.{{cite:B2}}

The field inside that thin step can reach 10⁷ V/cm, a hundred million times the field in the bulk of the worked example.{{cite:B2}} This is the electrical double layer of module 3, now seen molecule by molecule.

The first molecular layer

Simulations show what the innermost layer looks like in EC : DMC with LiPF₆. When the electrode is charged, either way, the more polar EC replaces DMC at the surface, an effect confirmed by experiment on LiCoO₂. At a negative electrode the carbonyl groups are pushed away from the surface, and at the most negative potentials lithium ions sit against it, solvated mainly by EC, which fits the observation that EC reduction dominates the SEI formed in these mixtures. At a positive electrode the layer fills with carbonyl groups and PF₆⁻ anions accumulate, where they are expected to take part in oxidation reactions.{{cite:R57}}

Getting in

To enter graphite a lithium ion has to leave its shell. In preliminary simulations of bare graphite, with no SEI, a lithium ion rests about 0.5 nm from the surface, still solvated; closer than about 0.4 nm the free energy climbs steeply as it is forced to shed its solvent, consistent with DMC going first and EC last. The size of that climb agrees with measured activation energies of 16.4 to 17 kcal/mol, about 70 kJ/mol. The two contributions usually discussed for the resistance of this interface are the SEI and this desolvation, and experiments show that at low temperature the interface can resist more than the whole bulk electrolyte.{{cite:R57}}

Uniform in the middle, ordered in the last nanometre: the electrolyte’s structure, and almost all of its field, change within a few molecules of the electrode.

So far, a liquid. Two other kinds of electrolyte carry lithium differently: a polymer, where there is no solvent at all, and a ceramic, where the ion moves through a solid framework.

A dry polymer such as poly(ethylene oxide), PEO, dissolves a lithium salt with no solvent at all. Wright and co-workers first saw ionic conduction in PEO complexes of sodium and potassium salts, and in 1978 Armand proposed the first dry PEO-lithium electrolyte for lithium batteries, conducting about 10⁻⁴ S/cm at 40 to 60 °C.{{cite:R44}}

The Li⁺ ion is held by the oxygen atoms of the chain and hops from one coordinating site to the next, helped by the segmental motion of the chain; the motion happens in the amorphous, not the crystalline, regions of the polymer.{{cite:R44,R2}} That is the weakness of the idea: no chain motion, no conduction.

An electrolyte must carry ions and block electrons; how it carries them decides how fast, and at what temperature.

The polymer route improved by steps. Changing the salt in PEO from lithium triflate, LiCF₃SO₃, to LiTFSI, a salt Armand introduced, gains about an order of magnitude, its bulky anion acting as a plasticizer;{{cite:R44}} adding 10 to 25 % of a plasticizer gains another, and such lightly plasticized polymers still suit a lithium-metal electrode. Gels, 60 to 95 % liquid held in a polymer, come within 2 to 5 times of the liquid’s conductivity but need a lithium-ion configuration. A dry-polymer lithium cell runs at up to 80 °C.{{cite:R2}}

LiTFSI itself is a good salt, conductive and safe, with one catch: above about 4 V (3.7 V in Henderson’s PC:DME data) it corrodes the aluminium current collector unless a passivating salt is added.{{cite:R2,R53}} Liquid or polymer, a nonaqueous electrolyte is thermodynamically stable over a window of about 3.5 V but is used, kinetically, up to 5.5 V.{{cite:R2}}

The neighbours matter: a single EC molecule is computed to resist oxidation to about 6.9 V vs Li/Li⁺; next to a PF₆⁻ anion, which can take a hydrogen from it as HF, the limit falls, and when the hydrogen passes to a neighbouring EC it falls to about 5 to 5.2 V, almost 2 V lower.{{cite:R51}} In the usual mixture, the cyclic EC dissolves the salt and builds the SEI, while a linear carbonate such as DMC thins the liquid so it conducts better in the cold.{{cite:R51}}

Worked example. What the conductivity costs, for a 25 μm electrolyte layer (an illustrative thickness) of 1 cm², using R = l/(κA) from module 0. liquid, κ = 10⁻² S/cm: R = 0.0025 cm ÷ (10⁻² S/cm × 1 cm²) = 0.25 Ω a solid at κ = 10⁻⁴ S/cm: 25 Ω, a hundred times more at 1 mA: ohmic drop 0.25 mV against 25 mV This is why 10⁻⁴ S/cm is the bar a solid electrolyte has to clear, and why it has to be thin.{{cite:B2,R1,R6}}

Both ions move, but only one is wanted. The transference number t₊ is the fraction of the ionic conductivity carried by Li⁺; in a dilute electrolyte it is the ratio of the lithium diffusion coefficient to the sum of both.{{cite:R36,B2}} (Tarascon and Armand call it the transport number.{{cite:R2}}) In the organic liquid electrolytes of 1994 it was typically below 0.5.{{cite:R46}}

The rest of the current is carried by anions that no electrode can take in, so in a working cell salt builds up at one electrode and runs short at the other, and the steady-state difference grows with (1 − t₊).{{cite:R36}}

That gradient is the concentration polarization of module 5, seen from the electrolyte. In polymer electrolytes t₊ is about 0.3; about 10 % of ceramic nanofillers lifts it to about 0.6, raises the conductivity severalfold at 60 to 80 °C, and gives a stable interface with lithium.{{cite:R2}}

The logical end point is a single-ion conductor, in which the anions are tied to the polymer so that t₊ approaches 1 and no salt gradient builds up; one block copolymer of this kind reached t₊ above 0.85, though at only 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ S/cm at 60 °C, and conducting 10⁻⁴ S/cm at room temperature remains the challenge.{{cite:R44}}

A solid separator that blocks dendrites would make a lithium-metal negative electrode possible (module 9), and Goodenough and Park list what a solid electrolyte must do: conduct Li⁺ at above 10⁻⁴ S/cm, block dendrites without being reduced by lithium, stay chemically stable, and form a robust, flexible thin membrane. The garnet Li₇₋ₓLa₃Zr₂₋ₓTaₓO₁₂ conducts above 10⁻³ S/cm and is stable against lithium; meeting all four at once, they suggested, points to ceramic-polymer composites. A polymer above 10⁻⁴ S/cm with good electrode contact had, in 2013, yet to be demonstrated.{{cite:R6}}

Even a good solid conductor has to keep touching the electrodes. Electrode particles swell and shrink as lithium goes in and out, and against an inorganic solid electrolyte the contact is lost.{{cite:R6}} In a liquid, the electrolyte simply flows after them.

You can now explain what the liquid in a lithium-ion cell is made of and why it mixes two solvents, what a solvation shell is and how ions pair up, how an ion wanders and drifts and how weak the field in the bulk is, how the liquid changes layer by layer towards an electrode, how a liquid, a polymer and a ceramic each carry a lithium ion, why dry-polymer cells run warm, what the transference number is and why a low one leaves a salt gradient, and why a solid electrolyte needs more than a high conductivity.

On the far side, the ion has to find a particle.

Go deeper: conductivity from the ions themselves

The conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of what each ion contributes: κ = F Σ|zj|ujCj, with uj the mobility and Cj the concentration of ion j. The share carried by one ion is its transference number, tj = |zj|ujCj / Σ|zk|ukCk.{{cite:B2}} For a salt of one cation and one anion this reduces to t₊ = |z₊|u₊/(|z₊|u₊ + |z₋|u₋), and in the dilute limit to DLi/(DLi + D₋).{{cite:R36}} Module 0 gave the mobility of a sphere in a viscous liquid, u = |z|e/(6πηr): a bigger ion, or a more viscous liquid, conducts less.{{cite:B2}}

How independent are the ions? Measure each ion’s self-diffusion coefficient, D₊ and D₋, and compute the conductivity the ions would give if each moved on its own, λNE = e²(n₊D₊ + n₋D₋)/(VkBT), for n ions of each kind in a volume V. The measured conductivity divided by it is the ionicity, αd = λ/λNE: 0.62 for LiTFSI in PC and 0.11 in DMC, both at 20 solvent molecules per lithium and 298 K.{{cite:R57}} Why about 1 mol per litre in the first place? Enough salt has to dissolve to provide plenty of charge carriers, and the conductivity is frequently found to peak near 1 M.{{cite:R53}}

Even water can be stretched. Water splits over a window of 1.23 V (module 3), but in “water-in-salt” electrolytes, with far more salt than water by weight, the salt crowds the electrode surfaces and its decomposition builds a LiF-rich interphase; with further measures to keep water off the negative electrode, aqueous electrolytes have reached stability windows above 4 V.{{cite:R51}}

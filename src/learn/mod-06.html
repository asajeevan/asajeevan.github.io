Module 6

Inside a lithium-ion cell

Where does a lithium ion actually sit, and what happens on the very first charge?

Where we are. Module 5 watched the voltage curve from outside, at the terminals. Now go inside, with our ion: where it sits in each electrode, how it gets in and out, and the one-off price a new cell pays the first time it is charged.

Neither electrode of a lithium-ion cell is used up. Both are insertion hosts: solids that conduct ions and electrons and let guest ions in and out by a solid-state redox reaction. Because their structure and shape survive, insertion electrodes are highly reversible; for layered hosts the process is called intercalation.{{cite:R1}}

Replacing the lithium-metal negative electrode with a second insertion host gave the lithium-ion or rocking-chair cell: demonstrated by Murphy and by Scrosati, put on the market by Sony in June 1991 as carbon against LiCoO₂.{{cite:R2}} In the graphite, lithium sits between the carbon sheets, up to one lithium per six carbons, LiC₆.{{cite:R6}}

In LiCoO₂ it sits between the oxide layers; only about half of it is taken out on charge, to about 4.2 V, because once more than about 0.55 of it is removed the oxide starts to give off oxygen.{{cite:R2,R6}}

Worked example. How many ions rock in a 3 Ah phone cell on each full discharge? charge = 3 Ah × 3600 s/h = 10 800 C ions = 10 800 C ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 6.7 × 10²², that is 10 800 ÷ 96 485 = 0.112 mol of lithium mass of that lithium = 0.112 mol × 6.94 g/mol = 0.78 g Less than a gram of lithium travels back and forth; the rest of the cell is the hosts, the electrolyte and the packaging that let it do so.{{cite:B2,R2}}

Nothing is consumed in an ideal lithium-ion cell: the ions only change address.

Graphite: sheets, and lithium by stages

Look closer at the two hosts. Graphite is a stack of graphene sheets: carbon atoms joined in hexagonal rings, the sheets held to one another by weak van der Waals forces and π–π interactions, 0.335 nm apart; in natural graphite they are mostly stacked in the order A, B, A, B.{{cite:R58}} The lithium goes into the gaps between the sheets, at most one lithium for every six carbons, LiC₆.{{cite:R6}} The edges of the sheets are the favourable sites for lithium to go in, and it diffuses faster there; the flat faces, the basal planes, are less reactive.{{cite:R58}}

It does not fill all the gaps at once. Lithium fills some gaps and leaves the others empty, forming stages: the stage number is the number of graphene sheets between two lithium layers. As the cell charges, the graphite passes from stage 4 through stage 3 to stage 2, LiC₁₂, and finally stage 1, LiC₆, with lithium in every gap. Stages 2 and 3 are stable phases that affect the equilibrium potential and the diffusion of lithium. Each change of stage is favoured thermodynamically but held back kinetically, the last one, from LiC₁₂ to LiC₆, most of all. Rüdorff and Hofmann pictured each stage as complete lithium layers, as the figure draws them; in the Daumas–Hérold picture the guest forms islands within the layers instead. Full lithiation swells the graphite by about 10 %, mostly by the sheets moving apart, not by the sheets stretching.{{cite:R58}}

LiCoO₂: lithium between oxide sheets

LiCoO₂ is a layered compound too: cobalt surrounded by oxygen in CoO₂ sheets, with the lithium in the layer between two of them. The idea came from sodium, which Hagenmuller and co-workers had put between such sheets in the 1970s; in 1979 and 1980 Goodenough’s group made the lithium compound, at twice the voltage of TiS₂.{{cite:R47}} On charge only about half of its lithium is taken out.{{cite:R2,R6}}

The sheets are what makes this work. Lithium comes and goes; the framework of carbon or of cobalt oxide stays, so the electrodes keep their structure and shape and the reaction can run back and forth many times.{{cite:R1}}

Two layered solids and one guest: charging moves lithium from between the oxide sheets to between the carbon sheets, and discharging moves it back.

The first charge

A lithium-ion cell is assembled discharged, with all its lithium in the positive electrode. On the first charge the graphite is driven down to about 0.2 V vs Li/Li⁺, below the lower edge of the window in which the carbonate electrolyte is stable (module 3). The electrolyte is reduced at the graphite surface, and its products form a passivating layer, the solid-electrolyte interphase or SEI. Forming it consumes lithium from the positive electrode irreversibly, and the layer adds to the interfacial impedance; it also changes as the cell cycles.{{cite:R6}}

Among the solvents, ethylene carbonate is reduced first, from about 0.8 V vs Li/Li⁺ (with LiPF₆ the anion starts earlier; see Go deeper). Two EC molecules, two lithium ions and two electrons give lithium ethylene dicarbonate (Li₂EDC) and ethylene gas; the layer that forms has a dense inorganic inner part next to the graphite (Li₂CO₃, LiF, Li₂O) and a porous organic outer part.{{cite:R52,R50}}

A crystalline layer only 2 to 3 nm thick would already stop electrons tunnelling through, but on graphite the SEI soon grows past 10 nm and reaches about 50 nm after cycling.{{cite:R52,R49}}

Why the layer helps

What makes the SEI useful is its selectivity: it is electronically insulating, so it stops further reduction, yet it conducts Li⁺, so the graphite keeps filling.{{cite:R1}} Of the main solvents, only ethylene carbonate forms a protective layer on graphite, which is why it is in almost every commercial electrolyte; propylene carbonate does not, and in 2001 Tarascon and Armand still called the reason unclear.{{cite:R2}}

The explanation usually given, which goes back to Dahn and co-workers in 1990, is that EC decomposes first and builds an SEI that stops PC from slipping into the graphite together with the lithium;{{cite:R47}} PC holds on to its Li⁺ more strongly, goes into the graphite with it, and peels the graphite layers apart.{{cite:R52}}

Brandt’s 1994 review put it simply: the layer forms during the first insertion, and after it intercalation is completely reversible.{{cite:R46}} Module 10 shows that this is only nearly true. An electrode that works inside the window needs no SEI: lithium titanate at 1.5 V forms none, and Zaghib and co-workers reported electrodes of lithium titanate and LiFePO₄ that survived 30 000 cycles at 5C.{{cite:R6}}

Steering the SEI on purpose

Today the SEI is also steered on purpose, with small amounts of additives that decompose first and leave a thin, controlled layer. The best known is vinylene carbonate (VC): reduced at the graphite above 1 V vs Li/Li⁺, it forms a passivation layer, and it suppresses the decomposition of PC.{{cite:R55}}

Why new cells are “formed”. Because the SEI decides how the cell will age, a factory does not leave its growth to chance: near the end of making a cell, it is cycled slowly under precisely defined conditions, for up to a day, and let the gas out before the cell is sealed for good.{{cite:R48}} It is expensive, about a third (32 %) of the cost of making a cell, mostly for keeping cells warm while they condition.{{cite:R50}} And the first cycle is where the big loss happens: in one recent laboratory cell the first cycle returned about 82.5 % of the charge put in, the second about 97.5 %.{{cite:R50}}

Not only on graphite. A positive electrode pushed above the upper edge of the window, about 4.3 V, oxidizes the electrolyte instead; LiNi₀.₅Mn₁.₅O₄ at about 4.75 V is protected by a passivation layer of its own, which Goodenough and Park also call an SEI.{{cite:R6}}

The SEI costs most of its lithium on the first charge, and then lets the cell work outside its electrolyte’s window.

The host decides how the ion can move once it is inside. In the layered oxides lithium travels within two-dimensional planes; the spinel LiMn₂O₄ offers a three-dimensional network; in olivine LiFePO₄ the paths are one-dimensional channels.{{cite:R6}}

Inside the solid the ion moves by diffusion, down its concentration gradient, as Fick’s first law describes; in a time t it typically gets about (2Dt)1/2 from where it started, where D is its diffusion coefficient.{{cite:B2}} Turned around, emptying a particle of size L takes a time of order L²/2D: halve the particle and it empties four times faster. Module 8 returns to that when the particles shrink to nanometres.

The pieces arrived one at a time. In 1972, at a conference at Belgirate, Steele singled out layered dichalcogenides such as TiS₂ as positive electrodes and Armand set out what an intercalation electrode needs; in 1976 lithium went reversibly into TiS₂ against a lithium-metal electrode.{{cite:R47,R44}} Exxon’s cell then failed on dendrites that shorted it and ignited the electrolyte.{{cite:R45}} Armand’s answer in the late 1970s was the rocking chair, and Lazzari and Scrosati built the first one, LiₓWO₂ against TiS₂, at about 2 V.{{cite:R47,R44}}

Around 1980 Goodenough’s group showed that over half the lithium could be taken out of LiCoO₂ reversibly, at about 4 V, so that a cell could be built discharged.{{cite:R45,R47}} In 1983 Yoshino found that petroleum coke cycled in a carbonate electrolyte and proposed carbon against LiCoO₂; Sony’s cell of June 1991 delivered about 80 Wh/kg and 200 Wh/L, more than any other rechargeable of the time.{{cite:R47}}

With the LiPF₆ in EC/DMC electrolyte proposed in 1993 and decades of refinement, the same chemistry reached about 250 Wh/kg and 600 Wh/L by 2020.{{cite:R47}} Since its launch the lithium-ion cell has gained a factor of three to four in energy density while its price fell by a factor of 18.{{cite:R48}}

You can now explain where the lithium sits in graphite and in LiCoO₂, why the cell is called a rocking chair, why a new cell loses lithium on its first charge and what it gets in return, how the host shapes the ion’s path, and how the cell came together between 1972 and 1996.

The ion had to cross something to get there.

Go deeper: why the SEI forms where it does

Goodenough and Park draw the electrolyte as having its own window: an energy gap between its lowest unoccupied and highest occupied molecular orbitals. A negative electrode whose electrochemical potential μA lies above the lowest unoccupied orbital reduces the electrolyte; a positive electrode whose μC lies below the highest occupied orbital oxidizes it, unless a passivating layer blocks the electron transfer. For the carbonate electrolytes the lowest unoccupied orbital sits about 1.1 eV below the lithium level and the practical limit at the top is about 4.3 eV below it, which on the voltage scale of module 3 is the window from about 1.1 to about 4.3 V vs Li/Li⁺. Graphite, at about 0.2 V, is outside it, and so works only behind its SEI.{{cite:R6}}

The orbital picture is a simplification. Real reduction and oxidation potentials depend on the salt and on the molecules around each one: estimates from isolated molecules can be far off: oxidizing EC next to PF₆⁻, with a hydrogen passed to a neighbour, takes almost 2 V less than oxidizing an isolated EC molecule,{{cite:R51}} and in an EC/EMC electrolyte reduction is measured to start at about 1.7 V with LiPF₆ but about 0.8 V with LiBF₄.{{cite:R52}} Most solvents and salt anions are thermodynamically unstable where lithium inserts into graphite; the electrolyte survives there only kinetically, behind the insulating interphase it has built.{{cite:R51}}

What a good SEI has to do comes down to four things: block electrons, so that no more electrolyte is reduced; let Li⁺ through without much hindrance; stay chemically stable, neither reacting with nor dissolving into the electrolyte; and stay mechanically intact while the electrode under it swells and shrinks.{{cite:R52}} Peled introduced the idea of an electronically insulating, ionically conducting interphase in 1979.{{cite:R52}}

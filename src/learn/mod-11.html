Module 11

Testing and diagnosis

How would you find out which thief it was?

Where we are. In {{ch:degradation}} we named the thieves that take a cell’s capacity. From the outside they all look alike: the cell holds less. How can we see these losses from outside, and tell one from another? In this module we build up the measurements that do it, from a single current pulse to a full impedance spectrum, and we learn to read the results honestly: what each one answers, when its formula holds, and how to analyse the data without fooling ourselves.

Let us put your cell back on the test bench of {{ch:polarization}}. Every measurement answers one question, so we choose the question first. Cycling at constant current gives the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the state of health; switching the current off and timing the recovery separates the three polarizations (figure {{fig:f5-4}}).{{cite:R1}} A short current pulse gives a resistance. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique, GITT, measures how fast lithium diffuses inside the electrode material.{{cite:R38,R67}} Impedance spectroscopy separates the processes in a cell by their speed.{{cite:R63}}

We take them in that order, from the simplest reading to the analysis behind published diffusion coefficients and impedance spectra. On the way we lean on three tools from {{ch:polarization}}: the test bench (figure {{fig:f5-1}}), the three clocks of polarization (figure {{fig:f5-4}}) and differential capacity (figure {{fig:f5-9}}).

Every technique answers one question; choosing the question comes first.

The simplest reading: a current pulse

We begin with the simplest question: how much does the cell resist? Let us switch on a constant current and watch the voltage. It jumps at once; the jump divided by the current, \Delta V/\Delta I, is the ohmic resistance of electrolyte, active material, current collectors and contacts. Industry calls this the DC internal resistance. After the jump the voltage keeps moving, first as the interfaces charge (charge transfer), then, more slowly, as diffusion sets in.{{cite:R64}}

So the value we get depends on when we read it, and also on how fast the instrument samples, on the size of the current, on heating and on the change of state of charge during the pulse. That is why test standards specify pulse lengths and currents for “pulse power” tests.{{cite:R64}} A pulse of length t probes roughly what an alternating current of frequency 1/(2\pi t) probes: values from the two methods approximately agree when the timescales match.{{cite:R64}}

Worked example. The cell in the figure, pulsed with 2 A. read at 0.1 ms: R \approx 20.5\ \mathrm{m\Omega}, so \Delta V \approx 41\ \mathrm{mV} read at 10 ms: 28 mΩ; at 1 s: 37 mΩ; at 10 s: 44 mΩ, so 88 mV Same cell, same current: more than a factor of two depending on the reading time.

A resistance quoted without its timescale is half a number.

GITT: a diffusion coefficient from a voltage

A pulse tells us how much the cell resists, but the slow, diffusive part of its answer is mixed in with everything else. If we keep the pulse short, let the cell rest afterwards and repeat, the voltage curve becomes a diffusion measurement: this is GITT. It starts from equilibrium, with the same lithium content everywhere. A constant current pulse of length \tau removes (or adds) a known amount of lithium through the surface; a rest follows until the electrode is in equilibrium again; and the pair is repeated across the whole charge.{{cite:R67}}

During the pulse, lithium is taken from the surface faster than diffusion can refill it from inside, so the surface composition runs ahead and the voltage climbs: the transient change \Delta E_t = E_3 - E_2, measured after the immediate IR jump. After the rest the electrode has settled at a slightly different composition: the steady-state change \Delta E_s = E_4 - E_1, a step along the equilibrium curve. The slower the diffusion, the larger \Delta E_t compared with \Delta E_s. Weppner and Huggins turned the ratio into the chemical diffusion coefficient:{{cite:R38,R67}}

D = \frac{4}{\pi\tau}\left(\frac{m_B V_M}{M_B S}\right)^2\left(\frac{\Delta E_s}{\Delta E_t}\right)^2

Here m_B, M_B and V_M are the mass, molar mass and molar volume of the electrode material and S its area in contact with the electrolyte; the bracket m_B V_M/(M_B S) is volume over area, the thickness of a film. For spherical particles of radius r it becomes r/3.{{cite:R67,R69}} The IR jump is left out of \Delta E_t because it is not diffusion: it holds the ohmic and the charge-transfer drops.{{cite:R68,R69}}

Worked example. Particles of radius 5 µm, a 30 min pulse, ΔEs = 10 mV and ΔEt = 30 mV. D = \frac{4}{\pi \times 1800\ \mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{5\ \mu\mathrm{m}}{3}\right)^2\left(\frac{10}{30}\right)^2 = 2.2\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m^2/s} = 2.2 × 10⁻¹² cm²/s check: D\tau/r^2 = 2.2\times10^{-16} \times 1800 / (5\times10^{-6})^2 = 0.016 The check, explained with figure {{fig:f11-4}}, says whether the pulse was short enough for the formula; for a sphere, 0.016 just passes.{{cite:R66}}

Before we trust such a number, we should ask what the formula assumes. It rests on two straight lines. First, the steady-state step must be small, so that the equilibrium curve is straight over it. Second, during the pulse the voltage must grow as \sqrt{t}, which is true only while the disturbed layer near the surface is thin compared with the sample: the short-time solution of the diffusion equation.{{cite:R67}}

What decides “short” is the pulse length in units of the time diffusion needs to cross the sample, \hat\tau = D\tau/L^2. The recommended limit is \hat\tau < 0.25 for flat samples and about ten times less for spheres and cylinders, and one survey found that most published GITT studies used longer pulses.{{cite:R66}} Since D is what we are measuring, we make the check afterwards, with the value just obtained. For spheres a second criterion applies: the \sqrt{t} law is within 5 % of the exact solution only for Dt/r^2 < 0.0032.{{cite:R68}}

Particle size is the other trap. The formula needs the diffusion length, and a porous electrode has secondary particles several micrometres across made of primary grains a few hundred nanometres across. Because D scales with r^2, choosing 5 µm instead of 0.25 µm gives a value 400 times larger.{{cite:R68}} A published diffusion coefficient means little without the radius it was referred to.

Short pulse, small step, known size: without all three, the GITT formula returns a number, not a diffusion coefficient.

Even with a short enough pulse, the answer depends on how we read the voltage. In one study the same GITT data on an NCM523 positive electrode were analysed by six procedures: two formulas that need the IR drop read off the curve (P1, P2), a straight-line fit of E against \sqrt{t} (P3), a two-line fit that leaves out the start (P4), and fits of the whole pulse to the spherical solution, with and without the start (P5, P6). At 40 °C the results differed by a factor of 3; on cooling, by more than an order of magnitude.{{cite:R68}}

The reason is the start of the pulse. The ohmic drop is instant, but the charge-transfer drop arrives only as the double layer charges, with time constant \tau_{dl} = R_{ct}C_{dl}: below 1 s above 0 °C at medium lithium content, far longer in the cold.{{cite:R68}} A recipe that reads the IR drop at a fixed 5 s, or fits a line through the start, mistakes double-layer charging for diffusion. Procedures that skip the transition (P4, P6) are expected to be more reliable; but if R_{ct} itself changes during the pulse, the full fit P6 is thrown off most.{{cite:R68}}

In practice this means: measure \tau_{dl} by impedance before each pulse; use a three-electrode cell, so that the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage, large at low temperature, is not mistaken for slow diffusion; keep the current small (0.1C agreed with 0.05C); and check that two currents give the same answer.{{cite:R68}}

The pulse itself is the weak point, so a more robust route reads diffusion from the rest after the pulse. During the pulse the voltage carries the IR drop on top of the diffusion signal, and if the total resistance drifts, as charge-transfer resistance does when the composition changes, the \sqrt t slope is wrong. In one worked example, a 17 % fall in resistance during the pulse halves the slope and makes D four times too large. During the rest no current flows, so no IR drop can drift.{{cite:R66}}

The rest can be solved exactly: plotted against \sqrt{t + \tau} - \sqrt{t}, where t is the time since the current stopped, the voltage is a straight line whose slope gives D, without needing the final equilibrium voltage. Fast processes that are not bulk diffusion, such as a surface layer, appear at the start of the rest and can be left out of the fit; the finite size of the sample bends the curve near the end, so the straight part in between is used, which for a short pulse lasts up to about ten pulse lengths of rest. The recommended procedure: large, dense, single-phase samples; at least three pulses of different size; and the \hat\tau check afterwards.{{cite:R66}}

The rest must also be long enough. In one study an NMC622 electrode needed about 7 to 9 h to relax below 3.8 V, and above 3.8 V the voltage was still drifting by 0.1 to 0.6 mV per hour after 9 to 12 h; published studies use fixed rests from 40 min to a day.{{cite:R69}} The same study showed that ignoring diffusion in the electrolyte inside the pores, as the simple formula does, made its GITT value about four times smaller than a transmission-line analysis of impedance (figure {{fig:f11-11}}).{{cite:R69}}

GITT is easy to run and easy to misread: check the pulse length, the reading of the IR drop, the rest and the size.

So far we have looked at one step at a time. A whole GITT run gives more than a diffusion coefficient. The voltage at the end of each rest is a quasi-open-circuit voltage (QOCV); joined up, these trace the equilibrium curve of the electrode. The voltage at the end of each pulse is the closed-circuit voltage (CCV). Their difference is the overpotential at that state of charge, and dividing by the current gives an internal resistance. With D from each step, the run yields three curves against composition.{{cite:R67}}

The price is time: sixteen steps of 10 min with 4 h rests take nearly three days, and where relaxation is slow even 4 h may not be enough.{{cite:R69}}

running and analysing a GITT experiment
  1. Build a cell with the electrode against lithium, preferably with a third, reference electrode, so that the counter electrode’s own overvoltage, large at low temperature, is not read as slow diffusion.{{cite:R68}} Before each pulse, measure the double-layer time constant by impedance.{{cite:R68}}
  2. Start from equilibrium, with the same lithium content everywhere in the material.{{cite:R67}}
  3. Apply a small constant current (0.1C gave the same result as 0.05C in one study) for a pulse of length τ, then switch it off.{{cite:R68}}
  4. Rest at open circuit until the voltage stops moving. Fixed rests of 40 min to a day are published; one NMC622 electrode needed 7 to 9 h below 3.8 V, and above 3.8 V was still drifting after 9 to 12 h.{{cite:R69}}
  5. Repeat pulse and rest across the whole composition range, recording the full voltage transient; the analysis uses the voltage before the pulse, at its start, at its end and after the rest, and the shape of the rest.{{cite:R67,R66}}
  6. Analyse each step with the Weppner–Huggins formula, using r/3 for spherical particles and leaving the IR jump out of ΔEt; or fit the rest with √(t + τ) − √t, which avoids the drifting IR drop.{{cite:R67,R66}}
  7. Check afterwards that Dτ/L² was below 0.25 for a film, about ten times less for spheres, and repeat with at least two, better three, pulse sizes.{{cite:R66,R68}} State the particle radius you used: taking 5 µm instead of 0.25 µm changes D by a factor of 400.{{cite:R68}}
Planning the time: 16 steps of a 10 min pulse and a 4 h rest take 16 × 4.17 h = 67 h, nearly three days.{{cite:R69}} The quasi-open-circuit voltages at the ends of the rests trace the equilibrium curve as a by-product.{{cite:R67}}

Impedance spectroscopy, from the beginning

Pulses and GITT work in time: we push the cell and watch every process answer on its own clock, all mixed in one voltage curve. Impedance spectroscopy works in frequency instead. It disturbs the cell with a small sine, a voltage or a current, and records the sine that answers, at many frequencies one after the other. If the cell behaves linearly and does not change during the measurement, the answer has the same frequency and differs only in size and timing.{{cite:R63}} The ratio of the two amplitudes is |Z|; the timing difference is the phase \varphi. Both fit in one complex number:{{cite:R63}}

Z = \frac{V_0}{I_0}\,e^{j\varphi} = Z' + jZ'', \qquad Z' = |Z|\cos\varphi,\quad Z'' = |Z|\sin\varphi

Each circuit element has its own signature. A resistor: Z = R, in phase, at every frequency. A capacitor: Z = 1/(j\omega C), with the current leading by 90° and the size falling as frequency rises (\omega = 2\pi f). An inductor: Z = j\omega L, current lagging, size rising.{{cite:R63,B2}} Because the three depend so differently on frequency, sweeping the frequency separates them.{{cite:R63}}

The instrument is a potentiostat with a frequency response analyser, which correlates the response with a sine and a cosine at the excitation frequency over whole periods to get the two parts of Z.{{cite:R63}} Commercial instruments span about 10 µHz to 1 MHz, but slow points are expensive: a single measurement at 10 µHz takes 27.8 h, so in practice the lowest frequency is about 1 mHz, often 10 to 100 mHz. Sixty frequencies from 100 kHz down to 0.1 Hz take 2 to 3 minutes.{{cite:R63}}

Worked example. Peak, peak-to-peak or rms? A published amplitude of “10 mV” could mean any of them.{{cite:R63}} V_p = 10\ \mathrm{mV}: peak-to-peak V_{pp} = 2V_p = 20\ \mathrm{mV} root mean square V_{rms} = V_p/\sqrt{2} = 7\ \mathrm{mV} A factor of nearly three between the largest and smallest reading of the same signal.

Once we have Z at many frequencies, we need to draw it, and there are two standard ways. The Nyquist plot puts -Z'' upward against Z', one point per frequency, high frequencies on the left; a resistor in parallel with a capacitor draws a semicircle of diameter R. Its weaknesses: the frequency of each point is not shown, and the large low-frequency values set the scale and squash the high-frequency end. The Bode plot shows |Z| and the phase against the logarithm of frequency, so every point’s frequency can be read and each decade gets equal room.{{cite:R63}}

Each R‖C pair has a time constant \tau = RC, and its arc peaks where \omega\tau = 1, that is at f = 1/(2\pi\tau).{{cite:R63}} Two pairs give two separate arcs only if their time constants differ by about a factor of 100 or more; closer, the arcs merge, and at equal \tau a single semicircle remains.{{cite:R63}} One rule is often broken: the two axes of a Nyquist plot must have the same scale; stretching the vertical one makes depressed arcs look round.{{cite:R63,R64}}

Worked example. Charge transfer in a commercial cell: Rct = 10 mΩ in parallel with a double layer of Cdl = 2 F. \tau = R_{ct}C_{dl} = 0.010\ \Omega \times 2\ \mathrm{F} = 0.020\ \mathrm{s} f = 1/(2\pi\tau) = 1/(2\pi \times 0.020\ \mathrm{s}) = 8 Hz Milliohms times farads still make milliseconds: the arcs of a large cell sit at hertz, as in figure {{fig:f11-10}}.

With these two pictures we can read the spectrum of a real cell, from left to right, high frequency to low. At the top of the range the cables and the wound electrodes act as an inductance, so the spectrum starts below the axis. Where it crosses the axis is the ohmic resistance, the sum of electrolyte, active material, current collectors and contacts. In the middle range come arcs from the interfaces, each a resistance in parallel with a capacitance; at low frequency a tail from lithium diffusing inside the particles.{{cite:R64}}

A commercial cell has large electrodes and so an impedance in the milliohm range, mostly below 0.1 Ω and at frequencies below a few tens of kilohertz. That makes it hard to measure: a small voltage drives a large current, of the order of an ampere.{{cite:R64}} And the interpretation is ambiguous: each electrode has its own SEI and its own charge transfer, at least four processes, but with only two terminals arcs of similar time constant merge into one or two.{{cite:R64}} Changing the state of charge or the temperature, which moves the processes by different amounts, helps tell them apart.{{cite:R64}}

Manufacturers use a single frequency, typically 1 kHz, to check and match cells on the production line; researchers use full spectra to follow degradation, which is the most common reason impedance is measured on commercial cells.{{cite:R64}}

Left to right is fast to slow: wires, ohmic parts, interfaces, diffusion.

The spectrum of figure {{fig:f11-10}} is idealized; real spectra are never made of perfect semicircles. Arcs are depressed: their centres sit below the axis. The usual fix replaces the capacitor with a constant phase element, Z = 1/(Y_0 (j\omega)^n): n = 1 is a capacitor, n = 0 a resistor and n = 0.5 a Warburg diffusion line. Surface roughness, fractal geometry and adsorption have all been proposed as its cause, and the question is not settled.{{cite:R63}} A CPE with a free exponent can fit almost any valid data, so its parameters cannot be read as real capacitances.{{cite:R64}}

Diffusion into a layer of finite thickness starts as the 45° Warburg line, then bends. If the far side lets lithium through, the line bends back to the axis in an arc; if it blocks lithium, as the inside of a particle does, the line turns vertical like a capacitor.{{cite:R63}} Particles are closer to spheres than to films: with the same diffusion resistance R_d and capacitance C_d, a sphere turns upward earlier and its tail stands at R_d/15 instead of R_d/3, and the diffusion coefficient follows as D = r^2/(R_d C_d).{{cite:R69}}

A porous electrode is a ladder: the electrolyte resistance runs down each pore, and every grain wall has its own interface. The result is a 45° line at high frequency followed by an arc.{{cite:R63,R64}} In the same study, fitted to an NMC622 electrode, such a transmission-line model gave a diffusion coefficient about four times larger than GITT or impedance models that put diffusion in simple series.{{cite:R69}}

Measuring well

So far we have trusted the measurement itself. Let us now check it, starting with the size of the sine. Impedance theory assumes a linear system, and an electrode reaction is not one: its current grows exponentially with overpotential. Close to equilibrium the Butler–Volmer curve is nearly straight with slope 1/R_{ct}, and that straight piece is what impedance measures. A nonlinear response to a pure sine is distorted; it contains signals at 2f, 3f and higher, because the harmonics reflect the curvature of the curve.{{cite:B2}}

The amplitude is therefore a compromise: large enough for a clean signal, small enough to stay linear. Under voltage control 5 to 10 mV is usual, and the Lissajous plot shows when it is too large.{{cite:R63}} Under current control, 0.1 to 1 A on a commercial cell gives a response of a few millivolts and may be more accurate.{{cite:R64}} The total harmonic distortion, the harmonics’ combined size over the fundamental, checks linearity at every frequency.{{cite:R64}}

Nonlinear methods turn this round. NLEIS drives the cell moderately harder and reads the harmonics as “higher-order impedance”; NFRA uses large currents to excite them on purpose. In one study the ratio of third to second harmonic rose in cells aged at −10 °C, where lithium had plated, while ordinary spectra looked the same. There is no standard method yet, and large currents can damage the cell.{{cite:R64,R65}}

Worked example. How large is Rct for an exchange current i₀ = 1 mA at 25 °C? R_{ct} = \frac{RT}{F i_0} = \frac{8.314 \times 298.15}{96485 \times 0.001}\ \Omega = 25.7 Ω A 1000 times larger exchange current, as on a large electrode, gives 25.7 mΩ.{{cite:B2}}

Next come the wires. With two terminals the analyser passes the current and measures the voltage through the same wires, so it measures the cell plus the wires. With four, one pair carries the current and a second pair senses the voltage at the cell’s own terminals; since the sense pair carries almost no current, its wires drop no voltage, and only the cell is measured. For batteries, where the wires are not negligible, four terminals are recommended.{{cite:R63,R64}}

Cables also bring inductance, proportional to their length and most visible on low-impedance cells: the high-frequency end of the spectrum drops further below the axis.{{cite:R63}} Short cables, current and sense wires twisted in pairs, a metal box around the cell, the same cables for calibration and measurement, and a fixed holder all help; simply re-inserting a cell in its holder changed the ohmic resistance by up to 10 % in one study.{{cite:R64}}

The cell must also be steady, and that takes several steps: set the state of charge, then rest until the voltage stops moving (4 h was found adequate in one study; others report 15 min to 100 h, and half of the surveyed studies did not say); let the temperature settle, remembering that the inside of the cell lags the chamber; and set the state of charge before changing the temperature.{{cite:R64}}

With a small sine, a good connection and a steady cell, one test remains before we model anything. A spectrum is worth modelling only if it could have come from a system that was linear, causal (the response comes only from the excitation), stable (unchanged during the sweep) and finite. For such a system the real and imaginary parts are tied together by the Kramers–Kronig relations: one can be computed from the other.{{cite:R63,R64}}

The relations need every frequency from zero to infinity, which no measurement has, so in practice a test circuit is fitted instead: many R‖C elements with fixed, evenly spread time constants, only their resistances free. Any valid spectrum can be fitted this way; if the fit fails, something is wrong with the data.{{cite:R63,R64}} The residuals should be small and scattered randomly around zero, well below 1 %.{{cite:R63,R64}} The test is used in only a minority of published work on commercial cells, though more often now that DRT, which needs excellent data, has spread.{{cite:R64}}

recording an impedance spectrum you can trust
  1. Set the state of charge, then rest until the voltage stops moving (4 h was adequate in one study) and the temperature has settled; set the state of charge before changing the temperature.{{cite:R64}}
  2. Connect four terminals, a current pair and a separate sense pair, with short cables twisted in pairs, the cell in a fixed holder inside a metal box, and the same cables for calibration and measurement.{{cite:R63,R64}}
  3. Choose the excitation: under voltage control 5 to 10 mV is usual, and the Lissajous plot shows when it is too large; on a low-impedance commercial cell, current control (0.1 to 1 A) gives a response of a few millivolts. Check the total harmonic distortion.{{cite:R63,R64}}
  4. Choose the frequencies, typically from about 100 kHz down to 10 to 100 mHz; in practice about 1 mHz is the lowest, because slow points are expensive.{{cite:R63}}
  5. Test the spectrum with Kramers–Kronig before modelling it: the residuals should be small and scattered randomly around zero, well below 1 %.{{cite:R63,R64}}
  6. Fit a circuit whose elements are tied to processes in the cell, with as few as possible; remember that the fit can depend on the starting guesses and the weighting; and plot the Nyquist diagram with equal axes.{{cite:R63,R64}}
Why the low end costs time: one period at 10 mHz lasts 1/0.01 = 100 s, at 1 mHz 1000 s; a single point at 10 µHz takes 27.8 h, while sixty frequencies from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz take 2 to 3 minutes.{{cite:R63}}

Check the measurement before believing the model: rest, temperature, connection, amplitude, then the Kramers–Kronig test.

From spectrum to meaning

With a valid spectrum in hand, we can ask what it means. The usual analysis fits an equivalent circuit to the spectrum by complex nonlinear least squares and judges the fit by its χ².{{cite:R63}} The trap is that circuits are not unique: different circuits can give exactly the same impedance at every frequency, and adding elements always improves the fit.{{cite:R63}} The same model can also return different values depending on the starting guesses and the weighting.{{cite:R64}}

So the circuit has to come from the physics of the cell, each element tied to a process, with as few elements as possible.{{cite:R63,R64}} Physics-based models go further: the transmission line of figure {{fig:f11-11}} with spherical diffusion in each grain, or full electrochemical models solved for their impedance.{{cite:R64,R69}}

If choosing the circuit is the problem, we can try not to choose one in advance. That is what the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) does. Every R‖C pair relaxes with one time constant, so a spectrum can be written as a sum, in the limit a distribution, of such pairs:{{cite:R64,R65}}

Z(\omega) = R_0 + R_{pol}\int_0^\infty \frac{g(\tau)}{1 + j\omega\tau}\,d\tau

Recovering g(\tau) turns the spectrum into peaks along the time axis, one per process, even where the arcs overlap in a Nyquist plot. That is why DRT is used on commercial cells: to count the processes and then build a circuit with the right number of elements.{{cite:R64,R65}}

The catch is that recovering g from noisy data is unstable and needs a regularization, a penalty of strength \lambda; only a narrow range of \lambda gives the right answer, and the number of peaks depends on it.{{cite:R64,R65}} Bad data points distort it strongly, so DRT needs a Kramers–Kronig check first, and because the inductive and diffusion parts distort it too, it is usually applied to the SEI and charge-transfer arcs.{{cite:R64}}

Following ageing

We now have the tools to do what {{ch:degradation}} asked of us: follow a cell as it ages. Impedance generally rises as a cell ages, and because the measurement does not harm the cell, it can be repeated through its whole life. Ageing studies run check-ups: the same measurement at fixed conditions every so many cycles, often enough to follow the ageing, rarely enough not to add to it.{{cite:R64}} Ageing has two visible effects: capacity fade, from losing active material, and power fade, from growing resistance through lost contacts, thickening surface films and delamination.{{cite:R64}}

A spectrum is reduced to a few features: fitted resistances, DRT peaks or impedance at chosen frequencies. Individual contributions may follow the state of health, and give early warning of sudden fade, better than the total impedance.{{cite:R64}} The difficulty is that temperature, state of charge and ageing all move the same spectrum; for some chemistries, LiFePO₄ in particular, estimating the state of charge from impedance is much harder than for NMC.{{cite:R64}}

Machine learning is the newest route: train a model on features from many cells, then predict the state of health or charge of a new one. Gaussian process regression is common, and models based on relaxation times have reached errors around 1 %.{{cite:R65}} Their knowledge ends at their training data, and the studies so far cover few systems and conditions.{{cite:R65}} Local impedance, measured on one spot of an electrode, is a further step, since a whole-cell spectrum averages over a structure that is not uniform.{{cite:R65}}

What a cyclic voltammogram is

In {{ch:lab/cell-testing}} we held the current and watched the potential. Cyclic voltammetry does the opposite: it drives the potential of an electrode and records the current that flows. The potential is swept linearly in time from an initial value to a switching potential and back; the rate of the sweep, in mV/s or V/s, is the scan rate, and the plot of current against potential is the cyclic voltammogram.{{cite:R83,R84}} A sweep in one direction only is linear sweep voltammetry.{{cite:R84}}

Why bother? A voltammogram shows at a glance where a material is oxidized and reduced and whether the reaction comes back; by changing the scan rate it separates fast from slow processes, diffusion from surface reactions, and it can estimate how fast ions diffuse.{{cite:R84}} That breadth is also the trap: the same review warns that interpretations of CV in battery research are often deficient or disputed, and that the technique should not be used without the basic electrochemistry behind it.{{cite:R84}} This page builds that basis, starting from a dissolved molecule and ending with battery electrodes and electrolytes.

The measurement needs three electrodes. The working electrode is where the reaction of interest happens: an inert disc of glassy carbon, platinum or gold, polished before each run, or, in battery work, the composite electrode itself. The reference electrode fixes the potential scale; the counter electrode, an inert platinum wire larger than the working electrode, carries the current so that the reference does not have to.{{cite:R83}} The solution holds the analyte in a large excess of supporting electrolyte, which carries the current and keeps the analyte from migrating in the field, so that it moves by diffusion alone; and dissolved oxygen, which gives peaks of its own, is removed by bubbling an inert gas through it first.{{cite:R83}}

The duck

Let us follow one sweep of a solution that holds only the reduced form, Red, of a couple Red = Ox + e⁻. The curve has the shape known as the duck, and two things make it. The first is the Nernst equation: at every moment the potential sets the ratio of the two forms at the electrode surface,{{cite:R83}}

E = E^{\circ\prime} + \frac{RT}{nF}\ln\frac{[\mathrm{Ox}]}{[\mathrm{Red}]}

so as the potential rises past the formal potential E°′, more and more of the Red at the surface must turn into Ox, and the current grows. The second is diffusion. The surface soon runs out of Red, and fresh Red has to diffuse in from the bulk. The region depleted of it, the diffusion layer, keeps growing through the sweep; the farther Red has to come, the slower it arrives, so past the peak the current falls although the potential keeps rising.{{cite:R83}}

On the way back the Ox made on the way out is reduced again, and a negative peak appears. For an electrochemically reversible one-electron couple the two peaks are 57 mV apart at 25 °C (2.22 RT/F), and their midpoint, E½, is the usual estimate of E°′.{{cite:R83}} A caution on reading other people’s plots: two sign conventions are in use, for which way potential increases along the axis and for which current is positive, and papers seldom say which they follow.{{cite:R83}} In this course oxidation current is positive and potential increases to the right.

Scan rate and the Randles-Sevcik equation

Sweep faster and the current grows, because the diffusion layer has less time to spread and stays thinner.{{cite:R83}} For a reversible couple whose species diffuse freely in solution, the peak current follows the Randles-Sevcik equation,{{cite:R83,R84}}

i_p = 0.446\,nFAC\left(\frac{nFvD}{RT}\right)^{1/2}

with A the electrode area (usually the geometric one), C the bulk concentration and D the diffusion coefficient. The peak current is proportional to the square root of the scan rate, and a straight line of ip against v1/2 is the test that the species diffuses freely rather than sitting adsorbed on the electrode; its slope gives D.{{cite:R83,R84}}

Every voltammogram also carries a current that has nothing to do with the analyte: charging the electrical double layer of {{ch:cell-voltage}}. This capacitive current, CdlA v, is proportional to the scan rate itself, so it grows faster with v than the diffusion peak does.{{cite:R83,R84}} That is why a background scan of the electrolyte alone comes first: it tests the cell and measures the capacitive current that must be subtracted.{{cite:R83}}

Worked example. At 25 °C the equation reads ip = 2.69 × 10⁵ n3/2A D1/2C v1/2, with ip in A, A in cm², D in cm²/s, C in mol/cm³ and v in V/s.{{cite:R84}} Take 1 mM (10⁻⁶ mol/cm³), a 3 mm disc (0.0707 cm²), D = 10⁻⁵ cm²/s, 100 mV/s. ip = 2.69 × 10⁵ × 0.0707 × (10⁻⁵)1/2 × 10⁻⁶ × (0.1)1/2 = 19.0 µA double layer, 20 µF/cm²: CdlA v = 20 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.0707 × 0.1 = 0.14 µA At 100 V/s the peak would be 32 times larger and the capacitive current 1000 times: the background that was negligible becomes the signal.

How fast is the electron?

The duck assumed that electron transfer at the surface is instantaneous, so that the Nernst equation holds at every moment. Such a couple is electrochemically reversible: the peak potentials do not depend on the scan rate, the peak separation stays at about 57 to 59 mV for one electron, and the two peak currents are equal.{{cite:R83,R84}} When electron transfer is slower, extra overpotential is needed to drive it, the peaks move apart and the separation grows with scan rate: a quasi-reversible process. Slower still, the process is irreversible, with the peaks far apart.{{cite:R84}} The scan-rate dependence is the test, and lithium insertion reactions tend to be quasi-reversible.{{cite:R84}}

Keep two meanings apart. Electrochemical reversibility is about the speed of electron transfer; chemical reversibility asks whether the product survives to be converted back. A follow-up chemical reaction that destroys the product shrinks the return peak, and a faster sweep, which outruns the chemistry, can bring it back.{{cite:R83}}

Molecules that take two electrons can show two waves. If the second electron is accepted more easily than the first, the two merge into one wave with a separation of 28.5 mV; if it is harder, the wave splits into two one-electron waves, each with 57 mV.{{cite:R83}} An organic electrode material from {{ch:sodium-organic}} gives an example: dissolved at 5 mM and swept on platinum, Li₂-PDFSA shows two successive oxidation waves at 0.23 and 0.08 V against the ferrocene couple, 3.66 and 3.51 V against lithium.{{cite:R78}} Ferrocene, added to the solution from the start, is the recommended reference in nonaqueous solvents, whose reference electrodes drift.{{cite:R83}}

Ohmic drop

The potentiostat controls the potential between the working and reference electrodes, but part of the solution resistance lies between them, uncompensated. The electrode then feels the applied potential minus i Ru, and the voltammogram stretches: the tell-tale sign is a peak separation larger than it should be for a couple known to be reversible, such as ferrocene in the same cell.{{cite:R83}} Ohmic drop and slow electron transfer look alike, and the larger the current, the worse it gets.

The remedies follow from i Ru. Pass less current, with a smaller electrode or a slower sweep; lower the resistance, with more supporting electrolyte; bring the reference closer to the working electrode. Many potentiostats can also measure Ru and compensate for it.{{cite:R83}} In a battery half-cell, the resistance of the porous electrode and of the separator adds to that of the solution, which is one reason battery CVs are run slowly.

Worked example. A 5 mM solution swept at 1 V/s on a 3 mm disc gives a peak of about 300 µA (figure {{fig:f11-21}}’s 19 µA, × 5 for the concentration, × 101/2 for the rate). i Ru = 300 µA × 200 Ω = 60 mV on each peak, in opposite directions The peak separation grows by tens of millivolts, enough to mistake a reversible couple for a quasi-reversible one.

Diffusion or surface? The b-value

Battery electrodes rarely store charge in one way only. Some current comes from diffusion into the bulk of the particles, which scales with v1/2; some from processes at the surface, double-layer charging or fast surface redox, which scale with v. Writing the peak current as a power law,{{cite:R84,R85}}

i = a\,v^{b}

b is the slope of log i against log v: 0.5 for semi-infinite diffusion, 1 for a surface-controlled process, and between them mixed control or diffusion over a finite length.{{cite:R85,R84}} A finer split works potential by potential: i(V) = k₁v + k₂v1/2, where a plot of i/v1/2 against v1/2 gives k₁ as slope and k₂ as intercept, and so the capacitive and diffusion-controlled fractions of the current.{{cite:R84,R85}}

These tools are popular and easily abused. The split ignores the shift of the peaks caused by ohmic losses, which becomes significant above about 10 mV/s; a b of 1 cannot tell double-layer charging from fast surface redox without other evidence; and electrochemical signatures alone cannot establish a mechanism.{{cite:R85}} In Nb₂O₅, b is close to 1 below 20 mV/s but falls to about 0.7 above it, which Kim and co-workers attribute to ohmic contributions of the active material and the SEI, and to diffusion.{{cite:R84}} Used carefully, the method shows, for instance, that smaller TiO₂ particles store more of their charge at the surface, and that a LiF–MnO composite stores about 94 % of its capacity in surface-controlled reactions.{{cite:R84}} Pseudocapacitive materials have a recognizable voltammogram: broad peaks whose anodic and cathodic branches are mirror images, barely moving with scan rate over some range; nearly all battery-type materials fail that test ({{ch:voltage-profiles}}).{{cite:R85}}

Battery electrodes

A battery electrode is a solid that takes up lithium, not a dissolved couple, but the voltammogram follows from the same bookkeeping. With the potential rising at v = dV/dt and the current being I = dQ/dt, the two combine into{{cite:R84}}

\frac{dQ}{dV} = \frac{I}{v}

so the current at each potential is the scan rate times the charge the electrode stores per volt there. At slow enough rates, a battery CV is the differential capacity of {{ch:lab/cell-testing}} turned on its side. It is not the same thing: a CV contains rate information that the dQ/dV plot of a galvanostatic test does not, it forces the potential rather than the current, and its i R drop changes along the sweep, so peak positions and shapes can shift.{{cite:R84}}

What shifts the peaks of a battery electrode? Smaller particles shorten the diffusion paths and raise the peak current; in silicon, the alloying peak that is barely visible with large particles becomes clear with small ones. Thick electrodes show peaks that split faster with scan rate than thin ones, because lithium has farther to go from the surface into the bulk. Higher temperature brings the peaks closer together and earlier.{{cite:R84}} So report the scan rate, the loading and the temperature with every battery CV, and compare voltammograms only at matching conditions. A slow example from the review: a LiₓMn₂O₄₊δ electrode swept at 0.04 mV/s against lithium, a full sweep up and down taking 6.9 hours.{{cite:R84}}

A diffusion coefficient from a voltammogram

The Randles-Sevcik slope is widely used to estimate an apparent diffusion coefficient of lithium in an electrode: plot the peak current against v1/2, and D follows from the slope, the area and the lithium concentration in the solid.{{cite:R84}} The conditions are strict. The analysis assumes semi-infinite diffusion in a single phase, so for a material that changes phase as it charges, such as LiMn₂O₄ with its two transitions, the numbers must be treated with care; the slope of log ip against log v should be 0.5, and when the resistance of the cell rather than diffusion limits the current, the relation breaks down.{{cite:R84}} At slow sweeps a thin electrode is filled throughout and its peak current grows with v, not v1/2.{{cite:R84}}

Figure {{fig:f11-26}} shows what follows in practice: CV and GITT or impedance ({{ch:lab/gitt}}, {{ch:lab/eis-measurement}}) give values that agree within a factor of a few for some materials and span orders of magnitude for others.{{cite:R84}} Quote an apparent D from CV with its method, its assumptions and its area, and compare trends rather than absolute numbers.

The stability window of an electrolyte

Voltammetry is also how the electrochemical stability of an electrolyte is measured, the window of {{ch:cell-voltage}}. A linear sweep runs the potential until one of the components starts to be oxidized or reduced and a current flows; the potential at which that current reaches a chosen cut-off is reported as the stability limit.{{cite:R86}} Single carbonate solvents measured this way, in a tetraalkylammonium salt on glassy carbon at 5 mV/s with a cut-off of 1 mA/cm², reach their oxidation limits between 6.2 V (EC) and 6.7 V (EMC, DEC) vs Li⁺/Li.{{cite:R54}}

Those numbers sit far above the 4.2 to 4.4 V at which carbonate electrolytes run in real cells, and the reasons are in the method. The cut-off current and the sweep rate are arbitrary choices that change the reported limit, as do the electrode, the electrolyte composition and whether a sweep or a step is used.{{cite:R86}} An inert, non-porous electrode such as platinum or glassy carbon measures the intrinsic stability of the liquid, but excludes the catalytic activity of porous transition-metal oxides, which a real positive electrode brings; Scheers and Johansson advise taking stability values from one source rather than mixing studies, and testing as close to the real cell as possible when the cell is the question.{{cite:R86}}

Before you press start

Most bad voltammograms are made before the first sweep. Polish the working electrode, and polish it again after each experiment, because analytes adsorb. Use a large excess of dry, pure supporting electrolyte. Remove oxygen by bubbling inert gas, then lift the gas line above the liquid. Run a background scan without the analyte. In nonaqueous solvents, add ferrocene from the start and quote potentials against it. Check the uncompensated resistance on a reversible couple, and compensate it if the instrument can. And say which sign convention the plot follows.{{cite:R83}} For a battery electrode, add the scan rate, the loading, the temperature and the cell format to that list.{{cite:R84}}

Building a cell that can be trusted

Before any measurement there is a cell to make, and most of the scatter in published cycling data is made at the bench, not at the cycler. A measurement institute that audited academic laboratories found the repeatability of coin-cell results “moderate to low” and the reproducibility between laboratories poor, and traced it to bespoke assembly methods; its guide treats assembly as a process, with a written procedure, training and a record for every cell.{{cite:R87}} We follow that guide here for the commonest format, the CR2032 half cell: 20 mm across and 3.2 mm tall, with the electrode under study facing a disc of lithium metal.{{cite:R87}}

The cell is built in an argon glovebox with oxygen and water below 0.1 ppm, and with a nitrogen filter, because lithium reacts with nitrogen as well; a dry room controls only moisture and is not enough.{{cite:R87}} The parts go in as {{fig:f11-28}} shows: the can, then the gasket with its narrower opening down against the can, the positive electrode, the separator, the electrolyte dropped onto the separator once it is in place, the lithium, two spacers (the top one may carry the wave washer) and the cap; the electrode is aligned by eye through the translucent separator.{{cite:R87}} Some laboratories build the same stack cap first, spring and spacers down, negative electrode, separator, positive electrode, can; what matters is that every cell of a batch is built the same way.{{cite:R88}} The lithium is punched at 15 mm and 200 µm, on a PEEK or POM base and never on PTFE, with a punch kept for lithium alone; only shiny, smooth, grey foil is used, and anything black, white or reddish is rejected.{{cite:R87}} The separator is a polypropylene-polyethylene trilayer, never dried above 50 °C, and filter paper is to be avoided.{{cite:R87}} The electrolyte, 100 µL from a calibrated pipette, is dispensed in the same amount in every cell, because any excess is squeezed out at the crimp and the amount then differs from cell to cell.{{cite:R87,R90}} The negative electrode is cut a little larger than the positive, so that a small misalignment does not leave positive material facing nothing; that is also how commercial cells are built.{{cite:R90}}

Three habits make the difference. Never mix parts from different suppliers, and record part numbers rather than descriptions: cans, caps, spacers and gaskets differ in diameter, thickness and coating, and a mismatch of about half a millimetre between the parts and the crimper has been found in several laboratories, one of which had its crimper retooled.{{cite:R87}} Take a break after every four or five cells, because fatigue is a source of error, and spread a large batch over several days.{{cite:R87}} And keep a spreadsheet with, for every cell, a unique number, the date and the assembler, the collector and electrode masses and the formulation, the diameters of the electrodes and separator, the electrolyte, the open-circuit voltage as assembled, and notes.{{cite:R87}}

A cell is a process: the same parts, the same order, the same amounts, and a record of each one.

How much is in the cell

Every capacity in mAh/g divides a measured charge by a mass, so the mass must be known at least as well as the charge. The electrode disc is weighed on a balance reading to 0.1 mg or better; at least ten bare collector discs are punched with the same punch and weighed, and their mean is subtracted; the active fraction of the formulation then gives the active mass.{{cite:R87}} The areal loading, in mg/cm² or mAh/cm², is that mass over the area of the disc, and it should be reported with every result: a thick electrode and a thin one of the same material behave differently at the same C-rate ({{ch:composite-electrodes}}).

The theoretical specific capacity of a material is nF/M, with n the electrons per formula unit and M its molar mass, which is 26 801 n/M in mAh/g: 372 mAh/g for graphite as LiC6 and about 274 mAh/g for LiCoO2.{{cite:R88}} The C-rate is the current divided by the capacity: 1C discharges the capacity in one hour, 2C in half an hour, C/2 in two hours.{{cite:R88}} Which capacity? A careful study defines it from the practical capacity measured in a slow cycle, C/20 or C/50, in a half cell, not from the theoretical value, because the practical value is what the electrode actually delivers;{{cite:R89}} whichever is chosen, it must be stated, because every current, every rate and every efficiency that follows depends on it.

Before the first cycle

The crimp makes the cell: a target of about 500 psi (3.4 MPa) on a typical manual crimper, undershooting rather than overshooting, because overpressure shorts cells and jams them in the die; one laboratory that pressed to over 700 psi lost a cell inside the glovebox.{{cite:R87}} Right after crimping the cell is wiped with a tissue of DMC, EMC or acetone, inside the glovebox and without immersing it, because the salt residue reacts with moisture to form HF, which corrodes the can and, later, the holders.{{cite:R87}}

Then the first measurement, with a hand-held meter: the open-circuit voltage as assembled. For an NMC 811 half cell with a carbonate electrolyte, 3.18 to 3.45 V is in range; a cell at 2.00 V or below is discarded; in between, the cell may cycle, but such cells are often irreproducible for no visible reason, and they are run at the user’s own risk.{{cite:R87}} The value is written in the spreadsheet and the number of the cell on the can, in permanent marker, never in graphite.{{cite:R87}}

Connecting is a step of its own. Holders suffer metal fatigue and lose contact pressure, and an exposed contact can short a cell, so every holder and lead is checked before every run; gold-plated holders with springs are preferred.{{cite:R87}} And a cell on a bench is a cell at whatever temperature the room has that day: coin cells exchange heat quickly with their surroundings, so the cycling happens in a temperature chamber, after a rest there for the cell to settle, because “room temperature” drifts and the drift shows up in the long-term data.{{cite:R90}}

A cell that reads wrong before the first cycle will not read right after it.

The cycling protocol

A galvanostatic cycle is written as a short programme. Constant current (CC) until an upper cut-off voltage; a constant-voltage (CV) hold at that cut-off until the current has decayed to a set fraction of the C-rate; a rest; a constant-current discharge to a lower cut-off; a rest. The cut-offs are chosen for the chemistry: 4.2 and 2.5 V for a layered-oxide full cell, 4.2 and 2.5 V for NMC against lithium, 1.5 and 0.005 V for a graphite-based negative electrode against lithium, the last chosen because lower voltages plated lithium as dendrites.{{cite:R88,R89}} Rests of two hours between steps let the cell relax before the next one.{{cite:R89}}

Why the CV hold? The constant-current step stops when the measured voltage, which is the open-circuit voltage plus the i R drop ({{ch:polarization}}), reaches the cut-off, not when the electrode is full. Holding the voltage lets the current fall and the hidden charge flow in; the faster the rate or the larger the resistance, the larger that share, as {{fig:f11-31}} shows. The capacity of each half cycle is the current integrated over time, which the cycler reports; the Coulombic efficiency is the discharge capacity divided by the charge capacity, and the difference between the two is the irreversible capacity.{{cite:R88}}

The first cycles are different. During them the SEI forms on the negative electrode by reduction of the electrolyte ({{ch:sei}}), consuming lithium that does not come back, so the first-cycle efficiency is low and is reported separately; a formation of two slow cycles, C/10 with CC-CV charging, precedes any test, and two further slow cycles recondition a cell before a new series.{{cite:R88,R89}} A slow cycle at C/20 also gives the pseudo-open-circuit curve of the cell: “pseudo” because the cell is never at open circuit and a small polarization remains in it.{{cite:R89}} Its differential capacity, {{fig:f5-9}}, shifts with polarization, the peaks moving to higher voltage on charge and lower on discharge.{{cite:R89}}

CC to the cut-off, CV until the current dies, rest, discharge: the cut-offs, the end current and the rests are part of the result.

What Coulombic efficiency can and cannot say

Coulombic efficiency is the measure of reversibility: the share of the charge put in that comes back out, with the rest gone into side reactions.{{cite:R88}} The arithmetic in {{fig:f11-32}} is the whole lesson. If a cell returns 99.9 % of its charge each cycle and loses the rest for good, it keeps 90 % after a hundred cycles and reaches 80 % after about 220; at 99.95 % it takes twice as long; at 99.99 %, ten times. A tenth of a percent per cycle is invisible in a single cycle and decisive over a thousand.

That sets the demands on the measurement. To tell a 99.9 % material from a 99.95 % one, the charge of a cycle must be measured to better than 0.05 %, which asks for a stable current, a stable temperature, a fixed rate and a fixed window; the same cell at a faster rate, a warmer chamber or a wider window gives another number. Ten cycles on an ordinary cycler cannot rank two materials by cycle life, and a plot that shows a Coulombic efficiency of “about 99 %” for a hundred cycles is showing a cell that has already lost most of its capacity. The first cycle is reported on its own, because its loss is the SEI forming rather than a steady leak.{{cite:R88}}

In a half cell the lithium counter electrode supplies lithium without limit, so the cell’s capacity can hold while the electrode under study is quietly consuming lithium; the efficiency, not the capacity, shows it. A full cell has only the lithium it started with, and the same loss appears as fade ({{ch:degradation}}). This is why the two formats are tested for different questions: the half cell for the electrode, the full cell for the pairing.

Efficiency, measured precisely, predicts; capacity retention over ten cycles does not.

Rate and temperature

A rate test cycles the cell a few times at each of a series of C-rates, slow to fast, and then slow again. The discharge capacity falls with the rate because the ohmic, activation and concentration polarizations grow with the current and bring the voltage to the cut-off before the electrode is empty; the ohmic part grows in proportion to the current.{{cite:R88}} The return to the slow rate is the control: if the capacity comes back, the fast cycles hid capacity without destroying it; if it does not, they did harm.

Temperature enters through the same polarizations. Warmer, the electrolyte is less viscous and more conductive and the ohmic and activation losses fall; colder, the kinetics slow, and the concentration polarization can push a graphite electrode to the potential at which lithium plates.{{cite:R88}} Warm cycling has its own cost, in the long run, in particle cracking and side reactions.{{cite:R88}} So a result quoted without its temperature is not comparable with any other, and a chamber with stable control is part of the test bench, not a luxury.{{cite:R90}}

How many cells, and what to report

One cell proves little. For laboratory coin cells, three to ten cells per batch are encouraged, enough to see the spread; a study of thirty identical cells showed that even smaller sets reveal a reasonable spread, and that spread belongs on the plot.{{cite:R90}} With every result, then: the format and parts, the electrode loading and formulation, the electrolyte and its volume, the formation, the window, the rates, the end-of-charge current, the rests, the temperature, the number of cells, and the capacity the C-rate was defined from. Cells that are built the same and tested the same can be compared; everything else is a comparison of procedures.

Report the conditions with the numbers, and the number of cells with the conditions.

When the outside is not enough

Everything so far was measured from outside, across both electrodes at once, and that is its limit. Telling the two electrodes apart needs a reference electrode inside the cell, placed where it does not disturb the current, which is still hard to build and not a production solution.{{cite:R48,R64}} The alternative is to open the cell. Before that come non-destructive checks: inspection, X-ray tomography, capacity, cyclic voltammetry and differential capacity. Then the cell is discharged slowly and opened in an argon glove box; its electrodes can be rebuilt into half cells or symmetric cells of two identical electrodes, whose spectra show which electrode carries which arc, and examined by microscopy and spectroscopy. A fresh cell treated the same way is the baseline.{{cite:R64}}

The full picture often needs this tear-down, with Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy, NMR, electron microscopy and X-ray absorption, while accelerating-rate calorimetry finds the conditions for thermal runaway.{{cite:R1}} Plastic lithium-ion cells opened the way to watching the electrodes while they work,{{cite:R2}} and an external pressure sensor can already follow the SEI growing through the swelling it causes.{{cite:R48}}

The cell itself has to be chosen too. The standard way to test a new positive electrode is a half-cell against lithium metal,{{cite:R6}} but half-cells can fail to predict how the same electrode behaves in a full cell, and small assembly errors change the result;{{cite:R21}} in GITT, the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage can pass for slow diffusion.{{cite:R68}}

You can now explain why a pulse resistance needs its timescale; how GITT turns two voltage changes into a diffusion coefficient, and the four checks that make it trustworthy (pulse length, IR drop, rest, particle size); what impedance is, and how to read Nyquist and Bode plots of a real cell from left to right; why the measurement must be small, steady, four-terminal and Kramers–Kronig valid; why a good circuit fit is not proof; and what DRT, nonlinear impedance and machine learning add.

We can now find out from outside what has gone wrong in a cell, and open it when the outside is not enough. The last module takes up what is left: how to keep the energy a cell stores from coming out all at once, and what becomes of the cell when it is truly finished.

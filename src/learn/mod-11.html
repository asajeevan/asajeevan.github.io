Module 11

Testing and diagnosis

How would you find out which thief it was?

Where we are. Module 10 named the ways a cell loses capacity. From the outside they all look alike: the cell holds less. This module is about the measurements that tell them apart, which ones leave the cell intact, and what each one actually answers.

Every measurement answers one question, so the question comes first. Cycling at constant current gives the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the state of health; switching the current off and timing the recovery separates the three polarizations (figure 5.4).{{cite:R1}} Impedance spectroscopy separates resistances by their speed; the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique, GITT, measures how fast lithium diffuses inside the electrode material.{{cite:R1,R38}} Measuring the open-circuit voltage at several temperatures gives the thermodynamics of the reaction itself.{{cite:R1}}

Every technique answers one question; choosing the question comes first.

Impedance spectroscopy applies a small alternating voltage and records the current at many frequencies, from fast to slow. Each process answers at its own speed, the same three clocks as in figure 5.4, read with an alternating current instead of a switch. The ohmic parts are independent of frequency; charge transfer with its double-layer capacitance draws a semicircle; diffusion draws a 45° line, the Warburg impedance. Real cells show capacitances of farads and resistances of milliohms (the figure uses small laboratory values).{{cite:R1}} The top of the semicircle lies at the angular frequency ω = 1/(RctCd), and its diameter is the charge-transfer resistance.{{cite:B2}}

That is why impedance is used to follow ageing: a semicircle that grows from one check-up to the next is an interface getting thicker. Recording how a cell’s resistance evolves during operation is one of the ways its state of health is tracked, and the state of charge is estimated from a combination of impedance and resistance measurements, current pulses, counting the charge in and out, and the open-circuit voltage.{{cite:R48}}

Worked example. Where is the top of the semicircle for Rct = 20 Ω and Cd = 20 μF? ω = 1/(RctCd) = 1/(20 Ω × 20 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 2500 rad/s f = ω/2π = 398 Hz; Rct = 40 Ω gives 199 Hz Doubling Rct doubles the diameter of the semicircle and halves the frequency at its top.{{cite:B2}}

GITT turns the voltage curve into a diffusion measurement. A short current pulse changes the lithium content of the electrode by a known amount; a rest lets it settle. The settled change, ΔEs, is a step along the open-circuit curve; the change during the pulse, ΔEt, without the IR drop, reflects the lithium content at the particle surface running ahead of the interior, because diffusion cannot keep up. Weppner and Huggins turned the two into the chemical diffusion coefficient, valid as long as the pulse is short compared with L²/D, the time lithium needs to diffuse across the sample (for a porous electrode, S is the contact area between active material and electrolyte).{{cite:R38}}

Charge-discharge and impedance measurements are nondestructive; the full picture often needs a tear-down, opening the cell and examining its parts with Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy, NMR, electron microscopy and X-ray absorption, while accelerating-rate calorimetry finds the conditions for thermal runaway.{{cite:R1}} Plastic lithium-ion cells opened the way to watching the electrodes while they work, with X-ray absorption, NMR, Mössbauer spectroscopy and the electron microscope.{{cite:R2}} Sensing is moving towards the cell itself: an external pressure sensor can already follow the SEI growing, through the irreversible swelling it causes, and sensors inside the cell would add where it happens.{{cite:R48}}

The cell itself has to be chosen too. The standard way to test a new positive electrode is a half-cell against lithium metal,{{cite:R6}} but Murray, Hall and Dahn show that half-cells can fail to predict how the same electrode behaves in a full cell, and that small assembly errors, such as a misaligned stack in a coin cell, change the result.{{cite:R21}}

Telling the two electrodes apart inside one cell needs a stable reference electrode placed where it does not disturb the current, which is still hard to build.{{cite:R48}}

You can now explain what question each of four standard measurements answers, how to read a Nyquist plot from left to right, how GITT gets a diffusion coefficient from two voltage changes, and why the cell format and the reference matter.

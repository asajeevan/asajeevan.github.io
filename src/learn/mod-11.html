Module 11

Testing and diagnosis

How would you find out which thief it was?

Where we are. Module 10 named the ways a cell loses capacity. From the outside they all look alike: the cell holds less. This module is about the measurements that tell them apart, from a single current pulse to a full impedance spectrum, and about reading the results honestly: what each one answers, when its formula holds, and how to analyse the data without fooling yourself.

Every measurement answers one question, so the question comes first. Cycling at constant current gives the capacity, the effect of rate and temperature, and the state of health; switching the current off and timing the recovery separates the three polarizations (figure 5.4).{{cite:R1}} A short current pulse gives a resistance. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique, GITT, measures how fast lithium diffuses inside the electrode material.{{cite:R38,R67}} Impedance spectroscopy separates the processes in a cell by their speed.{{cite:R63}}

This module goes through them in that order, from the simplest reading to the analysis behind published diffusion coefficients and impedance spectra. It builds on module 5: the test bench (figure 5.1), the three clocks of polarization (figure 5.4) and differential capacity (figure 5.9).

Every technique answers one question; choosing the question comes first.

The simplest reading: a current pulse

Switch on a constant current and watch the voltage. It jumps at once; the jump divided by the current, \Delta V/\Delta I, is the ohmic resistance of electrolyte, active material, current collectors and contacts. Industry calls this the DC internal resistance. After the jump the voltage keeps moving, first as the interfaces charge (charge transfer), then, more slowly, as diffusion sets in.{{cite:R64}}

So the value depends on when it is read, and also on how fast the instrument samples, on the size of the current, on heating and on the change of state of charge during the pulse. That is why test standards specify pulse lengths and currents for “pulse power” tests.{{cite:R64}} A pulse of length t probes roughly what an alternating current of frequency 1/(2\pi t) probes: values from the two methods approximately agree when the timescales match.{{cite:R64}}

Worked example. The cell in the figure, pulsed with 2 A. read at 0.1 ms: R \approx 20.5\ \mathrm{m\Omega}, so \Delta V \approx 41\ \mathrm{mV} read at 10 ms: 28 mΩ; at 1 s: 37 mΩ; at 10 s: 44 mΩ, so 88 mV Same cell, same current: more than a factor of two depending on the reading time.

A resistance quoted without its timescale is half a number.

GITT: a diffusion coefficient from a voltage

GITT turns the voltage curve into a diffusion measurement. It starts from equilibrium, with the same lithium content everywhere. A constant current pulse of length \tau removes (or adds) a known amount of lithium through the surface; a rest follows until the electrode is in equilibrium again; and the pair is repeated across the whole charge.{{cite:R67}}

During the pulse, lithium is taken from the surface faster than diffusion can refill it from inside, so the surface composition runs ahead and the voltage climbs: the transient change \Delta E_t = E_3 - E_2, measured after the immediate IR jump. After the rest the electrode has settled at a slightly different composition: the steady-state change \Delta E_s = E_4 - E_1, a step along the equilibrium curve. The slower the diffusion, the larger \Delta E_t compared with \Delta E_s. Weppner and Huggins turned the ratio into the chemical diffusion coefficient:{{cite:R38,R67}}

D = \frac{4}{\pi\tau}\left(\frac{m_B V_M}{M_B S}\right)^2\left(\frac{\Delta E_s}{\Delta E_t}\right)^2

Here m_B, M_B and V_M are the mass, molar mass and molar volume of the electrode material and S its area in contact with the electrolyte; the bracket m_B V_M/(M_B S) is volume over area, the thickness of a film. For spherical particles of radius r it becomes r/3.{{cite:R67,R69}} The IR jump is left out of \Delta E_t because it is not diffusion: it holds the ohmic and the charge-transfer drops.{{cite:R68,R69}}

Worked example. Particles of radius 5 µm, a 30 min pulse, ΔEs = 10 mV and ΔEt = 30 mV. D = \frac{4}{\pi \times 1800\ \mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{5\ \mu\mathrm{m}}{3}\right)^2\left(\frac{10}{30}\right)^2 = 2.2\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m^2/s} = 2.2 × 10⁻¹² cm²/s check: D\tau/r^2 = 2.2\times10^{-16} \times 1800 / (5\times10^{-6})^2 = 0.016 The check, explained with figure 11.4, says whether the pulse was short enough for the formula; for a sphere, 0.016 just passes.{{cite:R66}}

The formula rests on two straight lines. First, the steady-state step must be small, so that the equilibrium curve is straight over it. Second, during the pulse the voltage must grow as \sqrt{t}, which is true only while the disturbed layer near the surface is thin compared with the sample: the short-time solution of the diffusion equation.{{cite:R67}}

What decides “short” is the pulse length in units of the time diffusion needs to cross the sample, \hat\tau = D\tau/L^2. Kang and Chueh recommend \hat\tau < 0.25 for flat samples and about ten times less for spheres and cylinders, and they found that most published GITT studies used longer pulses.{{cite:R66}} Since D is what is being measured, the check is made afterwards, with the value just obtained. For spheres, Nickol and co-workers state that the \sqrt{t} law is within 5 % of the exact solution only for Dt/r^2 < 0.0032.{{cite:R68}}

Particle size is the other trap. The formula needs the diffusion length, and a porous electrode has secondary particles several micrometres across made of primary grains a few hundred nanometres across. Because D scales with r^2, choosing 5 µm instead of 0.25 µm gives a value 400 times larger.{{cite:R68}} A published diffusion coefficient means little without the radius it was referred to.

Short pulse, small step, known size: without all three, the GITT formula returns a number, not a diffusion coefficient.

Even with a good pulse, the answer depends on how the voltage is read. Nickol and co-workers analysed the same GITT data on an NCM523 cathode by six procedures: two formulas that need the IR drop read off the curve (P1, P2), a straight-line fit of E against \sqrt{t} (P3), a two-line fit that leaves out the start (P4), and fits of the whole pulse to the spherical solution, with and without the start (P5, P6). At 40 °C the results differed by a factor of 3; on cooling, by more than an order of magnitude.{{cite:R68}}

The reason is the start of the pulse. The ohmic drop is instant, but the charge-transfer drop arrives only as the double layer charges, with time constant \tau_{dl} = R_{ct}C_{dl}: below 1 s above 0 °C at medium lithium content, far longer in the cold.{{cite:R68}} A recipe that reads the IR drop at a fixed 5 s, or fits a line through the start, mistakes double-layer charging for diffusion. Procedures that skip the transition (P4, P6) are expected to be more reliable; but if R_{ct} itself changes during the pulse, the full fit P6 is thrown off most.{{cite:R68}}

Their practical advice: measure \tau_{dl} by impedance before each pulse; use a three-electrode cell, so that the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage, large at low temperature, is not mistaken for slow diffusion; keep the current small (0.1C agreed with 0.05C); and check that two currents give the same answer.{{cite:R68}}

Kang and Chueh went further: read diffusion from the rest after the pulse, not from the pulse itself. During the pulse the voltage carries the IR drop on top of the diffusion signal, and if the total resistance drifts, as charge-transfer resistance does when the composition changes, the \sqrt t slope is wrong. In their example a 17 % fall halves the slope and makes D four times too large. During the rest no current flows, so no IR drop can drift.{{cite:R66}}

They solved the rest exactly: plotted against \sqrt{t + \tau} - \sqrt{t}, where t is the time since the current stopped, the voltage is a straight line whose slope gives D, without needing the final equilibrium voltage. Fast processes that are not bulk diffusion, such as a surface layer, appear at the start of the rest and can be left out of the fit; the finite size of the sample bends the curve near the end, so the straight part in between is used, which for a short pulse lasts up to about ten pulse lengths of rest. Their recommended procedure: large, dense, single-phase samples; at least three pulses of different size; and the \hat\tau check afterwards.{{cite:R66}}

The rest must also be long enough. Abbas and co-workers found that an NMC622 electrode needed about 7 to 9 h to relax below 3.8 V, and above 3.8 V the voltage was still drifting by 0.1 to 0.6 mV per hour after 9 to 12 h; published studies use fixed rests from 40 min to a day.{{cite:R69}} They also showed that ignoring diffusion in the electrolyte inside the pores, as the simple formula does, made their GITT value about four times smaller than a transmission-line analysis of impedance (figure 11.11).{{cite:R69}}

GITT is easy to run and easy to misread: check the pulse length, the reading of the IR drop, the rest and the size.

One GITT run gives more than a diffusion coefficient. The voltage at the end of each rest is a quasi-open-circuit voltage (QOCV); joined up, these trace the equilibrium curve of the electrode. The voltage at the end of each pulse is the closed-circuit voltage (CCV). Their difference is the overpotential at that state of charge, and dividing by the current gives an internal resistance. With D from each step, the run yields three curves against composition.{{cite:R67}}

The price is time: sixteen steps of 10 min with 4 h rests take nearly three days, and where relaxation is slow even 4 h may not be enough.{{cite:R69}}

Impedance spectroscopy, from the beginning

Impedance spectroscopy disturbs a cell with a small sine, a voltage or a current, and records the sine that answers, at many frequencies one after the other. If the cell behaves linearly and does not change during the measurement, the answer has the same frequency and differs only in size and timing.{{cite:R63}} The ratio of the two amplitudes is |Z|; the timing difference is the phase \varphi. Both fit in one complex number:{{cite:R63}}

Z = \frac{V_0}{I_0}\,e^{j\varphi} = Z' + jZ'', \qquad Z' = |Z|\cos\varphi,\quad Z'' = |Z|\sin\varphi

Each circuit element has its own signature. A resistor: Z = R, in phase, at every frequency. A capacitor: Z = 1/(j\omega C), with the current leading by 90° and the size falling as frequency rises (\omega = 2\pi f). An inductor: Z = j\omega L, current lagging, size rising.{{cite:R63,B2}} Because the three depend so differently on frequency, sweeping the frequency separates them.{{cite:R63}}

The instrument is a potentiostat with a frequency response analyzer, which correlates the response with a sine and a cosine at the excitation frequency over whole periods to get the two parts of Z.{{cite:R63}} Commercial instruments span about 10 µHz to 1 MHz, but slow points are expensive: a single measurement at 10 µHz takes 27.8 h, so in practice the lowest frequency is about 1 mHz, often 10 to 100 mHz. Sixty frequencies from 100 kHz down to 0.1 Hz take 2 to 3 minutes.{{cite:R63}}

Worked example. Peak, peak-to-peak or rms? A published amplitude of “10 mV” could mean any of them.{{cite:R63}} V_p = 10\ \mathrm{mV}: peak-to-peak V_{pp} = 2V_p = 20\ \mathrm{mV} root mean square V_{rms} = V_p/\sqrt{2} = 7\ \mathrm{mV} A factor of nearly three between the largest and smallest reading of the same signal.

There are two standard ways to draw a spectrum. The Nyquist plot puts -Z'' upward against Z', one point per frequency, high frequencies on the left; a resistor in parallel with a capacitor draws a semicircle of diameter R. Its weaknesses: the frequency of each point is not shown, and the large low-frequency values set the scale and squash the high-frequency end. The Bode plot shows |Z| and the phase against the logarithm of frequency, so every point’s frequency can be read and each decade gets equal room.{{cite:R63}}

Each R‖C pair has a time constant \tau = RC, and its arc peaks where \omega\tau = 1, that is at f = 1/(2\pi\tau).{{cite:R63}} Two pairs give two separate arcs only if their time constants differ by about a factor of 100 or more; closer, the arcs merge, and at equal \tau a single semicircle remains.{{cite:R63}} One rule is often broken: the two axes of a Nyquist plot must have the same scale; stretching the vertical one makes depressed arcs look round.{{cite:R63,R64}}

Worked example. Charge transfer in a commercial cell: Rct = 10 mΩ in parallel with a double layer of Cdl = 2 F. \tau = R_{ct}C_{dl} = 0.010\ \Omega \times 2\ \mathrm{F} = 0.020\ \mathrm{s} f = 1/(2\pi\tau) = 1/(2\pi \times 0.020\ \mathrm{s}) = 8 Hz Milliohms times farads still make milliseconds: the arcs of a large cell sit at hertz, as in figure 11.10.

A real cell is read from left to right, high frequency to low. At the top of the range the cables and the wound electrodes act as an inductance, so the spectrum starts below the axis. Where it crosses the axis is the ohmic resistance, the sum of electrolyte, active material, current collectors and contacts. In the middle range come arcs from the interfaces, each a resistance in parallel with a capacitance; at low frequency a tail from lithium diffusing inside the particles.{{cite:R64}}

A commercial cell has large electrodes and so an impedance in the milliohm range, mostly below 0.1 Ω and at frequencies below a few tens of kilohertz. That makes it hard to measure: a small voltage drives a large current, of the order of an ampere.{{cite:R64}} And the interpretation is ambiguous: each electrode has its own SEI and its own charge transfer, at least four processes, but with only two terminals arcs of similar time constant merge into one or two.{{cite:R64}} Changing the state of charge or the temperature, which moves the processes by different amounts, helps tell them apart.{{cite:R64}}

Manufacturers use a single frequency, typically 1 kHz, to check and match cells on the production line; researchers use full spectra to follow degradation, which is the most common reason impedance is measured on commercial cells.{{cite:R64}}

Left to right is fast to slow: wires, ohmic parts, interfaces, diffusion.

Real spectra are never made of perfect semicircles. Arcs are depressed: their centres sit below the axis. The usual fix replaces the capacitor with a constant phase element, Z = 1/(Y_0 (j\omega)^n): n = 1 is a capacitor, n = 0 a resistor and n = 0.5 a Warburg diffusion line. Surface roughness, fractal geometry and adsorption have all been proposed as its cause, and the question is not settled.{{cite:R63}} A CPE with a free exponent can fit almost any valid data, so its parameters cannot be read as real capacitances.{{cite:R64}}

Diffusion into a layer of finite thickness starts as the 45° Warburg line, then bends. If the far side lets lithium through, the line bends back to the axis in an arc; if it blocks lithium, as the inside of a particle does, the line turns vertical like a capacitor.{{cite:R63}} Particles are closer to spheres than to films: with the same diffusion resistance R_d and capacitance C_d, a sphere turns upward earlier and its tail stands at R_d/15 instead of R_d/3, and the diffusion coefficient follows as D = r^2/(R_d C_d).{{cite:R69}}

A porous electrode is a ladder: the electrolyte resistance runs down each pore, and every grain wall has its own interface. The result is a 45° line at high frequency followed by an arc.{{cite:R63,R64}} Abbas and co-workers fitted such a transmission-line model to an NMC622 electrode and obtained a diffusion coefficient about four times larger than from GITT or from impedance models that put diffusion in simple series.{{cite:R69}}

Measuring well

Impedance theory assumes a linear system, and an electrode reaction is not one: its current grows exponentially with overpotential. Close to equilibrium the Butler–Volmer curve is nearly straight with slope 1/R_{ct}, and that straight piece is what impedance measures. A nonlinear response to a pure sine is distorted; it contains signals at 2f, 3f and higher, because the harmonics reflect the curvature of the curve.{{cite:B2}}

The amplitude is therefore a compromise: large enough for a clean signal, small enough to stay linear. Under voltage control 5 to 10 mV is usual, and the Lissajous plot shows when it is too large.{{cite:R63}} Under current control, 0.1 to 1 A on a commercial cell gives a response of a few millivolts and may be more accurate.{{cite:R64}} The total harmonic distortion, the harmonics’ combined size over the fundamental, checks linearity at every frequency.{{cite:R64}}

Nonlinear methods turn this round. NLEIS drives the cell moderately harder and reads the harmonics as “higher-order impedance”; NFRA uses large currents to excite them on purpose. In one study the ratio of third to second harmonic rose in cells aged at −10 °C, where lithium had plated, while ordinary spectra looked the same. There is no standard method yet, and large currents can damage the cell.{{cite:R64,R65}}

Worked example. How large is Rct for an exchange current i₀ = 1 mA at 25 °C? R_{ct} = \frac{RT}{F i_0} = \frac{8.314 \times 298.15}{96485 \times 0.001}\ \Omega = 25.7 Ω A 1000 times larger exchange current, as on a large electrode, gives 25.7 mΩ.{{cite:B2}}

With two terminals the analyzer passes the current and measures the voltage through the same wires, so it measures the cell plus the wires. With four, one pair carries the current and a second pair senses the voltage at the cell’s own terminals; since the sense pair carries almost no current, its wires drop no voltage, and only the cell is measured. For batteries, where the wires are not negligible, four terminals are recommended.{{cite:R63,R64}}

Cables also bring inductance, proportional to their length and most visible on low-impedance cells: the high-frequency end of the spectrum drops further below the axis.{{cite:R63}} Short cables, current and sense wires twisted in pairs, a metal box around the cell, the same cables for calibration and measurement, and a fixed holder all help; simply re-inserting a cell in its holder changed the ohmic resistance by up to 10 % in one study.{{cite:R64}}

The cell must also be steady. Meddings and co-workers list what that takes: set the state of charge, then rest until the voltage stops moving (4 h was found adequate in one study; others report 15 min to 100 h, and half the studies they surveyed did not say); let the temperature settle, remembering that the inside of the cell lags the chamber; and set the state of charge before changing the temperature.{{cite:R64}}

A spectrum is worth modelling only if it could have come from a system that was linear, causal (the response comes only from the excitation), stable (unchanged during the sweep) and finite. For such a system the real and imaginary parts are tied together by the Kramers–Kronig relations: one can be computed from the other.{{cite:R63,R64}}

The relations need every frequency from zero to infinity, which no measurement has, so in practice a test circuit is fitted instead: many R‖C elements with fixed, evenly spread time constants, only their resistances free. Any valid spectrum can be fitted this way; if the fit fails, something is wrong with the data.{{cite:R63,R64}} The residuals should be small and scattered randomly around zero, well below 1 %.{{cite:R63,R64}} The test is used in only a minority of published work on commercial cells, though more often now that DRT, which needs excellent data, has spread.{{cite:R64}}

Check the measurement before believing the model: rest, temperature, connection, amplitude, then the Kramers–Kronig test.

From spectrum to meaning

The usual analysis fits an equivalent circuit to the spectrum by complex nonlinear least squares and judges the fit by its χ².{{cite:R63}} The trap is that circuits are not unique: different circuits can give exactly the same impedance at every frequency, and adding elements always improves the fit.{{cite:R63}} The same model can also return different values depending on the starting guesses and the weighting.{{cite:R64}}

So the circuit has to come from the physics of the cell, each element tied to a process, with as few elements as possible.{{cite:R63,R64}} Physics-based models go further: the transmission line of figure 11.11 with spherical diffusion in each grain, or full electrochemical models solved for their impedance.{{cite:R64,R69}}

The distribution of relaxation times (DRT) avoids choosing a circuit in advance. Every R‖C pair relaxes with one time constant, so a spectrum can be written as a sum, in the limit a distribution, of such pairs:{{cite:R64,R65}}

Z(\omega) = R_0 + R_{pol}\int_0^\infty \frac{g(\tau)}{1 + j\omega\tau}\,d\tau

Recovering g(\tau) turns the spectrum into peaks along the time axis, one per process, even where the arcs overlap in a Nyquist plot. That is why DRT is used on commercial cells: to count the processes and then build a circuit with the right number of elements.{{cite:R64,R65}}

The catch is that recovering g from noisy data is unstable and needs a regularisation, a penalty of strength \lambda; only a narrow range of \lambda gives the right answer, and the number of peaks depends on it.{{cite:R64,R65}} Bad data points distort it strongly, so DRT needs a Kramers–Kronig check first, and because the inductive and diffusion parts distort it too, it is usually applied to the SEI and charge-transfer arcs.{{cite:R64}}

Following ageing

Impedance generally rises as a cell ages, and because the measurement does not harm the cell, it can be repeated through its whole life. Ageing studies run check-ups: the same measurement at fixed conditions every so many cycles, often enough to follow the ageing, rarely enough not to add to it.{{cite:R64}} Ageing has two visible effects: capacity fade, from losing active material, and power fade, from growing resistance through lost contacts, thickening surface films and delamination.{{cite:R64}}

A spectrum is reduced to a few features: fitted resistances, DRT peaks or impedance at chosen frequencies. Individual contributions may follow the state of health, and give early warning of sudden fade, better than the total impedance.{{cite:R64}} The difficulty is that temperature, state of charge and ageing all move the same spectrum; for some chemistries, LiFePO₄ in particular, estimating the state of charge from impedance is much harder than for NMC.{{cite:R64}}

Machine learning is the newest route: train a model on features from many cells, then predict the state of health or charge of a new one. Gaussian process regression is common, and models based on relaxation times have reached errors around 1 %.{{cite:R65}} Their knowledge ends at their training data, and the studies so far cover few systems and conditions.{{cite:R65}} Local impedance, measured on one spot of an electrode, is a further step, since a whole-cell spectrum averages over a structure that is not uniform.{{cite:R65}}

When the outside is not enough

Telling the two electrodes apart needs a reference electrode inside the cell, placed where it does not disturb the current, which is still hard to build and not a production solution.{{cite:R48,R64}} The alternative is to open the cell. Before that come non-destructive checks: inspection, X-ray tomography, capacity, cyclic voltammetry and differential capacity. Then the cell is discharged slowly and opened in an argon glove box; its electrodes can be rebuilt into half cells or symmetric cells of two identical electrodes, whose spectra show which electrode carries which arc, and examined by microscopy and spectroscopy. A fresh cell treated the same way is the baseline.{{cite:R64}}

The full picture often needs this tear-down, with Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy, NMR, electron microscopy and X-ray absorption, while accelerating-rate calorimetry finds the conditions for thermal runaway.{{cite:R1}} Plastic lithium-ion cells opened the way to watching the electrodes while they work,{{cite:R2}} and an external pressure sensor can already follow the SEI growing through the swelling it causes.{{cite:R48}}

The cell itself has to be chosen too. The standard way to test a new positive electrode is a half-cell against lithium metal,{{cite:R6}} but half-cells can fail to predict how the same electrode behaves in a full cell, and small assembly errors change the result;{{cite:R21}} in GITT, the lithium counter electrode’s own overvoltage can pass for slow diffusion.{{cite:R68}}

You can now explain why a pulse resistance needs its timescale; how GITT turns two voltage changes into a diffusion coefficient, and the four checks that make it trustworthy (pulse length, IR drop, rest, particle size); what impedance is, and how to read Nyquist and Bode plots of a real cell from left to right; why the measurement must be small, steady, four-terminal and Kramers–Kronig valid; why a good circuit fit is not proof; and what DRT, nonlinear impedance and machine learning add.

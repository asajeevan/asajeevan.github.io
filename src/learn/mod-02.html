Anatomy of a cell

If you cut a cell open, what would you find, and what does each part do?

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide.{{cite:R1}} The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.{{cite:R1}} The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching.{{cite:R1}} If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.{{cite:R1}}

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words. In battery usage the anode is the negative electrode, where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, and the cathode is the positive electrode, where reduction takes them back.{{cite:R1}} Because the roles reverse when the cell is charged, this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and lithium cobalt oxide on aluminium foil, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell.{{cite:R6}} Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal.{{cite:R6}}

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal. So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals.{{cite:R6}} A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.{{cite:R1,R2}}

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.{{cite:R1}} A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; module 8 returns to what happens where one of them does not.

The same four parts, in different materials, give very different cells. The table behind this chart lists the systems on the market in 2004: zinc and manganese dioxide in the 1.5 V cells of a torch, lead and lead oxide in the 2.0 V cells of a car battery, and graphite with lithium cobalt oxide in the 4.0 V cells of a phone.{{cite:R1}} Where the voltage comes from is the next question.

You can now explain the four parts of a cell and their jobs, why the separator must never let the electrodes touch, and why cells are built from thin, wide layers.

Two of those parts decide how hard the push is.

Go deeper: half cells and full cells in the laboratory

New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell”, a coin cell with a piece of lithium foil as the negative electrode; lithium is a convenient reference, but a half cell can fail to predict how the material behaves against a graphite negative electrode in a full cell, which is why full coin cells are also built.{{cite:R21,R6}}

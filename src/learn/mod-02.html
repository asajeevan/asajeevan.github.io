Anatomy of a cell

If you cut a cell open, what would you find, and what does each part do?

Where we are. Module 1 showed the four jobs in a cell: give electrons, take electrons, carry ions, keep the two electrodes apart. Now the parts that do those jobs in a real cell, and why they have the shapes they have.

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide.{{cite:R1}} The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.{{cite:R1}} The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching.{{cite:R1}} If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.{{cite:R1}}

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words. In battery usage the anode is the negative electrode, where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, and the cathode is the positive electrode, where reduction takes them back.{{cite:R1}} Because the roles reverse when the cell is charged, this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and the lithium host on aluminium, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell.{{cite:R6}} Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal.{{cite:R6}} The electrolyte is a liquid, added drop by drop during assembly: in Murray, Hall and Dahn’s research cells, about 38 mg of a 1 mol/L solution of LiPF₆ in a carbonate solvent, enough to soak the separator and wet both electrodes.{{cite:R21}} New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell” against a piece of lithium foil; such cells are easy to make but can fail to predict how the material behaves against graphite in a full cell, which is why full coin cells like this one are built.{{cite:R21}}

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal (module 0 showed why: an ion drags its way through a liquid at a terminal speed set by its mobility). So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals.{{cite:R6}} A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.{{cite:R1,R2}}

Worked example. Why area matters: the resistance of a layer is R = ρl/A (module 0).{{cite:B2}} Keep the electrolyte layer as thin as the separator allows (small l) and make the electrodes as wide as will fit (large A), and the ionic resistance of the whole cell falls with both. A wound strip is the way to put a large A inside a small can.

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.{{cite:R1}} The porous structure extends the surface area where the reaction can happen and lowers the current density, so the cell works more efficiently.{{cite:R1}} A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; module 8 returns to what happens where one of them does not.

The same four parts, in different materials, give very different cells. The table behind this chart lists the systems on the market in 2004: zinc and manganese dioxide in the 1.5 V cells of a torch, lead and lead oxide in the 2.0 V cells of a car battery, and graphite with lithium cobalt oxide in the 4.0 V cells of a phone.{{cite:R1}} Two patterns are already visible: every system with a water-based electrolyte stays at or below 2 V, and every system above 2 V is built on lithium with a nonaqueous electrolyte.{{cite:R1}} Where the voltage comes from, and why water sets a ceiling, is the next question.

You can now explain the parts of a cell and their jobs, why the separator must never let the electrodes touch, why cells are built from thin, wide layers, and why an electrode is a porous coating rather than a block.

Two of those parts decide how hard the push is.

Go deeper: what a research coin cell taught its makers

Murray, Hall and Dahn built full coin cells with graphite and a nickel-rich oxide and found that three “identical” cells kept 95 %, 90 % and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. Taking them apart showed the cause: the positive and negative discs were slightly misaligned, and lithium had plated on the parts of the positive electrode not covered by the negative one. The spring and spacer had been dropped in at an angle from tweezers; placing them from directly above with a vacuum pen, and using a thicker, more compliant separator, brought the cells to the reproducibility of machine-made pouch cells.{{cite:R21}} Module 9 explains why plated lithium is lost.

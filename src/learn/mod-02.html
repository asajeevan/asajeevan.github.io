Module 2

Anatomy of a cell

If you cut a cell open, what would you find, and what does each part do?

Where we are. {{Ch:galvanic-cells}} showed the four jobs in a cell: give electrons, take electrons, carry ions, keep the two electrodes apart. Now the parts that do those jobs in a real cell, and why they have the shapes they have.

Every cell has the same four jobs. The negative electrode gives up electrons: it is a good reducing agent, such as lithium, zinc or lead. The positive electrode takes them: an electron acceptor, such as lithium cobalt oxide, manganese dioxide or lead oxide.{{cite:R1}} The electrolyte “provides pure ionic conductivity” between the two: it lets ions through and blocks electrons.{{cite:R1}}

The separator is a porous barrier, permeable to ions and inert in the cell, that keeps the electrodes from touching.{{cite:R1}} If they touched, the whole stored energy would be released as heat inside the cell.{{cite:R1}}

Anode or cathode? You will meet both words, and they name a reaction, not a piece of metal: the anode is the electrode where oxidation releases electrons into the external circuit, the cathode the electrode where reduction takes them back.{{cite:R1}} On discharge the negative electrode is oxidized and the positive electrode is reduced, so the negative electrode is the anode and the positive electrode the cathode; that is the battery convention.{{cite:R1}} On charge the charger drives the current the other way:{{cite:R1}} electrons are pushed into the negative electrode, which is now reduced, and pulled out of the positive electrode, which is now oxidized, so by the reaction definition the names swap, and the graphite that was the anode while you used the phone is the cathode while it charges. The metals stay where they are and keep their signs; only the labels move. To avoid the confusion this page says negative electrode and positive electrode from here on.

In the coin cell above, graphite sits on copper foil and the lithium host on aluminium, exactly as in the first commercial lithium-ion cell.{{cite:R6}} Each foil is a current collector: a metal path for the electrons between the coating and the terminal.{{cite:R6}} The electrolyte is a liquid, added drop by drop during assembly: in Murray, Hall and Dahn’s research cells, about 38 mg of a 1 mol/L solution of LiPF₆ in a carbonate solvent, enough to soak the separator and wet both electrodes.{{cite:R21}}

New positive-electrode materials are usually tested in a “half cell” against a piece of lithium foil; such cells are easy to make but can fail to predict how the material behaves against graphite in a full cell, which is why full coin cells like this one are built.{{cite:R21}}

Every part has one job, and the electrolyte’s job is to let ions through and block electrons.

Ions move through an electrolyte far more slowly than electrons move through a metal ({{ch:electrostatics}} showed why: an ion drags its way through a liquid at a terminal speed set by its mobility). So a cell is built with large-area electrodes separated by a thin electrolyte, and metallic current collectors carry the electrons between the electrodes and the terminals.{{cite:R6}} A cylindrical cell is exactly that stack, wound into a roll; coin, prismatic and flat cells hold the same layers in other shapes.{{cite:R1,R2}}

Worked example. Why area matters: the resistance of a layer is R = ρl/A ({{ch:electrostatics}}).{{cite:B2}} Keep the electrolyte layer as thin as the separator allows (small l) and make the electrodes as wide as will fit (large A), and the ionic resistance of the whole cell falls with both. A wound strip is the way to put a large A inside a small can.

An electrode is not a solid block of the active material. It is a coating: particles of active material, a conductive diluent such as carbon powder, and a polymer binder that holds the mixture together and bonds it to the current collector. Typically about 30 % of its volume is pores, and the pores are filled with electrolyte.{{cite:R1}}

The porous structure extends the surface area where the reaction can happen and lowers the current density, so the cell works more efficiently.{{cite:R1}} A particle can only react where electrons reach it through the carbon and ions reach it through the electrolyte; a later module returns to what happens where one of them does not.

Four formats

The same layers come in four common shapes, and some of them are named by their size.{{cite:B3}} A coin cell is a flat stainless-steel case, wider than it is high, with a gasket, a cap, a spacer plate and a spring holding the layers together; in the laboratory its negative electrode is often lithium foil. Its name, standardized by the IEC, is a code: in CR2025, C stands for a lithium chemistry, R for round, 20 for the diameter in millimetres and 25 for the height in tenths of a millimetre, so 2.5 mm. CR2025 is the most common design at laboratory scale.{{cite:B3}}

In a cylindrical cell the electrodes and separator are wound into a spiral inside a cylindrical case, the roll of figure {{fig:f2-2}}: good cycling stability, but round cells pack less densely. Its five-digit name, such as 18650 or 21700, gives its size. A prismatic cell holds large stacked sheets in a rectangular case of aluminium, steel or semi-hard plastic, usually with a vent, and suits uses where swelling matters; its six digits give the thickness in tenths of a millimetre, then the width and the length in millimetres, so 504050 is 5.0 by 40 by 50 mm. A pouch cell stacks its electrodes in a sealed, soft, flat case: light and cheap to make.{{cite:B3}}

Shape matters in use as well. In electric vehicles, vibration and shock speed up the loss of capacity and the rise of resistance, through the loss of active material and of lithium inventory of {{ch:degradation}}; a 2026 review reports cylindrical 18650 cells as particularly susceptible, and pouch and prismatic cells as more tolerant when tightly packed.{{cite:R82}}

More names, for reference. A coin cell’s shape letter can be R for round, F for flat, S for square or P for not round; besides CR2025 there are CR2477, CR1616, CR1620, CR1632, CR2016 and CR2032, and such cells power watches, toys, LED lights, thermometers, heart-rate and glucose monitors, calculators, remote controls and car keys.{{cite:B3}} Cylindrical cells may carry the letters LIR, for lithium-ion rechargeable; besides the 18650 there are 14500, 14650, 18500, 21700, 26650 and 32650 cells, used in security and communication systems, medical equipment, laptops, e-bikes and power tools.{{cite:B3}}

What each part is made of

We have met the parts of a lithium-ion cell one at a time. Let us put names to the materials. The positive electrode is typically a layered lithium metal oxide such as LiCoO₂, coated on aluminium foil; the negative electrode is carbon or graphite on copper foil; the electrolyte is a lithium salt in organic solvents; and the separator is a polyethylene or polypropylene membrane.{{cite:B3}} The positive electrode needs lattice sites that take lithium in and give it back reversibly, and the larger the difference in electrochemical potential between the two electrodes, the higher the energy of the cell.{{cite:B3}}

Each part has a menu. Positive electrodes: LiCoO₂, the spinel LiMn₂O₄, LiFePO₄, the nickel-manganese-cobalt oxides (NMC), LiNi0.8Co0.15Al0.05O₂ (NCA) and layered dichalcogenides. Negative electrodes: graphitic and non-graphitic carbons, silicon-based alloys, tin alloys such as Cu₆Sn₅, Ni₃Sn₂ and Co₃Sn₂, and the titanium oxides Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ and TiO₂. Electrolytes: LiPF₆, LiBF₄, LiClO₄ or LiAsF₆ in a mixture of PC, EC, DMC, DEC and EMC, or, in a lithium polymer cell, a gel based on PEO, PAN, PVDF or PMMA. Separators: microporous polypropylene, non-woven mats, ceramic-based materials and copolymers of vinylidene fluoride. Current collectors: aluminium and copper, as foil, mesh or foam, etched or coated.{{cite:B3}}

Which way next? The same textbook names layered lithium-rich positive electrodes, silicon-based negative electrodes, electrolytes that are more stable and conduct ions better, and current collectors based on titanium carbide (MXene), lighter, cheaper and flexible. It adds recycling, both to cut electronic waste and as a second source of materials ({{ch:recycling}}).{{cite:B3}}

The same four parts, in different materials, give very different cells. The table behind this chart lists the systems on the market in 2004: zinc and manganese dioxide in the 1.5 V cells of a torch, lead and lead oxide in the 2.0 V cells of a car battery, and graphite with lithium cobalt oxide in the 4.0 V cells of a phone.{{cite:R1}}

Two patterns are already visible: every system with a water-based electrolyte stays at or below 2 V, and every system above 2 V is built on lithium with a nonaqueous electrolyte.{{cite:R1}} Where the voltage comes from, and why water sets a ceiling, is the next question.

You can now explain the parts of a cell and their jobs, why the separator must never let the electrodes touch, why cells are built from thin, wide layers, and why an electrode is a porous coating rather than a block.

Two of those parts decide how hard the push is.

Go deeper: what a research coin cell taught its makers

Murray, Hall and Dahn built full coin cells with graphite and a nickel-rich oxide and found that three “identical” cells kept 95 %, 90 % and 80 % of their capacity after 100 cycles. Taking them apart showed the cause: the positive and negative discs were slightly misaligned, and lithium had plated on the parts of the positive electrode not covered by the negative one. The spring and spacer had been dropped in at an angle from tweezers; placing them from directly above with a vacuum pen, and using a thicker, more compliant separator, brought the cells to the reproducibility of machine-made pouch cells.{{cite:R21}} Why plated lithium is lost for good is a story for a later module.

The mathematics of this module

Rion = l / (κA)the ionic resistance of the electrolyte layer between the electrodes: thin (small l) and wide (large A) is the whole design brief of figure 2.2. Winter and Brodd give κ of order 1 S/cm for aqueous and 10⁻² to 10⁻³ S/cm for the organic electrolytes of lithium cells.

κeff = κ εα, α ≈ 1.5inside a porous electrode only the pore fraction ε (about 0.3, figure {{fig:f2-3}}) carries ions, along winding paths; the Bruggeman relation gives the effective conductivity of the electrolyte in the pores. Boz and co-workers quote α = 1.5, and 1.53 measured for LiFePO₄ electrodes.

Deff = D ε/τ, τ = ε1−αthe same correction for diffusion, with τ the tortuosity of the pore network

With ε = 0.3 and α = 1.5, κeff = 0.31.5 κ = 0.16 κ: the electrolyte inside a coating conducts about a sixth as well as the free liquid. The module on composite electrodes, still to come, builds on this.
